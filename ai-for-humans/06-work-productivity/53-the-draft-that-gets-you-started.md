# The Draft That Gets You Started

## The blinking cursor isn’t a lack of ideas

The cursor blinks on an empty page. It’s been blinking long enough that you start questioning your entire identity as a competent adult.

What makes it worse is this: you *do* know what you want to say.

Maybe it’s a proposal you’ve been developing for months. A personal statement. An email you’ve rewritten in your head a hundred times. In conversation, you can explain it clearly. You can even feel the momentum.

But the moment you face the blank document, everything freezes.

That’s the paradox of the blank page: **knowing exactly what you mean, yet being unable to begin.**

## Why starting feels harder than writing

People often frame writer’s block as a creativity problem—like you’re “out of ideas.” But in many cases, it’s not that.

It’s a cognitive traffic jam.

The blank page quietly demands two difficult tasks at the same time:

1. **Generate ideas** (what should I say?)
2. **Organize them** (what’s the right order, tone, structure, wording?)

Those tasks compete for the same mental resources. And because each depends on the other, you can get stuck in a loop:

- “I can’t write this until I know the structure.”
- “I can’t know the structure until I get the ideas down.”

So you do what humans do when overwhelmed: you stall. You scroll. You tinker with formatting. You open a new tab and pretend you’re “researching.”

## The surprising power of being asked a question

Now imagine someone sits down with you and says, “Okay—tell me what you’re working on.”

Suddenly, you can talk.

That’s because conversation removes the burden of packaging. When someone asks a question, your job is simple: **answer it.** You don’t need the perfect outline. You don’t need the perfect first sentence. You just respond.

That’s exactly what Rachel did when she felt stuck. Instead of demanding a draft from an AI assistant, she typed:

“I can’t seem to start. Can you ask me questions about my proposal?”

The AI didn’t write her proposal. It asked:

- “What problem does this solve?”
- “Who benefits most?”
- “What’s the current cost of doing nothing?”
- “What would success look like in six months?”

Rachel answered, quickly and imperfectly. Twenty minutes later, she had over a thousand words—because the blank page was no longer asking her to *perform*. It was simply collecting what she already knew.

This is the first key insight: **questions create motion.**

## AI as scaffolding, not replacement

There’s a subtle but important difference between “AI writing for you” and “AI helping you write.”

When you ask an AI to generate a polished document from scratch, it often produces something that looks fine but feels generic. It may sound overly formal. It may miss your real priorities. It may bury the most compelling point under safe, vague language.

But when you use AI as scaffolding—question prompts, structure suggestions, rough drafts—you stay in the driver’s seat.

Think of it like this:

- **You** bring the lived experience, expertise, and stakes.
- **AI** provides momentum, prompts, and a shape to react to.

This is particularly helpful for people who think out loud. Some of us don’t discover what we believe until we hear ourselves say it—or see it written down.

AI can simulate that conversational partner who keeps asking, “Say more about that.”

## The “bad draft” is the point

After Rachel answered a set of questions, she organized her responses into loose sections and asked the AI for a rough draft.

The result wasn’t amazing. It was a little stiff. Some parts sounded like corporate wallpaper. Some nuance was missing.

But here’s the twist: **the draft worked anyway.**

Why? Because a rough draft isn’t just a draft—it’s a provocation.

The moment Rachel saw the text, she had strong reactions:

- “That’s too formal.”
- “This is the wrong emphasis.”
- “This should be the opening.”
- “I hate that phrasing.”

Those reactions are not annoyances. They are *information*. They reveal your voice, your values, your priorities—the things that can be hard to access when you’re staring at nothing.

A blank page gives you nothing to argue with. A mediocre draft gives you a target.

## Think like an architect: sketch first

Architects don’t begin with final blueprints. They sketch.

The early sketch is allowed to be wrong. In fact, being wrong is useful, because it tells you what needs to change. The sketch externalizes thinking. It turns vague ideas into something visible and adjustable.

AI drafts can serve the same purpose.

Not as a finished product you submit, but as a quick sketch you reshape. Rachel spent an hour revising. In the end, maybe 20% of the AI’s draft remained. But she was finished—and that’s what mattered.

Without scaffolding, she might still be staring at the cursor.

## A practical way to try this today

If you want to use AI to get started without outsourcing your voice, try a simple sequence:

1. **Ask for questions, not a draft.**  
   “Ask me 10 questions to clarify my idea.”

2. **Answer quickly.**  
   No polishing. No perfection. Just responses.

3. **Ask for a rough structure.**  
   “Turn my answers into an outline with section headings.”

4. **Generate a draft you can react to.**  
   “Write a rough draft in a simple, friendly tone.”

5. **Edit like a human with opinions.**  
   Keep what’s true. Delete what’s bland. Rewrite what matters.

This method works because it respects how thinking actually happens: messy first, clearer later.

## Closing thought

The question isn’t “Can AI write for me?” It’s “Can AI help me write?”

Sometimes the most powerful thing isn’t a perfect paragraph—it’s the first imperfect page. You don’t need brilliance to begin. You just need something, anything, to react to.
