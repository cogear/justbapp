# The Mood Board in Words  
## Using AI to Capture Creative Vision

## When You Can Feel It, But Can’t Say It
You know that feeling when you *know* exactly what you want—yet the moment you try to describe it, everything comes out wrong?

It’s not “blue.” It’s the blue of a winter morning before the sun rises. It’s not “cozy.” It’s the quiet warmth of lamplight on a rainy afternoon when you finally exhale.

But try putting that into a Slack message or a creative brief and suddenly you’re stuck with words like *nice*, *clean*, *modern*, *make it pop*. And those words don’t carry your actual vision. They’re placeholders.

That gap—between what you sense and what you can explain—is one of the most common creative frustrations. And it’s exactly where AI can be surprisingly useful.

## The Vision Problem (And Why Mood Boards Exist)
Creative people have always built translation tools.

Designers make mood boards because a single adjective can’t communicate a whole atmosphere. Filmmakers create look books because “cinematic” can mean a thousand different things. Writers share playlists, photographers reference paintings, founders send brand examples with “kind of like this, but… not this.”

We point at external things because it’s easier than describing what’s internal.

But referencing images has limits. It can box you into someone else’s aesthetic. It can lead collaborators to copy the reference instead of understanding what you *meant*. And sometimes you don’t even know what to reference—because what you want feels original, personal, and oddly specific.

AI can’t see what’s in your head. But it can help you *say* what’s in your head.

Think of it as a mood board made of words.

## AI as a Mirror: Turning Feelings into Language
Here’s the magic: AI is good at generating options.

You might start with something vague like:  
“I want this brand to feel trustworthy but not boring.”

A helpful AI won’t just give you a single answer. It will offer a range of interpretations:

- Trustworthy like old-world craftsmanship  
- Trustworthy like clinical precision and clarity  
- Trustworthy like a neighbor who always shows up  
- Trustworthy like consistency over time—quiet, steady, reliable  

Some of those will feel off. That’s not failure—that’s part of the process.

Because then something clicks: *Yes. The handcrafted one.* You realize what you meant by “trustworthy” wasn’t corporate polish. It was warmth. Texture. Evidence of human hands.

Now you’re not just telling a designer to “make it trustworthy.” You’re saying:  
“I want it to feel like something well-made—handcrafted, consistent, quietly confident.”

That is language someone can build from.

## The Specificity Spiral: Where Vision Becomes Shareable
Most creative direction fails because it stays too general.

“Beautiful.”  
“Elevated.”  
“Premium.”  
“Hopeful.”  
“Minimal.”

These words are empty containers. Everyone fills them differently.

What you’re really after is specificity—the kind that creates a shared mental image.

AI can help you get there by asking the questions you might not think to ask yourself.

If you say, “I want the scene to feel like the moment before a storm,” AI might expand it into sensory detail:

- Green-gold light filtered through heavy clouds  
- The air thick and still, like the world is holding its breath  
- Sound sharpened—distant birds, a faint hum, the anticipation of thunder  

Suddenly, you’re not giving “vibes.” You’re giving direction.

This is what I call the *specificity spiral*: you start with a vague feeling, then you spiral downward into clearer and clearer detail until the vision becomes concrete enough to share.

AI is useful here because it can generate prompts like:
- “What kind of hopeful?”
- “Is this more nostalgic or futuristic?”
- “Is the warmth coming from color, texture, or story?”
- “What should this *not* feel like?”

And when it offers examples—“like the first warm day after winter,” or “like finding a handwritten note from someone who loves you”—you get to notice what resonates.

You’re not outsourcing your taste. You’re discovering it.

## Practical Ways to Use a “Mood Board in Words”
You don’t need a complicated workflow. Try one of these the next time you’re stuck:

**1) Start with messy adjectives.**  
Write the vague words you keep using: *fresh, grounded, luxurious, playful, calming.*

**2) Ask for contrasts.**  
Prompt: “Help me define ‘luxurious but not flashy.’ Give me 10 interpretations.”

**3) Ask for sensory translation.**  
Prompt: “Describe this feeling using light, texture, temperature, sound, and pacing.”

**4) Ask for “yes/no” examples.**  
Prompt: “Give me five examples that match this vibe and five that don’t—explain why.”

**5) Turn it into a brief.**  
Prompt: “Based on what I’ve said, write a one-page creative direction I can send to a designer.”

Ten minutes of this can replace three rounds of misunderstandings later.

## What AI Can’t Do (And Why That Matters)
AI can help you articulate vision. It can’t have vision *for* you.

If there’s nothing genuine inside—no preference, no intention, no feeling—AI will still produce words. They’ll just be generic, trend-shaped, and hollow. The tool works best when you bring the spark and use AI as a lens.

In other words: don’t ask AI to decide what you like. Ask it to help you name what you already like but haven’t been able to describe.

## Closing Reflection
A mood board pulls images from the outside world. A mood board in words pulls something from the inside.

AI can’t replace your creative instinct. But it can ask the questions—and offer the language—that helps you see your own vision more clearly.

The vision is still yours. It was always yours.

Now you just have more ways to say what you mean.
