# A Thousand Bad Ideas  
*Why AI's quantity enables your quality*

## The surprising secret behind a “good idea”
What if the real secret to having one great idea is being willing to produce a thousand bad ones?

Talk to anyone who writes, designs, builds, teaches, or starts things, and you’ll hear the same pattern: the good stuff doesn’t arrive fully formed. It shows up after a lot of false starts, awkward drafts, and “well, that didn’t work” experiments.

The diamond comes from sifting. The catch is that sifting takes energy—and most of us don’t have endless creative fuel. That’s where AI can be a genuinely useful partner: it can generate without getting tired, while you do what humans do best—notice what’s worth keeping.

## Two different skills: generating vs. evaluating
One of the most freeing truths about creativity is this: generating ideas and evaluating ideas are completely different skills.

Generation is messy. It thrives on speed, looseness, and volume. It benefits from permission to be wrong.

Evaluation is discerning. It requires context, taste, and a sense of what you’re trying to accomplish. It benefits from slowness and honesty.

Most people struggle because they try to do both at once. You generate a thought and immediately judge it. That inner critic slams the door before the idea gets a chance to breathe.

AI makes it easier to separate these phases. You can outsource the “raw material” stage and save your limited human attention for the stage that actually needs you: choosing, shaping, and refining.

## Quantity isn’t laziness—it’s a strategy
Imagine you need a name for a project.

Without AI, you might come up with five options. Each one feels precious because each one cost you effort. You hesitate to discard them because you don’t want to start over.

With AI, you can ask for a hundred names in minutes.

Most will be terrible. Some will be boring. A few will be oddly close. And then there’s usually one that makes you stop scrolling. Not because it’s perfect, but because it has *something*: a tone, a twist, a spark.

That spark is the whole point.

Quantity doesn’t replace creativity; it creates more opportunities for creativity to happen. When you have more options, you’re more likely to bump into unexpected combinations—phrases you wouldn’t have invented, angles you wouldn’t have considered, metaphors you didn’t know you needed.

## Embracing the waste (on purpose)
Here’s the mindset shift that makes AI useful instead of overwhelming: most of what it generates will be wrong.

That’s not a bug. It’s the process.

Nature works this way. An oak tree drops thousands of acorns. Most never become trees. Nature’s strategy isn’t efficiency—it’s abundance. The system works because the cost of generating many attempts is low, and the payoff of one successful outcome is high.

AI lets you apply that same strategy without burning out.

You can generate a hundred options quickly, discard ninety-five, and focus your energy on the five that might be worth developing. The “waste” isn’t a sign of failure; it’s evidence that you’re letting the process do its job.

## The art of recognition: your real job
AI can generate output, but it can’t truly tell you what’s *good*.

Good for *who*? Good for *what goal*? Good in *what context*? Good with *what values*?

That’s where you come in. Quality requires taste. And taste is deeply human—it’s built from experience, empathy, intuition, and understanding the moment you’re in.

Pay attention to the pause.

When you read something and you stop—when a line makes you think, “Wait… that could work”—that’s recognition. That’s the moment your creative judgment is doing what no tool can automate.

You’re not handing over the creative process. You’re dividing the labor. AI helps with volume. You decide what deserves a future.

## The trap: settling for “the best of the batch”
There is a real danger in using AI for ideas: it can nudge you into picking the best option you *see* rather than pushing for what you *want*.

Because when a list appears, it’s tempting to choose something simply to move forward. “This one is fine.” “This is the least bad.” “I guess this works.”

But abundance should raise your standards, not lower them.

A simple discipline helps: if nothing excites you, don’t settle. Generate more. Change the prompt. Add constraints. Ask for bolder options. Ask for weirder options. Ask for ideas that would scare you a little.

AI is especially powerful when you treat it like an iterative brainstorming partner rather than a vending machine for answers.

## How to use AI without losing your voice
If you want to keep your work feeling like *you*, use AI for divergence first, then bring in your own convergence.

Try this pattern:
- **Generate widely:** many angles, titles, hooks, names, outlines.
- **Select ruthlessly:** keep only what creates energy or clarity.
- **Refine deliberately:** rewrite in your voice, align with your goals, add your lived insight.
- **Stress-test:** ask, “Would I stand behind this? Does it fit my audience? Is it true?”

AI can give you a mountain of rocks. Your job is still to recognize the diamond—and then cut it into something that shines.

## Closing thought
One good idea rarely appears in isolation. It usually arrives through volume, patience, and the willingness to throw away what doesn’t work.

AI doesn’t eliminate the work of creativity. It changes where your energy goes. You generate broadly. You evaluate carefully. A thousand bad ideas to get to one that’s worth building.

And now you have a lot more chances to find it.
