# How to Talk to a Machine

## Why AI Feels Weird to Talk To

Have you ever tried to explain something to someone and watched it bounce right off them? Now imagine explaining something to a “someone” who has read almost everything ever written… but has never lived a single day.

That’s what talking to AI is like.

It can feel awkward at first because you’re speaking to something that’s fluent in language but unfamiliar with your life. It doesn’t see your face. It doesn’t know your habits. It can’t infer what you meant based on tone, context, or history—unless you provide it.

The good news: you don’t need special commands or technical jargon. The skill isn’t “prompt engineering” in the way people often imagine. The skill is simple communication.

## Forget Commands, Think Conversation

One of the most common mistakes is treating AI like an old search engine: a few keywords, no context, minimal detail.

But AI models were trained on human language—full sentences, messy explanations, follow-up questions, clarifications, and all. So the best way to get useful results is to write like a human who is genuinely asking for help.

Instead of:

“yellow leaves pothos”

Try:

“Can you help me understand why my pothos leaves are turning yellow? It’s near a north-facing window and I water it once a week. The soil feels damp for days.”

That’s not “too much.” That’s what makes the answer better. Details narrow the possibilities. They reduce guessing. They give the AI something to work with.

A helpful rule: if you’d tell it to a helpful coworker or friend, you can tell it to the AI.

## Context Is Everything: Stranger vs. Friend

When you talk to a friend, you can skip steps. They already know your preferences and history. When you talk to a stranger, you fill in the blanks.

An AI is always the stranger—at least at the start of a new conversation.

So offer the relevant context upfront. You don’t need your life story. Just the pieces that shape the answer.

For example:

- “I’m writing for small business owners who are skeptical about technology.”
- “Keep the tone friendly but not condescending.”
- “I’m a beginner—avoid jargon.”
- “I already tried X and it didn’t work.”

Think of context as the difference between “help me” and “help me *like this*.”

If you’re using AI for wellness and personal growth, context matters even more. A question like “How do I stop procrastinating?” can be answered a thousand ways. But “I work from home, I’m overwhelmed, and I freeze when tasks feel undefined” gives the AI a clearer map.

## Say What You Want (AI Doesn’t Do Subtext)

Humans communicate in layers. We soften requests. We hint. We expect others to “get it.”

AI doesn’t reliably get it.

If you say, “Maybe shorten this a bit,” you might mean “Please cut it down drastically.” The AI might interpret your softness literally—and barely change anything.

Directness isn’t rude here. It’s generosity.

Try:

- “Please shorten this from 500 words to 300 words.”
- “Keep the main argument and remove repetitive examples.”
- “Rewrite this in a warmer tone, at an 8th-grade reading level.”
- “Give me three options: conservative, balanced, and bold.”

Clarity is kind—because it reduces friction and saves you from doing extra rounds of correction.

## Expect a Back-and-Forth (That’s the Point)

The first response doesn’t have to be the final response.

In fact, the best results usually come from iteration:

- “This is close—make it less formal.”
- “I like the structure—add a real-life example.”
- “The advice feels generic—ask me three questions to personalize it.”
- “That’s too long—keep it under 150 words.”

This isn’t failure. This is collaboration.

A useful mindset shift: treat the AI like a draft partner, not an oracle. You’re not looking for perfection on the first try. You’re shaping something together through conversation.

## Give It a Role to Change the Output

Here’s a surprisingly powerful trick: tell the AI *who it is*.

When you assign a role, you’re nudging the model toward different language patterns and priorities.

For example:

- “You are a patient teacher. Explain this step-by-step.”
- “You are a harsh but fair editor. Point out what’s unclear.”
- “You are a coach. Help me make a realistic plan for this week.”
- “You are a supportive friend. Help me process this situation.”

Roles work because language carries identity. The words we choose change depending on whether we’re teaching, coaching, comforting, or critiquing. When you specify a role, you’re effectively selecting a style—and making the response more predictable.

## The Simple Strategy: Just Talk

If you only remember one thing, let it be this: talk to AI like you’re speaking to a brilliant assistant who’s never met you.

Be clear. Be honest. Provide context. Ask for what you want. And don’t be afraid to revise.

AI won’t read your mind. But if you meet it halfway, it can become a surprisingly useful partner for learning, planning, writing, and reflection.

The conversation is the process—and sometimes, just putting your thoughts into words is the first step toward clarity.
