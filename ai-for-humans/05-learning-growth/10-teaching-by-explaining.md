# Teaching by Explaining

## The Illusion of Understanding

Have you ever walked away from a podcast, a book chapter, or a YouTube video thinking, “Yep, I totally get it”—only to blank the moment someone asks you to explain it?

That’s the illusion of understanding. When information is presented smoothly, our brains confuse recognition with mastery. It *feels* familiar, so we assume it’s stored in a way we can use. But familiarity is not the same as fluency.

In wellness and personal growth, this shows up all the time. We hear phrases like “set boundaries,” “be present,” or “reframe your thoughts,” and they sound right. They’re easy to nod along with. Yet when we try to apply them in a stressful moment—or articulate what they actually mean—we realize we’re holding a concept more like a slogan than a skill.

The uncomfortable truth: if you can’t explain something clearly, you probably don’t understand it as well as you think you do. The good news: that’s not a failure. It’s a map.

## The Gaps Revealed

Explanation is like shining a flashlight into your knowledge. The second you try to teach an idea—even casually, to a friend—you find the parts that are foggy.

You might notice:
- You can describe the *what*, but not the *why*
- You remember a definition, but not an example
- You know the steps, but not when to use them
- You can repeat the concept, but you can’t connect it to real life

These “gaps” are incredibly valuable. Most of us spend a lot of time collecting information and very little time testing whether it actually holds together. Explaining forces a test.

And it does something else too: it makes your thinking visible. When thoughts stay in your head, they can feel coherent even when they’re not. The moment you put them into words, you can hear the wobbles. That’s where learning begins.

Instead of being embarrassed by the gaps, treat them like helpful signals. They’re showing you exactly where to focus next.

## Explanation as Learning Tool

In personal growth, we often assume learning means consuming: reading more, watching more, highlighting more. But one of the fastest ways to deepen understanding is to *produce*—to translate what you’ve absorbed into your own words.

Try this simple practice after you learn something new:

1. **Explain it like you’re talking to a smart friend.** No jargon, no fancy phrasing.
2. **Use a concrete example from your life.** If it can’t touch real life, it’s not integrated.
3. **Identify what you’re unsure about.** Don’t skip it—circle it.
4. **Refine the explanation.** Tighten it until it feels clean and usable.

This is especially powerful in wellness topics because they’re often abstract. “Self-compassion,” for example, isn’t just an idea—it’s a behavior pattern. Explaining it might reveal you only understand the comforting part, not the accountability part. Or you might realize you confuse self-compassion with letting yourself off the hook.

That kind of clarity is gold. It turns inspiration into something you can actually practice.

## The Generation Effect

There’s a reason this works so well: your brain remembers what it *creates*.

Psychologists call this the **generation effect**—we learn better when we generate information ourselves rather than passively receive it. When you explain, you’re not just copying. You’re constructing. You’re making decisions: what matters, what comes first, what connects to what.

That effort acts like a mental workout. It strengthens memory and understanding because you’re building multiple “handles” for the concept: language, examples, structure, meaning.

This is also where AI can be surprisingly helpful—if you use it wisely. Instead of asking an AI to explain everything to you (which can keep you in consumer mode), try using it as a practice partner:

- Ask it to **quiz you** on a concept
- Paste your explanation and ask, **“Where is this unclear?”**
- Request **counterexamples** to test your understanding
- Ask for **a simpler version**, then compare it to yours

The goal isn’t to outsource thinking. It’s to strengthen it. Use tools to support your ability to generate, refine, and apply.

## The Gift of Teaching

Teaching sounds formal, like standing in front of a class. But most teaching is everyday. It’s telling a friend what helped your anxiety. It’s explaining a habit shift to your partner. It’s writing a short post to organize your thoughts. It’s mentoring someone at work. It’s even talking out loud to yourself on a walk.

When you teach, you do something generous: you turn your learning into something shareable. But you also receive a gift in return—your own understanding becomes more durable.

Even better, teaching creates accountability. It encourages you to embody what you’ve learned, not just collect it. You can’t convincingly explain “sleep hygiene” while ignoring your own bedtime. You can’t teach “boundaries” without confronting where you leak your energy.

In that way, teaching becomes a mirror. It shows you what you know—and what you’re ready to live.

## Closing Reflection

If you want to grow faster, don’t just ask, “Do I understand this?” Ask, “Can I explain it simply—and apply it today?” The act of explaining isn’t a final step after learning. It’s part of the learning itself. And the more you practice teaching, the more your knowledge turns into something real: clarity, confidence, and change.
