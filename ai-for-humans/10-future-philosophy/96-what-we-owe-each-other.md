# What We Owe Each Other

## The Village Rule We Forgot
In some villages, when someone builds a house, the whole community shows up.

Not because there’s a check waiting at the end of the day. Not because it’s a formal program. But because that’s what neighbors do. One weekend you’re hauling timber and holding beams steady. Another weekend, when it’s your turn, people arrive with tools and strong arms and the quiet assumption that you matter.

It’s a simple arrangement. And it carries a profound message: our lives are linked.

As artificial intelligence reshapes how we work, earn, learn, and connect, an old question is becoming urgent again—almost unavoidable:

What do we owe each other?

## AI Is Not Just a Tech Shift—It’s a Moral Test
A lot of the AI conversation stays in the realm of productivity hacks and market forecasts. Which jobs will disappear? Which new careers will emerge? Who will “win” the AI race?

Those are real questions. But underneath them is something more personal.

When AI makes certain skills obsolete, what do we owe the people who built their lives around those skills?

When a small group learns how to use AI to multiply their output—and others can’t keep up—what do the capable owe the struggling?

When disruption arrives faster than systems can adapt, what responsibility do we have to the families whose rent, healthcare, and dignity are tied to jobs that may evaporate?

These aren’t abstract policy debates. They’re questions about real neighbors. Real coworkers. Real parents. Real people trying to do their best in a world that’s changing without asking permission.

## A Small Story That Points to a Bigger Truth
Consider Thomas, a developer who started using AI tools and felt his productivity surge. He wasn’t working twice as hard—he was simply working differently. The tools helped him draft faster, debug quicker, and explore ideas with less friction.

But then he noticed something: not everyone around him was benefiting.

Some colleagues looked overwhelmed, quietly anxious, unsure where to begin. Not lazy. Not unintelligent. Just stuck at the edge of a new learning curve while the ground shifted beneath them.

Thomas could have focused on himself. In a competitive workplace, that’s a rational strategy.

Instead, he started hosting informal sessions. Short meetups. “Here’s what I’ve learned.” “Here are prompts that help.” “Here’s how I check output so it’s reliable.” Nothing fancy—just a person refusing to let others fall behind alone.

That instinct matters. Because it’s one of the clearest signs of a healthy community: people don’t treat knowledge as a weapon when it could be used as a bridge.

## Shared Flourishing Beats Solo Advantage
We often talk as if we’re isolated individuals competing for scarce resources.

But in real life, we’re woven into families, workplaces, neighborhoods, and societies. And the truth is: when people around you fall into instability, it doesn’t stay neatly contained. It becomes stress in the culture, resentment in politics, fragility in institutions, and fear in everyday life.

More importantly, it’s simply not how most of us want to live.

There’s something that feels right—deeply right—about refusing to look away when change hurts people. About noticing the person who’s struggling and offering a hand before they have to ask. About being the kind of neighbor you’d hope exists when it’s your turn to need help.

That’s not sentimental. It’s practical. Communities that share burdens tend to survive change better than communities that pretend burdens are someone else’s problem.

## What This Looks Like at Different Scales
The “village raising a roof” principle applies at every level.

In families, it might look like one person helping another learn new tools, update a résumé, practice for interviews, or simply regain confidence. It can be as basic as: “Let’s figure this out together.”

In workplaces, it can mean investing in training rather than quietly replacing people. Building pathways for employees to adapt. Creating internal mentorship instead of turning every new technology into a silent layoff machine.

In society, it raises bigger questions we can’t avoid much longer:

If AI creates abundance, who shares in it?

If it creates disruption, what safety nets do we owe?

Those safety nets might include accessible retraining, stronger unemployment support, portable benefits, wage insurance, or new models of sharing productivity gains. The exact policies are complicated. The moral direction is not: people should not be discarded as collateral damage for progress.

## A Useful Moral Lens: “Not Reasonably Rejectable”
Philosopher T.M. Scanlon offered a simple way to think about morality: act on principles that others couldn’t reasonably reject.

Applied here, it nudges us toward a standard of fairness that isn’t just legal or efficient, but human. If a new system concentrates benefits while pushing the burdens onto the least powerful, the people bearing the burdens can reasonably reject that arrangement.

A community worthy of the name doesn’t treat change like a private lottery where winners owe nothing to losers. It looks for principles that share both the benefits and the costs.

## We Don’t Owe Everything—But We Owe Something
This is where the conversation gets real. “What do we owe each other?” doesn’t mean unlimited responsibility. No single person can fix every disruption. No one can carry the whole roof.

But we do owe something.

Attention. Care. The refusal to look away. A willingness to teach what we know. A habit of checking in. An instinct to build ramps, not walls, as new tools arrive.

Because the village rule isn’t about charity. It’s about belonging.

AI will change many things. But it doesn’t change this: we are bound to one another. If anything, it intensifies that truth—because the pace of change will test whether our communities are real or merely convenient.

And in the end, the measure of a society has always been how it treats those most vulnerable to change.

AI doesn’t change that measure. It raises the stakes.
