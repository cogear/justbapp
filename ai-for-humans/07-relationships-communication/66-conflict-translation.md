# Conflict Translation

## When it feels like you’re speaking different languages

You’re sure you’re right.

The facts line up. Your reasoning is solid. You’ve explained it clearly—twice, maybe three times. And yet the other person keeps responding in a way that makes you wonder if you were even in the same conversation.

It can feel surreal, like you’re talking past each other through thick glass.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: they probably feel the exact same way about you.

Most conflict isn’t a battle between right and wrong. It’s a clash between two people who are each seeing real things—just different things. Different priorities. Different fears. Different definitions of what “success” looks like. Different histories that make certain outcomes feel safe or threatening.

When we don’t recognize that, we assume the other person is being irrational, difficult, or stubborn. And they do the same to us.

## The hidden problem: two monologues, not one conversation

A lot of workplace conflict (and honestly, relationship conflict too) has the same shape:

- You state your position.
- They object.
- You respond with more evidence.
- They respond with more objections.
- Both of you start repeating yourselves, only louder or more detailed.

It’s not a dialogue anymore. It’s two monologues with a brief pause in the middle while the other person reloads their arguments.

The reason this happens is simple: most of us argue our position before we truly understand theirs.

We may think we do—because we can summarize what they said. But “what they said” isn’t the same as “what they’re protecting.”

Under every objection is usually a deeper layer:

- What outcome are they trying to prevent?
- What responsibility do they feel they’re carrying?
- What risk are they anticipating?
- What value are they defending?

If you don’t find that layer, you end up debating the surface while the real issue stays untouched.

## AI as translation—emotional and perspectival

AI can’t fix your conflict for you. That part is human work: courage, humility, repair, boundaries, negotiation.

But AI *can* help you do something powerful before you walk into the conversation: translation.

Not language translation. Perspective translation.

It helps you move from “How can they not see this?” to “What might they be seeing that I’m missing?”

Here’s a simple way to use it:

1. Describe the conflict neutrally.  
   What you proposed, what they said, what happened next. Keep it factual, not editorial.

2. Ask a specific translation question:  
   **“How might they be experiencing this situation?”**  
   Or: **“What legitimate concerns could be underneath their objections?”**

3. Request steelmanning, not mind-reading:  
   **“Help me articulate their position in a way they would agree is accurate.”**

That last part matters. The goal isn’t to invent a story about them. It’s to build the best, most respectful version of their reasoning—so you can actually engage with it.

## What you often discover when you translate

When you invite AI to help you explore the other person’s angle, you’ll often get possibilities that are… annoyingly reasonable.

Maybe they’re seeing risks you minimized:

- The team is already at capacity.
- Implementation will be messy and political.
- The change introduces downstream work no one has budgeted for.
- A previous “simple improvement” turned into a months-long fire drill.

Maybe what looks like stubbornness is actually stewardship.

They might be protecting something they care about: team morale, customer trust, operational stability, a deadline, a reputation. Even if you disagree with their conclusion, you can usually respect the value underneath it.

This is where conflict starts to shift—because you stop treating the other person like an obstacle and start treating them like a human with a job to do.

## The sentence that changes everything

Compare these two responses:

- “I hear you, but…”
- “I think you’re worried this change might overextend the team.”

The first one sounds polite, but often lands as dismissal. It’s a verbal pat on the head before you continue with your argument.

The second one signals something rarer: *recognition.*

When people feel understood, they become more capable of understanding. Nervous systems settle. Defensiveness drops. The conversation becomes less about winning and more about solving.

A useful practice is to aim for a reflection that would make them nod and say, “Yes—exactly.”

AI can help you craft that reflection by giving you language that’s clear, non-accusatory, and anchored in their likely concerns.

## Bringing translation into the real conversation

Once you have a clearer view of their perspective, you can enter the conversation differently.

Instead of leading with your case, you can lead with understanding:

- “I want to make sure I’m getting your concerns right…”
- “It sounds like your main worry is X. Did I capture that?”
- “What would need to be true for this to feel safe and workable on your side?”

This approach doesn’t mean you abandon your position. It means you widen the frame. You stop trying to *push* someone into agreement and start trying to *build* agreement from shared reality.

And once you’re working with the real underlying constraints and values, creative solutions become possible—solutions neither of you could see while you were busy proving each other wrong.

## Closing thought

AI can’t resolve your conflict. But it can prepare you to enter it with curiosity instead of certainty.

And that one shift—walking in ready to translate instead of ready to win—often changes the entire dynamic.

If someone has to go first in creating mutual understanding, it might as well be you.
