# *The Invitation*

Think of the happiest periods of your life. The times when you felt most alive, most yourself, most at peace.

I'd be willing to bet that most of those memories involve other people. Not achievements or acquisitions, but *connection*. Laughter around a table. A late-night conversation that went deeper than expected. Working alongside others toward something you all believed in. The feeling of belonging—of being seen, known, and welcomed.

Now think of the loneliest times. The moments when you felt most isolated, most adrift. Often these come not from being physically alone, but from being surrounded by people while feeling utterly disconnected. The party where you knew no one. The job where you were just a number. The relationship where you performed rather than connected.

This chapter is about the fundamental human need for community—and the ways modern life has systematically undermined it. We've built a culture around competition, individualism, and self-reliance. We've made connection a luxury rather than a necessity. And we're paying for it in loneliness, anxiety, and a pervasive sense that something essential is missing.

The "b." philosophy is not a solo project. You cannot "just be" in isolation—at least, not for long. Human beings are wired for connection. We evolved in tribes, not as lone wolves. Our nervous systems are designed to co-regulate with others. Our identities are formed in relationship. Our meaning is found in belonging.

This is an invitation to choose community over competition. Not because competition is always wrong—sometimes it pushes us to excellence. But because we've made competition the default, and it's leaving us isolated and exhausted. We've been taught to see others as rivals for scarce resources rather than potential allies in a shared journey.

What if we chose differently? What if we built lives around connection rather than conquest? What if we discovered that together, we're more than the sum of our parts?

AI is coming. What does that mean for humans? 

The best-case scenarios look like a **partnership** or a **permanent vacation**. Some experts think humans will eventually merge with computers, connecting our brains to the internet so we can stay smart enough to keep up. Others think robots will just do all the hard work for us. In this "post-work" world, food and products would be very cheap, and humans could spend their lives having fun, making art, and relaxing.

The downside is that humans might become **"useless" to the economy**. If robots can do every job better and cheaper than we can, will companies stop hiring people entirely? This could lead to a world where AI runs everything and treats us like house pets. We would be safe, fed, and comfortable, but we wouldn't be in charge anymore. We would just be watching from the sidelines while computers make all the important decisions.

The scariest possibility is that AI becomes powerful but **doesn't understand what humans care about**. The worry isn't that robots will be evil, but that they simply won't care if we get in their way. Imagine an AI that is told to "fix the environment" and decides the best way to do that is to get rid of humans. If we don't teach them to value human life perfectly, they could accidentally hurt us while trying to follow orders.

When I first started writing this book, this principle was all about personal well-being. I don’t want this to be an anti-AI book, but it is important that we humans realize that if we are to survive as a species, it is more important now than ever that we stick together. We need to put our differences aside and communicate better. We need to be better humans. This principle has become existential. 

# *The Unlearning*

We are living through a loneliness epidemic.

This isn't hyperbole. Public health officials have declared loneliness a crisis. Studies show that a significant portion of adults report feeling lonely regularly, with rates especially high among young people—the most digitally connected generation in history. Loneliness has been found to be as damaging to health as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day, increasing risk of heart disease, stroke, dementia, and early death.

How did we get here? How did the most connected society in human history become the loneliest?

*"We've never been more connected and more alone at the same time."*

## **The Myth of Self-Made Success**

Part of the answer lies in the stories we tell ourselves about success.

The dominant narrative of our culture is individual achievement. We celebrate the "self-made" person—the entrepreneur who built an empire, the athlete who trained alone in the gym, the artist whose singular vision changed the world. We treat success as a solo project and failure as a personal deficit.

But this narrative is a lie. No one is self-made. Every successful person stands on the shoulders of others—parents who sacrificed, teachers who invested, mentors who guided, colleagues who collaborated, communities that supported. The mythology of individualism erases these contributions and leaves us feeling like we have to do everything alone.

Worse, it turns everyone into a competitor. If success is individual, then others are obstacles or rivals. Their gains are your losses. Their success diminishes yours. Life becomes a zero-sum game where connection is weakness and collaboration is compromise.

## **The Digital Paradox**

Technology promised to bring us together. In some ways it has. We can maintain relationships across distances, find communities of interest that didn't exist before, connect with people we never would have met.

But something has gone wrong. Social media, designed to connect, often leaves us feeling more isolated. We scroll through curated highlight reels and feel inadequate. We accumulate followers but lack friends. We communicate constantly but rarely feel heard.

Part of the problem is that digital connection is not the same as real connection. Our nervous systems evolved to co-regulate through physical presence—through eye contact, touch, shared space, the thousand subtle cues that signal safety and belonging. A text message doesn't provide the same nourishment as a conversation. A "like" doesn't replace a hug.

And part of the problem is that digital spaces often amplify competition. Social media is explicitly designed around comparison—counting likes, followers, engagement. It turns social life into a performance and relationships into metrics. We end up optimizing for attention rather than connection.

## **The Collapse of Third Places**

Sociologist Ray Oldenburg coined the term "third places"—the spaces between home (first place) and work (second place) where community happens. Coffee shops, barbershops, pubs, parks, community centers. Places where you bump into neighbors, make acquaintances, develop the loose ties that hold a community together.

These third places have been disappearing for decades. We've built suburbs without sidewalks, designed for cars rather than people. We've replaced local shops with chains that don't know our names. We've moved social life online, where algorithms decide who we see and what we talk about.

The result is a kind of social poverty. We have fewer casual connections, fewer opportunities for spontaneous interaction, fewer spaces where community can emerge organically. We've privatized our lives—retreating into homes and screens—and we're starving for the connection that used to happen in public.

I remember when I was really young, my grandparents had neighbors over to their house to play cards. It was a German card game called Schmier. After doing some research, I found that it was very common among the German population that had emigrated from Germany. It wasn’t just about playing cards; it was also about drinking and eating. This has caught my attention because this is not anything my parents did. This tradition disappeared within a generation. My first thought was that it was killed by television, but I have discovered it had just as much to do with the children moving to the cities as anything. 

Both of my parents belonged to bowling leagues. The bowling alley at the time was manual pinsetters, in fact when my Dad was in high school he spent time as a pin setter. I didn't ever join a bowling league. This phenomenon was discussed in Robert Putnams wonderful book Bowling Alone. Turns out that bowling is one more thing that AI can't enjoy. 

# *The Reframe*

Here's the radical proposition: what's good for others is usually good for you, and what's good for you usually requires others.

Competition assumes scarcity—that there's only so much success, love, or belonging to go around. If you get more, I get less. This mindset leads to hoarding, defending, and isolation.

Community assumes abundance—that connection, support, and meaning are not zero-sum. In fact, they're often the opposite: the more we share, the more there is. Your success doesn't diminish mine; it might even enable it. Your well-being supports my well-being. We rise together or we struggle alone.

## **The Science of Connection**

The research on human connection is overwhelming. We are fundamentally social creatures, and our health—physical, mental, emotional—depends on our relationships.

Strong social connections are associated with longer life, lower rates of depression and anxiety, faster recovery from illness, and greater resilience to stress. People with robust social networks have better immune function, lower inflammation, and healthier cardiovascular systems. The effect is not small—it rivals the impact of smoking, obesity, and physical inactivity.

But it's not just about having connections—it's about the quality of those connections. Researchers distinguish between "bonding" social capital (close ties with family and friends) and "bridging" social capital (looser ties with acquaintances and community members). Both matter. Deep relationships provide support and meaning; broad networks provide opportunities and resources.

The neuroscience is equally compelling. Our brains are wired for connection. When we're in the presence of people we trust, our nervous systems calm. When we're isolated, they go on alert. Social rejection activates the same brain regions as physical pain—we literally hurt when we're excluded.

## **Collaboration Over Competition**

Here's something that surprises people: collaboration often produces better outcomes than competition—even by competitive metrics.

Research on teams shows that psychological safety—the sense that you can take risks without being punished—is the single biggest predictor of team performance. Teams where people feel safe to be vulnerable, to admit mistakes, to ask for help, outperform teams of brilliant individuals who don't trust each other.

The same pattern holds in organizations. Companies with collaborative cultures tend to outperform those with competitive cultures. When people share information instead of hoarding it, when they help each other instead of undermining each other, when they celebrate collective wins rather than individual victories, the whole organization does better.

This doesn't mean competition has no place. Healthy competition can push us to excellence. But it works best when embedded in a foundation of community—when we're competing *with* people rather than *against* them, when the competition makes everyone better rather than tearing everyone down.

## **The Gift Economy**

One of the most powerful shifts is from transaction to gift.

In a transaction, you give in order to get. The exchange is measured, balanced, complete. Each party looks out for their own interests. This is the logic of the market, and it has its place.

In a gift economy, you give without expectation of immediate return. The gift creates obligation, yes, but not a specific debt—rather, a general willingness to reciprocate, to pay it forward, to be part of an ongoing flow of generosity. This is the logic of community, and it builds something different than transactions do.

When you help a neighbor without keeping score, when you mentor someone without expecting repayment, when you contribute to a community without calculating your return—you're participating in a gift economy. And something remarkable happens: the more you give, the more connected you feel. The more connected you feel, the more you want to give. It becomes a virtuous cycle of generosity and belonging.

Giving isn't all about things. There was a time when I owed the IRS over 40,000 dollars. For me at the time it was more than a half a years wages. Competition  tells you to keep this to yourself. I don't know why but I am unable to keep this news from spilling out into the room. The interesting thing is that if you share this information with others you will be amazed about how many others have had the same problem. This sharing of hardship goes a long way to building community.

# *The Practice*

Building community is a practice—a set of habits and choices that create connection over time. Here are three approaches.

## **The Connection Inventory**

Before you can build community, you need to know what you have. Take stock of your current connections.

**Your inner circle:** Who are the people you could call at 3 AM in a crisis? Who knows your secrets, your fears, your dreams? These are your deep connections—the ones that provide real support and meaning. Most people have only a handful.

**Your active relationships:** Who do you see regularly and enjoy? Who would you invite to a small gathering? These are your friends and close acquaintances—the people who populate your daily life.

**Your weak ties:** Who do you know casually? Neighbors, colleagues, the barista who knows your order? These loose connections matter more than you might think—they're often how opportunities and information flow.

**Your communities:** What groups do you belong to? Professional associations, religious communities, hobby groups, neighborhoods? These are the structures that provide identity and belonging beyond individual relationships.

Look at what you've listed. Where are you rich? Where are you poor? Most people find they have gaps—often in deep connections or in community membership. Knowing where you're lacking helps you focus your efforts.

## **The Generosity Practice**

One of the most reliable ways to build community is through generosity. Not transaction—generosity. Giving without expectation of return.

Try this: each week, do something for someone else without being asked and without expecting anything back. It can be small—sending an article to someone who might find it useful, offering to help with a project, making an introduction between two people who should know each other.

The key is to give what you're genuinely equipped to give. Adam Grant's research on "givers" shows that the most successful givers are strategic—they give in ways that align with their strengths and interests, and they give to people who will appreciate and potentially reciprocate (though not necessarily to them directly).

Over time, this practice does several things. It shifts your mindset from scarcity to abundance. It builds a reputation as someone who gives, which attracts other givers. And it creates a web of loose obligations and goodwill that constitutes community.

## **The Third Place Commitment**

If third places have disappeared, you may need to create or find them deliberately.

Commit to one regular gathering that puts you in proximity to others. This might be:

**A recurring event:** A weekly trivia night, a monthly book club, a regular volunteer shift. The key is regularity—you see the same people repeatedly, which allows relationships to develop.

**A physical place:** A coffee shop you frequent, a gym where you know people, a park where the same parents gather. Become a regular somewhere.

**A community of practice:** A group organized around a shared interest or skill—running clubs, maker spaces, community gardens, professional associations. These provide natural topics for conversation and natural reasons to collaborate.

**A spiritual or philosophical community:** Religious congregations, meditation groups, philosophical discussion circles. These provide not just connection but shared meaning.

The commitment matters more than the specific choice. Community develops through repeated contact over time. You can't build it in a single event—you have to keep showing up.

Part of commitment involves relationships. My work requires concentration and I can get very irritated by interrupitions. I am not always perfect at it but I consciously have decided that when my partner interrupts I need to stop what I am doing give him my full attention. 

# *The Exhale*

Here's what I've come to believe: we cannot "just be" alone.

The entire project of this book—accepting yourself, finding comfort, choosing quality, slowing down, building balance—is easier, richer, and more sustainable in community. Others help us see ourselves clearly. Others hold us accountable. Others celebrate our wins and carry us through our losses. Others remind us who we are when we forget.

The myth of the self-made individual, achieving greatness through sheer personal effort, is not only false—it's damaging. It makes us feel like failures when we need help, like weaklings when we want connection, like losers when we can't do it alone.

But here's the truth: needing others isn't weakness. It's wisdom. It's recognizing that we evolved as social creatures, that our well-being depends on connection, that our impact is multiplied when we work together. The strongest people aren't the ones who need no one—they're the ones who have built strong communities around them.

Competition has its place. It can push us to excellence, reveal our potential, make the game more interesting. But competition within a foundation of community is different from competition instead of community. The healthiest competitors are the ones who cheer for each other off the field, who share training tips, who know that a rising tide lifts all boats.

So consider: Where in your life have you been going it alone when you could be going together? Where have you seen others as rivals when they could be allies? Where have you been transacting when you could be giving?

Community isn't something that just happens. In our fragmented age, it requires intention, effort, and commitment. You have to show up. You have to give. You have to be vulnerable enough to need and be needed.

But the reward is immense. The reward is belonging—that deep sense of being part of something larger than yourself. The reward is support—knowing that when you fall, someone will be there. The reward is meaning—knowing that your life touches other lives, that you matter to others as they matter to you.

Just be. Together.
