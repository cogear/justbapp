# **Chapter Two**

# **The Revolutionary Act of Getting Comfortable**

## ***Comfort as Achievement***

*"Almost everything will work again if you unplug it for a few minutes, including you."*

*— Anne Lamott*

## ***The Invitation***

Picture this: A Sunday afternoon. Rain against the windows. You're wrapped in something soft, holding something warm. There's nowhere you need to be, nothing demanding your attention. For this hour, this moment, you are simply comfortable.

Now notice what happens in your mind. If you're like most people, a voice appears almost immediately. *Shouldn't you be doing something?* it asks. *There's laundry. There are emails. You could be productive.*

That voice—the one that can't let you rest without guilt—is the subject of this chapter. Not because we're going to fight it, but because we're going to question something it takes for granted: the idea that comfort must be earned, that rest is a reward for labor, and that being cozy is somehow less valuable than being productive.

What if comfort itself were an achievement?

**This chapter argues that comfort is not the absence of achievement—comfort is an achievement. Creating genuine conditions of ease and restoration is a skill, a practice, and a form of wisdom that our culture has systematically devalued.**

Not comfort as escape—numbing yourself with screens or substances to avoid life. Not comfort as laziness—an unwillingness to engage with anything difficult. But comfort as a deliberate practice. As something you create, cultivate, and protect. As a form of wisdom in a world that has forgotten how to rest.

In the pages ahead, we'll first diagnose the problem: how our culture came to treat rest as suspicious and comfort as something that must be earned (The Unlearning). Then we'll reframe the issue, exploring what comfort actually does for our bodies and why the Danes have built an entire cultural practice around it (The Reframe). Finally, we'll turn to practical tools: how to create spaces, rituals, and permissions that allow you to experience genuine restoration (The Practice). Throughout, I'll invite you to notice something specific: the deep exhale that happens when you finally let yourself settle. That exhale is our compass. It tells us when we've arrived.

The Danes have a word for what we're after: *hygge* (pronounced "hoo-gah"). It's often translated as "coziness," but that doesn't quite capture it. Hygge is the art of creating warmth and well-being through intentional comfort—candles and blankets, yes, but also presence, connection, and a deliberate slowing down. Denmark consistently ranks among the happiest countries on Earth, despite dark winters and high taxes. Researchers believe hygge is part of the reason why.

This chapter is an invitation to take comfort seriously. Not as a guilty pleasure or an occasional treat, but as a fundamental human need—and one that our culture has systematically devalued in favor of relentless achievement.

It's time to redefine what success looks like. And sometimes, success looks like a warm mug, a soft blanket, and absolutely nowhere to be.

As I write this, there's a chill in the air. I'm in my office—not really a comfort zone, but a safe place. Maybe because I seem to have some control over it. I'm wrapped in my leopard-print throw with a hot cup of coffee and old familiar music on the headphones. This combination seems to be working.

I know that outside the door there's an argument waiting for me. But for now, I am here. I also know I don't do this perfectly—I'm cheating by combining productivity with comfort. Am I okay with that? Sure. Comfort doesn't require purity. It requires presence. And right now, I notice a small exhale as I settle deeper into the chair. That's the signal. That's what we're learning to recognize.

## ***The Unlearning***

*The Diagnosis:* Before we can practice comfort without guilt, we need to understand where the guilt comes from. The answer lies in our culture's complicated history with rest.

We live in a culture that treats rest as suspicious.

Think about the language we use. We "steal" moments of peace. We feel "guilty" about relaxing. We have to "earn" our weekends. The underlying message is clear: comfort is a reward for labor, not a legitimate state of being. If you're comfortable, you must have finished something. Otherwise, what right do you have to rest?

This wasn't always the case. Before the Industrial Revolution reshaped our relationship with time and productivity, humans understood something we've largely forgotten: that periods of ease and restoration were essential to life, not indulgences to be earned.

*"We have confused busyness with virtue and exhaustion with commitment."*

The modern cult of productivity has roots in Puritan theology, which viewed idleness as morally suspect. This attitude was turbocharged by industrial capitalism, which needed workers who would see rest as waste. And it's been supercharged again by our digital age, where we carry our offices in our pockets and "hustle culture" influencers preach that sleep is for the unsuccessful.

The through-line is clear: *Puritans taught us rest was sinful → factories taught us rest was wasteful → smartphones taught us rest was impossible.* Each era added another layer of guilt to the simple act of being comfortable.

The result? We're exhausted. Not just physically, but spiritually. We've lost the ability to be still without feeling like we're falling behind. We've forgotten how to exhale.

## ***The Comfort Deficit***

Here's what the research tells us: we are chronically under-rested and over-stimulated.

Studies show that the average person checks their phone over 150 times per day. We consume more information before 9 AM than our great-grandparents did in their entire lives. Our nervous systems are in a constant state of low-grade alert, flooded with cortisol and adrenaline, never quite settling into the parasympathetic "rest and digest" mode that allows for genuine restoration.

And when we do try to relax, we often don't know how. We "rest" by scrolling through social media—which is actually stimulating, not calming. We watch television while also checking our phones. We're physically still but mentally racing.

True comfort—the kind that actually restores us—requires something more deliberate. It requires creating conditions that allow our nervous systems to genuinely settle. It requires an environment, a mindset, and crucially, permission.

## ***The Permission Problem***

For many people, the biggest barrier to comfort isn't time or resources—it's the inability to let themselves have it.

I've spoken with people who have beautiful homes they never enjoy because they're always working. Who own comfortable clothes they never wear because they feel like they should be "put together." Who have the means for leisure but fill every moment with productivity because sitting still feels like failure.

This is the permission problem. Somewhere along the way, we absorbed the message that we don't deserve comfort unless we've earned it. And since there's always more to do—always another email, another project, another way we could be improving ourselves—we never quite arrive at the place where comfort feels justified.

The result is a strange kind of poverty: surrounded by the materials of comfort, unable to experience it.

I want to be honest here: not everyone's barriers are purely psychological. Some constraints are real. If you're working two jobs to pay rent, if you're a caregiver with no backup, if you're dealing with chronic pain or illness—the advice to "just relax" can feel tone-deaf or even cruel. Comfort requires resources that not everyone has equally.

This chapter isn't about pretending those constraints don't exist. It's about working with whatever margin you have—even if that margin is tiny. A thirty-second exhale in the car before walking into the house. A single cup of tea held with both hands. Five minutes of warmth before the day begins. Micro-comforts count. They're not a consolation prize; they're a practice. And they're available to almost everyone, even when larger rest isn't.

Of course, not all stress is imagined. Even if our wants are right-sized, we still need to provide for the basics in life. I'm sure the basics I have in mind are more than I really need, but still—there are bills to be paid. They're a powerful motivator and a convenient excuse to keep pushing yourself.

Even as I write this, I feel the nagging insistence on accomplishing something. But here's what I've come to understand: ten minutes of doing nothing isn't going to stop the world. People talk about "living in the now." I've always had a problem with that phrase, because the way I see it, how can you not? I'm not a time traveler. I have no choice but to be here now. The question isn't whether I'm in the present—it's whether I'm *at peace* in the present. Whether I can find that exhale.

## ***The Reframe***

*The Theory:* Now that we've diagnosed the problem—a culture that has systematically devalued rest—we can reframe what comfort actually is and why it matters.

Here's the radical proposition: **Comfort is not the absence of achievement. Comfort is an achievement.**

Creating genuine conditions of ease and well-being is a skill. It requires self-knowledge (what actually restores you?), intentionality (creating environments that support rest), and often, courage (choosing comfort over the endless demands of productivity culture).

Think about what it takes to be truly comfortable. You have to know yourself well enough to recognize what you need—not what Instagram tells you relaxation should look like, but what actually works for your particular nervous system. You have to be willing to protect that time and space from the thousand intrusions that threaten it. And you have to override years of conditioning that tell you rest is laziness.

That's not nothing. That's hard-won wisdom.

## ***The Science of Restoration***

Let's talk about what comfort actually does for you physiologically.

When you're in a genuinely comfortable state—warm, safe, at ease—your nervous system shifts from sympathetic (fight-or-flight) to parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) activation. Your heart rate slows. Your blood pressure drops. Stress hormones like cortisol decrease while restorative hormones increase.

This isn't just "feeling nice." This is your body doing essential maintenance. During parasympathetic states, your immune system functions better. Your digestion improves. Your brain consolidates memories and processes emotions. Sleep becomes more restorative. This is when healing happens—physically and psychologically.

Here's what this looks like in practice. Imagine two versions of an evening:

**Before:** You come home exhausted. You collapse on the couch with your phone, scrolling through news and social media while the TV plays in the background. Two hours pass. You feel vaguely guilty about "wasting time" but also still wired. You go to bed but can't fall asleep. Your body never got the signal that it was safe to rest.

**After:** You come home exhausted. You change into soft clothes, dim the lights, and make a cup of tea. You sit in a specific chair you've designated for rest—no phone, no screens. You do nothing for twenty minutes except feel the warmth of the mug, the softness of the blanket. You notice a deep exhale somewhere around minute five. Your shoulders drop. By bedtime, you're genuinely tired rather than wired. You sleep deeply.

The time investment is similar. The physiological outcome is completely different. The second version triggers parasympathetic activation; the first keeps you stuck in low-grade sympathetic arousal. *Comfort is an achievement* because it produces measurable restoration—and that takes skill.

In other words, comfort isn't a break from your life. It's what allows your life to continue functioning. Treating it as optional or earned is like saying you'll only breathe after you've accomplished enough.

## ***The Hygge Model***

The Danes don't see comfort as something separate from achievement. They see it as central to a good life. This is why *hygge* matters to our argument: it's proof that an entire culture can treat comfort as an achievement worth pursuing, and that doing so makes people happier.

*Hygge* is woven into Danish culture. It's not a luxury or an indulgence; it's a practice, a priority, almost a civic value. Danes create hygge deliberately—through lighting (candles everywhere, soft lamps instead of harsh overheads), through food (simple, warm, shared), through clothing (comfortable fabrics, no need to "look good" at home), and through presence (putting phones away, actually engaging with the people around them).

And it works. Despite dark winters, high taxes, and a climate that makes Florida seem tropical, Denmark consistently ranks among the happiest nations on Earth. Researchers have tried to explain this through various factors—social trust, work-life balance, the welfare state—but many believe hygge itself is part of the equation. When a culture actively values and practices comfort, its people are happier.

The lesson for us is specific: *hygge proves that comfort can be treated as a skill and a value, not just an indulgence.* The Danes don't feel guilty about creating coziness—they've made it a national practice. We can learn from this. Happiness isn't just about achieving more. It's about creating conditions for contentment. And you don't have to move to Scandinavia to do it.

## ***Comfort Without Guilt***

The key word in this reframe is *without guilt*.

Guilty comfort isn't really comfort at all. When you're mentally beating yourself up for resting, your nervous system doesn't settle. You're physically still but psychologically stressed. The restoration you need doesn't happen. There's no exhale.

True comfort requires a shift in how you understand your own worth. You have to believe—really believe, not just intellectually acknowledge—that you deserve rest simply because you're a living being that requires rest. Not because you've earned it. Not after you've accomplished enough. But as a baseline condition of human existence.

This is harder than it sounds. Many of us have built our entire identities around productivity. We derive our sense of worth from what we accomplish. The idea that we might be valuable simply by existing—and therefore deserving of comfort simply by existing—can feel almost transgressive.

But it's true. And it's the foundation on which genuine comfort must be built.

My favorite comfort ritual is the hot tub. There's something about the indulgence of a soak that allows me to turn everything off. I let the heat sink into my bones and my mind go blank. This is usually a meditative time for me and my partner—I think it's when we feel the closest, when we can share this experience without needing to fill it with words. And if we're lucky, we'll see a launch from the nearby Cape Canaveral, a streak of light climbing into the dark.

There's always a deep sigh that accompanies my entry into the hot water. That sigh—that exhale—is what real comfort feels like. The body letting go of what it's been carrying. I've come to recognize that exhale as the signal that I've actually arrived at rest, not just physically stopped moving.

## ***The Practice***

*The Tools:* We've diagnosed the cultural forces that make rest feel guilty, and we've reframed comfort as a genuine achievement. Now we turn to practical application: how do you actually build this skill?

Comfort, like any skill, improves with practice. Here are three approaches to cultivating it deliberately. Each one builds toward that deep exhale—the signal that your nervous system has actually settled.

## ***Creating a Comfort Sanctuary***

Your physical environment matters more than you might think. When a space signals safety and ease to your nervous system, you settle faster and more completely.

Start small. You don't need to redesign your entire home. Choose one corner, one chair, one spot that will become your designated comfort zone.

**Lighting:** Harsh overhead lights trigger alertness. Soft, warm lighting signals rest. Consider candles, salt lamps, or simply a lamp with a warm-toned bulb. The Danes light more candles per capita than any other nation. There's a reason.

**Texture:** Softness matters. A blanket that actually feels good against your skin. A pillow that supports without strain. Fabrics you want to touch—cotton, linen, wool. Your body responds to texture on a level below conscious thought.

**Temperature:** Slight warmth helps the body relax. A warm drink, a space heater on cool days, layers you can adjust. The sensation of being gently warm (not hot) is deeply soothing to the nervous system.

**Scent:** Our sense of smell connects directly to the brain's emotional centers. Familiar, pleasant scents—lavender, vanilla, cedar, whatever you associate with safety—can shift your state faster than almost anything else. Find what works for you.

**Reduction:** Sometimes it's about what you remove. Screens, clutter, visual noise. A clear space creates mental clarity. Comfort zones should be sanctuaries from stimulation, not sources of it.

## ***The Comfort Inventory***

Not everyone finds comfort in the same things. What works for your partner or your best friend might leave you cold. The key is specificity—knowing exactly what your nervous system needs.

Take some time to create a personal comfort inventory. Ask yourself:

*What sensory experiences soothe me?* (Specific textures, sounds, temperatures, tastes, smells)

*What activities actually restore me?* (Not what should restore me—what actually does? For some it's reading; for others, a particular kind of music; for others, gentle movement)

*What social configurations feel restful?* (Solitude? One close friend? A small gathering? Quiet parallel presence with a partner?)

*What time of day am I most able to settle?* (Mornings before the world wakes? Late nights? Weekend afternoons?)

*What conditions destroy my comfort?* (Knowing what to avoid is as important as knowing what to seek)

Write these down. Treat your comfort inventory as important information about yourself—because it is.

## ***The Permission Ritual***

For many people, the hardest part of comfort is the permission. Here's a simple practice for giving it to yourself.

Before entering a period of intended rest, pause. Take three slow breaths. Then say to yourself—out loud if possible:

*"I give myself permission to be comfortable right now."*

Then add:

*"I don't have to earn this."*

It sounds almost absurdly simple. But for people who have spent years unable to rest without guilt, naming the permission explicitly can be powerful. You're interrupting the automatic programming that says rest must be earned. You're overriding the voice that insists you should be productive.

The first few times, it might feel strange or even false. That's okay. Keep doing it. You're building a new neural pathway, one that eventually will allow you to settle into comfort without having to consciously permit it.

Eventually, the permission becomes automatic. But it has to be practiced first.

Let me tell you about someone I know—I'll call her Sarah. She's a project manager, always on, phone always buzzing. She came to this practice skeptical. "I don't have time to sit around giving myself pep talks," she told me. But she tried it anyway—thirty seconds before sitting down in the evening. Just the two sentences.

The first week, nothing changed. She said the words but didn't feel them. The second week, she noticed something: on the nights she did the ritual, she reached for her phone less. By week three, she reported something she hadn't expected—she was actually *enjoying* her evenings for the first time in years. "It's like I gave my brain permission to stop," she said. "And it actually listened."

The measurable benefit? She started sleeping better. Her doctor noted her blood pressure had dropped at her next checkup. She was more patient with her kids. All from thirty seconds of explicit permission, practiced consistently. *Comfort is an achievement*—and like any achievement, it compounds over time.

I lose energy in the early afternoon, once the coffee dissipates. This is my time for a nap. I'm far from good at it—I can't turn off the noise in my head on demand—so I trick my brain by putting on a familiar podcast or TV show. Something that feels comfortable and doesn't grab my attention. The familiarity lets me drift rather than engage.

I'm going to do that now, actually. Maybe you should try it too.

## ***The Exhale***

There's a particular kind of sigh that happens when you finally let yourself be comfortable. A deep exhale that seems to come from somewhere below your chest. A physical release of tension you didn't even know you were holding.

That exhale is what we're after. Not just the conditions of comfort—the blanket, the tea, the quiet room—but the internal release that allows those conditions to actually reach you. It's the moment when comfort stops being a concept and becomes an experience.

Here's what I've learned: comfort is an act of trust. Trust that the world won't fall apart if you stop managing it for an hour. Trust that you're valuable even when you're not producing. Trust that rest is not weakness but wisdom.

In a culture that treats every moment as an opportunity for optimization, choosing comfort is almost rebellious. It's a statement: *I refuse to see myself as a machine to be maximized. I am a living being that requires rest. And I will take it.*

The Danes understood something that our culture has forgotten. They built a practice—*hygge*—around the simple truth that human beings need warmth, softness, and ease. Not occasionally, not as a reward, but regularly, deliberately, as a foundation for everything else.

You can build this too. You can create spaces of comfort, rituals of rest, moments when you are simply and completely at ease. And when you do—when you learn to settle into comfort without guilt—something remarkable happens.

You find that you have more energy, not less. That rest isn't the opposite of productivity but its foundation. That the world doesn't fall apart when you stop carrying it.

You find that comfort, practiced deliberately, is not escape from life but engagement with it. The deepest kind of engagement: being fully present, fully at ease, fully here.

That's not laziness. That's not giving up. That's the wisest thing you can do.

Just be comfortable. Without apology. Without guilt. Without the nagging sense that you should be doing something else.

This, too, is an achievement.

But I want to add something: there's also value in being uncomfortable. A friend of mine was an avid biker. We were talking once about getting soaked on a local trip, and he told me, "It's the rain you remember." He meant that the difficult moments—the cold, the wet, the struggle—are what make the comfortable moments matter. The magic of comfort requires the contrast of chaos. You can't truly appreciate warmth until you've been cold.

So rest deeply when you rest. And when life is hard, know that you're earning an appreciation for comfort that will make it even sweeter.

Here is what I want you to remember: **Comfort is an achievement, and the exhale is the proof.** Find your exhale today.
