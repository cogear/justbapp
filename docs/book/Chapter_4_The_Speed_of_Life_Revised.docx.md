# **Chapter Four**

# **The Speed of Life**

## ***Slow Down Intentionally***

*"There is more to life than increasing its speed."*

*— Mahatma Gandhi*

## ***The Invitation***

Quick: What did you have for breakfast three days ago?

Can't remember? Neither can most people. And it's not because the meal was unremarkable—it's because we weren't there for it. We were checking email, scrolling headlines, planning the day ahead, mentally rehearsing a conversation. The food went in, but we missed it. We were already somewhere else.

Now try this: Think of a meal you *do* remember vividly. Maybe a dinner on vacation, or a holiday at someone's home, or an evening with people you love. What made it memorable wasn't just the food—it was the pace. The lingering. The fact that no one was rushing to the next thing.

This is the difference between experiencing your life and merely passing through it. Breakfast will serve as our guide through this chapter—we'll return to it at the end, transformed.

**This chapter's premise: Speed has become our unexamined default, and it's costing us our lives—not in years, but in moments. The remedy is intentional pace: choosing when to move fast and when to slow down, rather than letting acceleration choose for us.**

We live in an age of acceleration. Everything moves faster—communication, transportation, information, expectation. The average attention span has shrunk. The pace of work has intensified. We speak of "life hacks" and "productivity" and "optimization," as if existence were a machine to be tuned for maximum output.

And we wonder why we feel so empty. Why, despite all the efficiency, do we seem to have less time than ever? Why do our days blur together and years vanish, and we can't remember what we had for breakfast?

This chapter offers three practical remedies: **transition rituals** that create space between activities, **single-tasking hours** that restore presence, and **slow meals** that train the capacity to savor. But first, we need to understand how we got here—and why the solution requires more than just "trying to relax."

Just be. Slowly, sometimes. Intentionally, always.

I've known for years how to step away from a problem. Walk away, and usually a solution comes to me before long. But even knowing this, it's rarely my first instinct. *Let me try one more thing—I'm almost there.* The frustration builds to a breaking point before I finally pause. I fight the very thing that helps me most. The wisdom to slow down sits right there in my head, and I ignore it, convinced that more speed, more effort, more pushing will get me there faster. It never does.

## ***The Unlearning***

*The Problem:* Before we can slow down, we need to understand the forces that keep us speeding up. The acceleration isn't accidental—it's built into our technology and our culture.

Speed is so deeply embedded in modern life that we barely notice it anymore. Like fish who don't know they're wet, we swim in acceleration and mistake it for normal.

Consider how recently everything changed. For most of human history, the pace of life was determined by natural rhythms—sunrise and sunset, seasons, the speed of walking, the growth of crops. A letter took weeks to arrive. News traveled slowly. People lived and died in the same village, and time moved at a human scale.

Then, in what amounts to a blink in evolutionary terms, everything accelerated. The telegraph, the railroad, the automobile, the airplane. Each innovation compressed time and space. What once took days took hours; what took hours took minutes. And we adapted—or thought we did.

*"We have more time-saving devices than ever, and less time than ever."*

The digital revolution accelerated the acceleration. Email made communication instant. Smartphones made us reachable everywhere, always. Social media created a twenty-four-hour stream of content demanding attention. We carry the entire world's information in our pockets and check it a hundred times a day.

And the strange thing is: we're busier than ever. All that time-saving technology hasn't given us more leisure—it's raised expectations. What once took a week is now expected in a day. What took a day is expected in an hour. We've used our efficiency gains to do more, not to rest more.

## ***The Cult of Busy***

Somewhere along the way, busyness became a status symbol.

Ask someone how they're doing, and the answer is almost always "busy" or "so busy" or "crazy busy." We say it almost with pride, as if being overwhelmed were an achievement rather than a problem. To be busy is to be important, in demand, valuable. To not be busy is to be somehow less than.

This is a modern phenomenon. For most of history, leisure was the status symbol. The wealthy didn't work; they had others work for them. Busyness was for servants and laborers. The aspiration was a life of ease, not a life of constant activity.

Now we've inverted it. The CEO who brags about eighty-hour weeks. The entrepreneur who "hustles" while others sleep. The parent who shuttles children between a dizzying array of activities. We've made busyness a virtue and rest a vice.

But here's what the research shows: chronic rushing and time pressure are associated with worse health outcomes, lower life satisfaction, and—paradoxically—lower productivity. A 2015 study in the *Journal of Consumer Research* found that people who feel "time-poor" experience lower well-being regardless of their actual schedules. The cult of busy is making us sick, unhappy, and less effective.

The comedian Mitch Hedberg captured this absurdity perfectly. When a manager once asked him to "look busy" despite having no actual work to do, he suggested the manager simply pretend he looked busy instead. It's a joke that lands because we recognize the truth in it: how often do we value the *appearance* of productivity over the real thing? How much of our rushing is performance rather than progress?

## ***The Time Famine***

Researchers have a term for what many of us experience: "time famine." It's the chronic feeling of not having enough time, regardless of how much time we actually have.

Time famine is partially objective—modern life does make many demands—but it's also partially psychological. Studies show that how we *perceive* our time matters as much as how much we have. Research by Ashley Whillans at Harvard Business School has found that people who feel time-poor make worse decisions, experience more stress, and report lower well-being, even controlling for actual hours available. Her book *Time Smart* (2020) synthesizes decades of research on this phenomenon.

The cruel irony is that rushing often creates the very scarcity it's trying to solve. When we hurry through tasks, we make mistakes that require correction. When we multitask, we do everything worse. When we fill every moment with activity, we lose the spaciousness needed for creativity and insight.

We're running faster and faster to stay in the same place—and we're too exhausted to notice that maybe the running itself is the problem.

## ***Two Mornings***

Let me show you what this looks like in practice. Here are two versions of the same Tuesday morning:

**The Accelerated Morning:** The alarm jolts you awake. You grab your phone before your feet hit the floor—emails, news, notifications. You shower while mentally composing responses. Breakfast is coffee gulped standing up, maybe a protein bar eaten in the car. You arrive at work already depleted, having "accomplished" several things but experienced nothing. By noon, you can't remember what you ate. By evening, the morning is a blur. By next week, it might as well not have happened.

**The Intentional Morning:** The alarm sounds. You take three breaths before reaching for anything. You shower and feel the water. Breakfast is fifteen minutes at the table—eggs, toast, coffee—with no screens. You notice the taste, the warmth, the quiet. You arrive at work having done fewer things but having actually been present for them. That evening, you can recall the morning clearly. A year later, you still remember that particular breakfast, because you were there for it.

Same amount of time. Radically different experiences. The first morning is efficient; the second is *lived*. This is what's at stake when we talk about pace.

**The connection between problem and solution is direct:** Technology and cultural values have made speed our default. The practices in this chapter—transition rituals, single-tasking, slow meals—are deliberate interventions that restore our ability to choose our pace. They don't fight technology or reject ambition; they create space where presence becomes possible again.

## ***The Reframe***

*The Insight:* The goal isn't to be slow all the time. That's just another rigid prescription, another "should" to add to the pile.

The goal is to be intentional about your pace. To choose speed when it serves you and slowness when it doesn't. To recognize that some activities deserve haste and others deserve lingering, and to know the difference.

This is the Slow Movement's real message—not that slow is always better, but that we've lost the ability to choose. Speed has become our default, our unconscious setting. We hurry even when there's nothing to hurry toward. We rush through experiences that deserve savoring. We've forgotten that another gear exists.

## ***The Italian Lesson***

In 1986, McDonald's opened a restaurant near the Spanish Steps in Rome. For many Italians, this was an affront—not just to cuisine, but to a way of life.

One journalist, Carlo Petrini, responded by founding what he called the Slow Food movement. The idea was simple: food should be good, clean, and fair, and meals should be lingered over, not rushed through. A Big Mac eaten standing up is not a meal—it's fuel. A proper meal is an experience, a ritual, a form of connection.

From Slow Food grew a broader philosophy. Slow Cities, which rethink urban design around human pace rather than automotive speed. Slow Travel, which favors depth over breadth. Slow Parenting, which pushes back against the over-scheduling of childhood. Each variation shares a core insight: that speed often comes at the cost of quality, and that slowing down need not be a sacrifice—it can be an enrichment.

The Italians understand something we've forgotten. Their phrase *dolce far niente*—"the sweetness of doing nothing"—names an experience that has no positive equivalent in English. For us, doing nothing is wasted time. For them, it's a pleasure to be cultivated.

## ***Time Affluence***

Researchers have identified the opposite of time famine: "time affluence." It's the feeling of having enough time—enough margin, enough spaciousness, enough room to breathe.

Time affluence turns out to predict well-being more strongly than material affluence. A landmark study by researchers at the University of British Columbia, published in *Social Psychological and Personality Science* (2016), found that once basic needs are met, having more money contributes little to happiness—but having more time (or feeling like you do) contributes a lot. People who feel time-affluent are happier, healthier, more creative, and more productive.

The counterintuitive finding is that time affluence isn't just about having fewer obligations. It's about how we relate to time itself. People can feel time-affluent despite busy schedules if they feel in control of their time, if they build in transitions and margins, and if they're present for the time they have rather than always mentally elsewhere.

In other words, time affluence is cultivated, not just found. It's a skill and a practice, not just a circumstance. The practices that follow are designed to build exactly this skill.

## ***The Present Tense***

Here's what slowing down actually offers: the present moment.

When we rush, we're never fully where we are. We're at breakfast but thinking about the meeting. We're in the meeting but planning the afternoon. We're with our children but scrolling on our phones. We're physically present but mentally absent, always one step ahead of ourselves.

Slowing down is how we arrive in our own lives. It's how we actually experience what's happening rather than merely getting through it. The meal savored, the conversation unhurried, the walk with nowhere to be—these are the moments we remember. These are the moments that constitute a life.

The meditation traditions have known this for millennia. They call it presence, awareness, mindfulness. But you don't need to sit on a cushion to access it. You just need to occasionally stop rushing. To let yourself be where you are. To discover that this moment—right here, right now—is where your life is actually happening.

There's a crude but effective expression I once heard: "If you keep one foot in the future and one foot in the past, you piss all over today." I know people who can only see a better life somewhere in the future. Others cling to past accomplishments to maintain their sense of competence. I've been there myself.

But here's the thing: we have no choice in the matter. Time travel hasn't been invented yet; we have to live in the here and now. If you choose to dwell somewhere other than the present, that's your choice—but recognize that you're choosing to miss the only moment you actually have.

## ***The Practice***

*The Remedies:* Now that we understand the forces driving acceleration (technology and cultural values) and the costs they impose (time famine, missed experience), we can turn to practical solutions. These three practices directly counter the problem: they create margins, restore presence, and train the capacity to choose your pace.

## ***The Transition Ritual***

One of the reasons we feel so rushed is that we move from activity to activity without pause. Meeting to meeting, task to task, screen to screen—no margins, no transitions, no space to arrive or depart.

Try building micro-transitions into your day. Before entering a meeting, take three breaths and ask: What do I want to bring to this? After finishing a task, pause for sixty seconds before starting the next. When you arrive home, sit in the car for a moment before going inside.

These tiny pauses accomplish several things. They create a sense of spaciousness—the feeling that there's room to breathe. They help you arrive fully in each activity rather than carrying the residue of the last one. And they give your nervous system a chance to settle between stressors.

The transitions don't have to be long. Thirty seconds is enough. The point is to interrupt the relentless forward motion, to create small islands of stillness in the stream.

## ***The Single-Tasking Hour***

Multitasking is the enemy of presence. When we do multiple things at once, we do none of them fully. Research consistently shows that what we call "multitasking" is actually rapid task-switching, and it comes with significant cognitive costs—studies at Stanford found that heavy multitaskers performed worse on cognitive tasks even when they weren't multitasking.

Try designating one hour per day—or even one hour per week—as a single-tasking zone. During this time, you do one thing. Just one. No phone beside you, no tabs open, no half-attention. Whatever you're doing gets all of you.

It can be work—deep work is more productive than scattered work. It can be leisure—reading a book, taking a walk, having a conversation. It can be mundane—washing dishes, folding laundry, preparing a meal. The activity matters less than the quality of attention you bring to it.

What you'll likely discover is that single-tasking feels luxurious. The mind, freed from constant switching, settles into a kind of flow. Time seems to expand. The experience becomes richer. You're actually there for it.

## ***The Slow Meal***

Food is where the Slow Movement began, and it's still one of the best entry points into intentional slowness. Remember that breakfast you couldn't recall from three days ago? This practice is how you change that.

Once a week, practice a slow meal. Not just eating slowly—though that's part of it—but making the entire experience deliberate.

**Prepare with attention.** If you're cooking, don't rush through it. Notice the textures, the smells, the transformation of ingredients. If you're ordering in or eating out, choose with care rather than grabbing the fastest option.

**Set the scene.** Clear the table of clutter. Put away devices. Light a candle if you want. The environment signals to your nervous system that this is different, that you're about to do something worth attending to.

**Eat without distraction.** No screens, no reading, no working. Just the food and whoever you're sharing it with—even if that's just yourself.

**Linger.** Don't rush to clear the table. Sit with the satisfaction of having eaten. If you're with others, let the conversation meander. The Italians stay at the table for hours. You don't need hours, but give yourself more than you usually would.

A slow meal is practice for a slow life. It trains the capacity to be present, to savor, to let time expand rather than compress. And it reminds you, once a week, that this pace exists—that you can access it whenever you choose.

I'm fortunate to genuinely enjoy what I do—probably because it's what I choose to do. When I'm absorbed in work, time seems to disappear. But I've learned the hard way that if I'm cooking, I should just cook.

Here's an example: we have a whole process for making bacon. I get out a sheet pan, turn on the oven, lay down aluminum foil with the edges turned up to catch the rendered fat. Because our oven runs unevenly, I have to turn the pan halfway through. Six minutes per side. Six minutes is nothing when you're engaged in something else, so I'll wander back to my computer. And then I burn the bacon. Every time.

I've finally learned to set a timer on my way back to whatever I was working on. But what amazes me is how quickly those six minutes pass when I'm doing something else—and how interminable they feel when I'm just waiting. The perception of time is remarkably fluid. We have more control over it than we think. A watched pot may never boil, but at least you're there when it does.

## ***The Exhale***

Here's a thought experiment: Imagine you're ninety years old, looking back on your life. What will you remember?

Not the tasks you completed. Not the emails you answered. Not the efficient way you got through your to-do list. You'll remember the moments you were actually present for. The meals that lingered. The conversations that wandered. The afternoons when nothing happened and everything did.

The days are long but the years are short, people say. And it's true—but only if we're not there for the days. When we rush through each moment to get to the next one, the moments blur together into an undifferentiated stream. We look up and years have passed, and we can't account for them.

Slowing down is how we make time expand. Not by adding hours—we all have the same twenty-four—but by being present for the hours we have. A single hour lived fully is worth more than a day lived in distraction.

This isn't about productivity, though presence often makes us more productive. It isn't about efficiency, though being fully engaged often makes us more effective. It's about something more fundamental: actually experiencing the life you have.

You don't get this time back. The breakfast you rushed through is gone. The conversation you half-attended is over. The walk you took while mentally elsewhere has passed. These moments aren't stored somewhere, waiting for you to eventually pay attention. They're simply gone.

But the next moment is coming. And the one after that. And you get to choose how you meet them.

You can rush. You can hurry through your life, checking boxes, getting things done, always moving toward some future that never quite arrives. That's one way to live.

Or you can slow down. Not always, not in everything, but sometimes, intentionally. You can build margins and transitions. You can single-task and linger. You can discover that the present moment—this one, right here—is where your life actually is.

The period in "b." means a pause. A full stop. A moment to arrive before moving on.

Just be. Here. Now. At whatever speed allows you to actually be present.

That's slow enough.

A great deal of stress is self-induced. The expectations I place on myself are generally far greater than anyone else's expectations of me. Part of slowing down is giving myself permission not to meet every one of those standards. What are the true consequences of a short delay? Usually far less severe than the anxiety I create by rushing. The deadline I'm racing toward is often one I set myself—and I can reset it just as easily.

**The chapter in brief:** Speed has become our default because technology accelerated expectations and culture turned busyness into status. This costs us presence—we pass through our lives without experiencing them. The remedy is intentional pace: transition rituals create margins, single-tasking restores attention, and slow meals train the capacity to savor. Time affluence comes not from having fewer obligations, but from being present for the time you have.

**Your micro-commitment:** Tomorrow morning, before you check your phone, before you plan your day, before you rush into anything—eat breakfast. Just breakfast. Fifteen minutes, no screens, actually tasting the food. Notice whether you remember it that evening. Notice whether it changes how the day feels.

Remember that breakfast you couldn't recall from three days ago? You now have everything you need to make tomorrow's breakfast different. Not because the food will be better, but because you'll actually be there for it. That's the whole lesson of this chapter in a single meal.
