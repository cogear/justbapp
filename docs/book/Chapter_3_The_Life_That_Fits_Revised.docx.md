# **Chapter Three**

# **The Life That Fits**

## ***Quality Over Status***

*"Beware the barrenness of a busy life."*

*— Socrates*

## ***The Invitation***

Think about the last time you posted something on social media. Maybe it was a photo from a trip, a milestone, a meal at a nice restaurant. Now ask yourself: Did you share it because the moment meant something to you? Or because you wanted others to see a certain version of your life? This small question—*for me or for them?*—is actually the question of this chapter. It's the difference between quality and status, between a life that fits and a life that performs.

There's no judgment here. We all do it. We choose the flattering angle, the impressive backdrop, the story that makes us look good. We curate.

But here's what I've started to notice: the things we share often aren't the things that actually make us happy. The fancy dinner gets posted; the quiet evening at home with someone you love doesn't. The vacation photos go up; the lazy Sunday morning that restored your soul stays private. We broadcast status and keep quality to ourselves.

**This chapter's premise is simple: Choose what makes your life fit, not what makes it look impressive. A good Tuesday is worth more than a good title.**

This happens in bigger choices too. We take the job that sounds impressive instead of the one that fits our life. We buy the thing that will turn heads instead of the thing that will serve us well. We say yes to invitations that will look good on our calendar instead of protecting time for what actually matters.

Over and over, we trade quality for status. Substance for appearance. How our lives feel for how they look. We get on the status treadmill and run faster and faster—but the finish line keeps moving. The only way to win is to step off.

This chapter is an invitation to step off. To ask, before every decision big or small: Will this make my life better, or just make it look better?

**A personal example:** If you've read this far, you know something about my troubles in my early years. I went back to school and got my degree in my forties. After graduation, I moved to the big city and took a second job—retail work, nothing glamorous. At the time, I looked at it as a failure. Here I was with a fresh degree, stocking shelves and working a register. But I really enjoyed it. The work was easy, and I met many interesting people—characters I never would have encountered in a corporate office. That job provided the inspiration for my first book and still teaches me lessons to this day. What looked like a step down was actually a life that fit. I just couldn't see it at first because I was measuring with the wrong yardstick—status instead of quality.

## ***The Unlearning***

We've been taught to measure our lives by all the wrong things.

From the time we're kids, the questions come: What do you want to be when you grow up? Where do you go to school? What do you do? These all really mean the same thing: *How important are you?* Life gets framed as a ladder, and success means climbing as high as you can.

But nobody asks: How do you spend your Tuesday afternoons? Do you have time to read? Do you laugh much? Are you actually there for the people you love? These questions don't fit on a resume. They won't impress anyone at a cocktail party. But they might actually measure a life worth living.

*"Nobody asks: Do you laugh much? Are you actually there for the people you love?"*

## ***The Treadmill That Never Stops***

Here's the thing about status: it exists entirely in other people's heads. You can't actually experience status—you can only experience your guess about what others think of you.

And yet we sacrifice so much real, lived life for it. We take jobs we hate because of the title. We buy things we can't afford because of the image. We stay in cities we don't love because that's where ambitious people are supposed to live. We get on the treadmill and run.

The problem is that the status treadmill never stops. There's always someone with a better title, a bigger house, a shinier bio. Every level you reach just reveals another level above it. Even when you win, the prize is just... more running. Not peace. Not satisfaction. Just another rung to climb, another mile to run.

Psychologists have a name for this: the *hedonic treadmill*. It's our tendency to adapt to whatever we achieve—and then want more. Researchers studied lottery winners and found something surprising: after about a year, most were no happier than before they won. The thrill wore off. The new normal became... normal. **This matters for our premise because it proves that status achievements don't deliver lasting satisfaction.** The treadmill keeps moving. The only way off is to stop chasing status and start choosing quality.

The same thing happens with promotions, fancy purchases, and status achievements. Each one gives you a little high—and then your brain adjusts. To feel that good again, you need an even bigger win. This is why chasing status is so exhausting. You're not actually getting anywhere. You're running in place.

## ***Two Ways to Mattero***

Here's something interesting from the research on how humans seek importance. There are actually two very different paths to social standing.

The first is *dominance*—getting rank through force, intimidation, or just grabbing what you can. Think chest-puffing, territory-marking, making people afraid of you. This kind of status is exhausting to maintain. You always have to watch your back, and people follow you because they have to, not because they want to. Dominance is the fast lane on the status treadmill.

The second is *prestige*—earning respect because you're genuinely good at something, genuinely helpful, genuinely wise. People follow you because they admire you, not because they fear you. This kind of standing is stable. It doesn't require constant defending. Prestige is what happens when you step off the treadmill and focus on getting genuinely good at what matters.

Most status-chasing is really dominance-chasing—collecting symbols of power rather than developing real skill. The "b." philosophy points toward prestige: becoming genuinely good at what matters, and letting any recognition be a bonus rather than the goal.

**How this plays out in practice:** I've worked both in the corporate world and as an independent contractor. Over the years, I've had the opportunity to teach computer applications to thousands of people. Most people are intimidated by computers, so I don't judge them when they struggle. What I've come to understand is that most problems arise from our misunderstanding of how things work. I approach most tasks with a preconceived notion of how I think they're supposed to work. When they don't, I get frustrated. But what I really need is an open mind and the wisdom to accept that I don't know everything—and to read the instructions. That's prestige thinking over dominance thinking: being willing to learn rather than needing to already know. It's choosing competence (quality) over the appearance of competence (status).

## ***The Reframe***

What if we measured our lives differently? What if we stepped off the status treadmill and asked: does this actually make my life better?

Decades of psychology research have identified three things humans genuinely need to thrive—not status symbols, but actual psychological nutrients, as essential to our well-being as food and water are to our bodies. Understanding these three needs shows us exactly why quality beats status.

The first is *autonomy*—the feeling that you're the author of your own life, making your own choices. **In practice:** When you take a job because it sounds impressive rather than because it fits your life, you sacrifice autonomy. You become a puppet of public opinion. But when you choose the role with more flexibility and less prestige—because it lets you pick up your kids or pursue a side project—you exercise real autonomy. You author your own life.

The second is *competence*—the feeling that you're good at things, that you're capable and effective. **In practice:** When you chase status, your sense of competence becomes fragile—always dependent on how you compare to others. But when you spend your leisure time getting genuinely good at something you love—woodworking, cooking, gardening, music—you build real competence. The skill is yours regardless of what anyone thinks.

The third is *connection*—the feeling that you belong, that you matter to people who matter to you. **In practice:** When you invest in networking events and LinkedIn connections, relationships become transactions—people become opportunities. But when you invest deeply in five real friends instead of five thousand followers, you experience genuine belonging. You have people who know you, not just people who've seen your posts.

Here's the key insight: **Chasing status undermines all three needs. Choosing quality feeds all three.** When you pick what genuinely serves you, you exercise real autonomy. When you focus on getting better rather than looking better, you build real competence. When you invest in deep connections rather than wide networks, you experience real belonging.

## ***The Real Measures***

Quality of life isn't one thing. It's the sum of many things: your mornings, your commute, your work hours, your evenings, your weekends, your relationships, your health, your energy, your sense that your days mean something.

Think about each one:

**Time:** How much of your time do you actually control? How much gets eaten by things that drain you?

**Energy:** At the end of a regular day, do you have anything left? What about at the end of a week?

**Relationships:** Do you have time and attention for the people who matter most? Or do they only get your leftovers?

**Health:** Does your life support your body or wear it down? Do you sleep enough? Move enough?

**Meaning:** Does your work matter to you? Do your days feel purposeful or just busy?

**Joy:** How often do you laugh? Feel delight? Have moments of real pleasure?

None of this fits on a business card. Nobody will be impressed when you tell them about it. But these things might actually measure whether your life is good—whether it *fits*.

## ***The Three A.M. Test***

Here's something fascinating: our brains can only really keep track of about 150 people. Beyond that, we just can't hold all the information needed for real relationships. Anthropologist Robin Dunbar figured this out by studying how human communities work. **This research supports our premise directly:** your brain literally cannot maintain quality relationships with thousands of people. Quantity and quality are trade-offs. Choose quality.

Social media pushes us way past this limit. We collect thousands of "friends" and "followers." But research keeps showing that these weak connections can't replace a handful of strong ones. Having five people who really know you is worth infinitely more than five thousand people who've seen your posts.

Here's a simple test: Who could you call at three in the morning if something went wrong? Who would actually pick up? Who would come? That's your real network. Everything else is just the treadmill—running to accumulate connections that don't actually sustain you.

But it's not just about who you can call. It works the other way too. Will *you* answer the three a.m. call from a friend? Will you meet with them—in person or on the phone—when they need you? I know this: if you do, you'll create a bond that will last a long time. Quality relationships aren't just about receiving support. They're about being the person others can count on. That's connection—one of the three needs that quality feeds and status starves.

## ***The Practice***

Choosing quality over status is a practice—a habit of asking different questions before you decide things. Here are three approaches, each with a concrete action you can take this week.

## ***What You Can and Can't Control***

The ancient Stoic philosophers had a useful way of looking at things. They divided the world into what you can control and what you can't.

Status? You can't control it. It lives in other people's heads. You can influence it, but you can't make people think anything. Basing your happiness on status is basing it on something outside your control—which is, as those old philosophers pointed out, a recipe for misery. It's running on a treadmill someone else controls.

Quality of life? That's mostly in your hands. How you spend your time. How you treat the people you love. Whether you do work that means something to you. Whether you get good at things that bring you joy. These are choices you can make no matter what anyone else thinks.

When you're facing a decision, ask: *Am I choosing this because it will make my actual life better, or because it will make people think better of my life?* The first is worth chasing. The second is chasing shadows.

**This week's practice:** Identify one commitment you've made for status reasons—something you said yes to because it would look good, not because it fits your life. Consider whether you can gracefully exit or decline to renew it.

## ***The Vital Few***

There's a writer named Greg McKeown who talks about "the disciplined pursuit of less." His idea is simple: figure out the few things that really matter, and have the courage to say no to everything else.

We often confuse being busy with being productive. Chasing status leads to what he calls "the undisciplined pursuit of more"—more meetings, more commitments, more networking events, more everything. We spread ourselves so thin that we can't do anything well. We're occupied all the time but never fulfilled. We're running on the treadmill at maximum speed and going nowhere.

Quality requires trade-offs. You can't be everything to everyone (status) and also be excellent at the things that matter most (quality). Not at the same time. The practice is learning to choose.

Before saying yes to anything, ask yourself: Is this essential? Does this serve what I care about most? Or am I saying yes because I'm afraid of missing out, because it looks good, because I can't stand to disappoint someone?

**This week's practice:** Write down the three things that matter most to you—not the three things that would impress others, but the three that actually make your life feel good. Put them somewhere you'll see them before your next decision.

## ***The Joy of Missing Out***

You've heard of FOMO—the Fear of Missing Out. It's that anxiety that makes us check social media constantly, say yes to every invitation, try to keep up with every trend. FOMO is status-seeking in its most anxious form. It's the voice that keeps you on the treadmill.

The antidote is JOMO—the Joy of Missing Out. It's the contentment that comes from knowing you're exactly where you want to be, doing exactly what you want to do, even if it means missing something others find exciting.

JOMO is what the quality life feels like. It's being home with a book while the party happens without you—and feeling genuinely good about it. It's watching others climb ladders you've decided not to climb—and feeling relief instead of envy. It's stepping off the status treadmill and discovering that the world goes on without you, and that this is perfectly fine.

Missing out, it turns out, is often the price of showing up fully for your own life.

**This week's practice:** Deliberately miss one thing you'd normally attend out of obligation or FOMO. Use that time for something that genuinely restores you. Notice how it feels.

I spend a lot of time watching online videos. The algorithm knows exactly how to select videos I'll click on. Many times I end up watching something because I genuinely want to learn what they claim to teach. But lately, I've found myself asking: *Is this really getting me to my goal?* Or is it just another form of FOMO—fear that I'll miss some crucial piece of information?

Here's a humbling admission: I've been involved with computers my whole life, and I have a terrible track record at predicting what will and won't work. When I first heard that Google had spent $1.65 billion to buy YouTube, I thought it was a stupid investment. At the time, the quality of videos wasn't what it is today. That purchase is now worth about 300 times what Google paid. I also thought Amazon was a ridiculous name for an online bookstore.

The point isn't that I'm bad at predictions—it's that none of us really know what will matter. Which means chasing the "next big thing" for status is a fool's errand. Better to focus on what matters to you *now*, in your actual life, than to chase what might impress people later. Step off the prediction treadmill. Choose quality today.

## ***The Exhale***

There's a freedom in stepping off the status treadmill.

Patrick Pichette was Google's CFO—one of the most prestigious jobs in the corporate world. When he retired, he wrote about the tension between his impressive role and his actual quality of life. Despite being at the very top, he couldn't find balance. So he walked away. Not to a bigger title—to more time. He stepped off the treadmill.

Joshua Fields Millburn left a six-figure corporate job at thirty. He describes his old life as the American Dream on paper—but a nightmare in reality, full of debt, depression, and disconnection. His shift to a simpler life was a direct trade: less income and status for more time and quality. He stopped running.

These are dramatic examples. Not everyone can quit their big job. But the principle works at any scale. You can choose quality over status in small ways every day: taking the job with less prestige but more flexibility, living in the less fancy neighborhood that's closer to friends, skipping the networking event that would drain you.

When you stop optimizing for how things look and start optimizing for how things feel, something shifts. You notice what actually makes you happy instead of what's supposed to. You choose things that fit instead of things that impress. You build a life that works for you instead of a life that performs for others.

Some people won't get it. They're still playing the game, still keeping score, still measuring success by external yardsticks. That's their choice. It doesn't have to be yours.

Because here's what I've come to believe: the life that fits is better than the life that impresses. A good Tuesday is worth more than a good title. How you actually experience your days matters more than how your resume reads.

Build a life you actually want to live. Not one that looks good in a highlight reel. Not one that wins approval from people who aren't really paying attention. Not one that checks every box except the ones that matter.

A life that fits. A life of quality. A life that's yours.

I'll be honest: "just being" isn't something I'll ever completely achieve. The best I can do is keep trying to improve, while accepting that I'll do this at my own pace, with small steps. That's the quality approach—not perfection, but presence. Not arriving, but walking in the right direction.

**Three ways to start stepping off the treadmill today:**

1\. **Identify one status commitment** — something you said yes to for appearances — and consider letting it go.

2\. **Write down your three vital things** — the things that actually make your life good, not impressive — and keep them visible.

3\. **Practice JOMO once** — deliberately miss something you'd attend out of obligation, and use that time for what genuinely restores you.

Remember: **Choose what makes your life fit, not what makes it look impressive. A good Tuesday is worth more than a good title.**
