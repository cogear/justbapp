## ***Why We Need a New Way to Be***

## ***The Age of Anxiety***

Something is happening to us.

You feel it, don't you? That low hum of unease that follows you through your days. The sense that the ground beneath your feet isn't quite solid anymore. The way your chest tightens when you read the news, scroll through your phone, or think too hard about where the world is heading.

You're not imagining it. And you're not alone.

We are living through a moment of unprecedented change—and unprecedented anxiety. The technologies that promised to make our lives easier have made them more complicated. The connections that were supposed to bring us together have often left us more isolated. The future that once seemed like a distant horizon now arrives daily, demanding that we adapt, adjust, and accept disruptions we never anticipated.

Consider what has happened in just the last few years. Artificial intelligence has evolved from a science fiction concept to a daily reality—writing our emails, generating our images, threatening our jobs, raising questions about what it even means to be human. A global pandemic reshaped how we work, connect, and think about safety. Political divisions have deepened into chasms. Climate anxiety has become a clinical category. The twenty-four-hour news cycle delivers an endless stream of crises, each one competing for our already depleted attention.

And underneath it all, there's a quieter crisis—one that doesn't make headlines but shows up in therapists' offices and sleepless nights and the vague sense that something essential has gone missing. We're exhausted. We're overwhelmed. We're running as fast as we can and somehow falling further behind.

*"The world is changing faster than our nervous systems were designed to handle."*

This is the age of anxiety. Not the dramatic anxiety of imminent catastrophe, but the chronic, grinding anxiety of perpetual uncertainty. Of not knowing what's coming next. Of feeling like the rules keep changing and no one sent us the memo.

## ***The Machine Age***

Let's talk about the elephant in the room: artificial intelligence.

In what feels like an instant, AI has moved from research labs to our daily lives. It writes articles and code. It creates images indistinguishable from photographs. It passes bar exams and medical boards. It holds conversations so natural that we forget we're talking to a machine.

For many people, this evokes a profound unease—and understandably so. The questions are genuinely unsettling: Will my job exist in five years? What does creativity mean if machines can create? What does expertise mean if AI can outperform experts? What does *human* mean when the things we thought made us special can be replicated by algorithms?

These aren't abstract philosophical puzzles. They're practical anxieties that keep real people awake at night. Parents wonder what to tell their children about careers. Workers wonder whether to retrain or hold on. Artists wonder if their life's work will be devalued by generators that can produce infinite content in seconds.

And beyond the economic questions, there's something deeper. A sense that we're being displaced from the center of our own story. That the machines we created are somehow outgrowing us. That we're becoming obsolete in a world we built.

This book doesn't pretend these concerns aren't real. They are. The disruption is genuine. The uncertainty is warranted. Anyone who tells you they know exactly how this will unfold is either lying or deluded.

But this book does offer something: a way to be human in the machine age. A set of principles and practices that become more important, not less, as technology advances. A path to peace that doesn't depend on predicting or controlling what's coming—because we can't.

## ***What Machines Can't Do***

Here's what I've come to believe: the more powerful our machines become, the more valuable our humanity becomes.

Think about it. As AI gets better at generating content, authentic human expression becomes more precious. As algorithms optimize everything, the deliberately unoptimized—the slow, the handmade, the inefficient—becomes more meaningful. As digital connections proliferate, real human presence becomes rarer and therefore more valuable.

The things this book is about—acceptance, comfort, quality, slowness, balance, community, gratitude—these are irreducibly human. Machines can simulate them, but they can't experience them. And experiencing them is precisely the point.

A machine can generate a meal plan, but it can't savor a meal. It can optimize your schedule, but it can't feel the peace of an unhurried afternoon. It can analyze your social network, but it can't know the warmth of genuine belonging. It can produce gratitude journals, but it can't feel grateful.

These experiences—the felt sense of being alive, of being present, of being connected—are not optimizable. They're not problems to be solved. They're the point of being here at all.

In a world increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, the most radical act might be to cultivate natural presence. To slow down when everything speeds up. To be satisfied when everything demands more. To find meaning in the ordinary when the extraordinary is manufactured at scale.

This isn't anti-technology. It's pro-human. It's recognizing that technology is a tool, not a destination. That efficiency is a means, not an end. That the question isn't whether machines can do what we do—it's whether we can do what machines can't.

And what machines can't do is *be*. They can process. They can generate. They can optimize. But they can't simply be—present, aware, alive. That's ours. That's what no algorithm can automate.

I came to understand this through my own unexpected journey. A few years ago, I found myself suddenly retired. I had always been interested in computers and had worked hard to get where I was. Now I found myself struggling with a question I hadn't anticipated: *Who am I now that I'm no longer a programmer?* I felt lost. It seemed my whole life had been defined by my job. But now?

Maybe it was time to write that book I'd always said I was going to write. So I did. As part of the process, I joined a local writers group. One of the hot topics was AI. Everyone was afraid of what it would mean for the book business.

Because I was now an ex-programmer, I had consciously ignored this new development that my old colleagues had been telling me about. But the group discussion made me curious. So I logged into the popular chatbot of the time, pasted a paragraph from my work in progress, and asked it to rewrite the passage to be more exciting.

It did. And it did it wonderfully.

I had been involved with computers for decades. That whole time, naysayers would remind me that someday computers would program themselves. So far, they hadn't come close, and I believed it might never happen. But now, not only did this machine rewrite my paragraph—it did so with a sophistication I hadn't thought possible. I sat back in awe. My imagination was on fire. I knew then that this was the biggest technological advancement of my lifetime.

Suddenly, I was a programmer again.

But even as I dove back into the technical world, something else was crystallizing. The very power of these tools threw into sharp relief what they couldn't do. They could generate text, but they couldn't know what it meant to struggle with a blank page. They could optimize processes, but they couldn't feel the satisfaction of a problem solved. They could simulate conversation, but they couldn't experience connection. The more capable the machines became, the more clearly I could see what remained irreducibly human.

## ***The Case for Being***

So what do we do? How do we live in a world that seems to be spinning faster every day?

One response is to spin faster with it. To hustle harder. To upgrade constantly. To optimize every hour and monetize every skill and stay one step ahead of the disruption. This is the dominant advice: adapt or die. Learn the new tools. Acquire the new skills. Keep up or get left behind.

There's some wisdom in that. We do need to adapt. We can't pretend the world isn't changing or wish ourselves back to a simpler time. The future is coming whether we're ready or not.

But there's another response—not instead of adapting, but alongside it. A response that doesn't try to out-spin the spinning, but finds stillness at the center of the storm. A response rooted not in doing more, but in being more fully.

This is the "b." philosophy.

The period after the "b" is intentional. It's a full stop. A pause. A declaration that there's value in completion, in enough-ness, in arriving rather than endlessly chasing. It's a gentle rebellion against the tyranny of more—more productivity, more optimization, more striving, more anxiety.

"Just be" sounds simple. And in some ways it is. But simplicity shouldn't be confused with ease. In a culture that rewards constant doing, choosing to just be is a radical act. It requires unlearning deep conditioning. It requires resisting powerful pressures. It requires courage.

This book is a guide for that journey.

## ***What This Book Offers***

This is not a self-improvement book in the traditional sense. I'm not going to tell you how to be more productive or successful or impressive. There are plenty of those books, and if that's what you're looking for, this isn't it.

This is a book about a different kind of flourishing—one that doesn't depend on external achievements or others' opinions or staying ahead of the curve. One that's available to you now, in this moment, regardless of what's happening in the world around you.

In the chapters that follow, we'll explore seven principles for living well in anxious times:

**Acceptance, Not Settling** — Learning to accept yourself as you are, without confusing acceptance with resignation or giving up on growth.

**Comfort as Achievement** — Recognizing that creating conditions of ease and well-being is valuable in itself, not just a reward for productivity.

**Quality Over Status** — Choosing things that genuinely serve you over things that impress others.

**Slow Down Intentionally** — Reclaiming your relationship with time and discovering what's available only at a slower pace.

**Balance Over Burnout** — Managing your energy sustainably so you can keep going without running yourself into the ground.

**Community, Not Competition** — Building genuine connection in a culture that often treats others as rivals.

**Gratitude and Small Joys** — Cultivating the capacity to notice and appreciate what's already here.

Each chapter follows the same structure. We'll start with an invitation into the topic. Then we'll examine what needs to be unlearned—the cultural messages and personal habits that get in the way. Next, we'll reframe the issue, offering a new way to think about it. Then we'll explore practical ways to put the principle into action. Finally, we'll close with an exhale—a moment of rest and permission.

Throughout, I'll share my own stories—moments when these principles became real for me. But I've also left space for your stories. Because this isn't really a book about me. It's a book about you—about how you might find your own way to peace in a chaotic world.

## ***How to Read This Book***

You could read this book straight through, cover to cover. The chapters build on each other, and there's a logic to the sequence.

But you could also read it differently. You could start with the chapter that speaks to your current struggle. If you're exhausted, maybe start with Balance Over Burnout. If you're lonely, try Community, Not Competition. If you're chasing something that never arrives, Acceptance or Gratitude might be your entry point.

You could keep it by your bed and read a section each night. You could work through it with a friend or a group, discussing the questions and sharing your stories. You could return to it in different seasons, finding different chapters relevant as your life changes.

What I'd encourage you not to do is rush through it. This isn't a book to be conquered or checked off. It's a book to be savored, to be lived with, to be returned to. The irony of speeding through a book about slowing down wouldn't be lost on me—or, I hope, on you.

At the end of each chapter, you'll find a simple closing—a few lines of permission. These are reminders that the things this book invites you toward are allowed. In a world that tells you to do more, be more, achieve more, sometimes you just need someone to say: you're allowed to be enough. You're allowed to rest. You're allowed to just be.

Consider those closings as small gifts. Hold them loosely. Return to them when you need them.

The years that followed my AI awakening consisted of frantic work—learning, experimenting, trying to capitalize on what felt like a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. I even went back to my last job for a while. This was my element; I was happy again. And even though things occasionally got out of control, I was able to cope, mostly successfully, drawing on skills I had developed over decades of practice.

But I could also see that many people were struggling. The same technologies that energized me were terrifying others. I found myself wishing I could pass on some of the lessons I had learned—not about the technology itself, but about how to stay grounded while the ground shifts beneath you.

Then came a vision. It was simple: a small letter "b" followed by a period. I chuckled to myself as the thought came to me: *just be*. It was one of those lightbulb moments you hear about. I knew immediately that I was onto something—a symbol to coalesce around, a philosophy simple enough to remember and deep enough to live by.

So here we are.

**b.**

*Just be.*

## ***An Invitation***

The world is spinning faster. That's not going to change. The technologies will keep advancing. The disruptions will keep coming. The news will keep arriving at all hours with new reasons to worry.

You can't control that. Neither can I.

But you can control how you meet it. You can choose to find stillness in the midst of motion. You can choose to be present even when the future is uncertain. You can choose to value the irreducibly human things that no machine can replicate—connection, presence, peace, love.

You can choose to just be.

That's not a retreat from the world. It's a way of being in the world—fully, presently, humanly. It's not about checking out; it's about checking in. Not about giving up on the future, but about not sacrificing the present to anxiety about the future.

I don't know what's coming. No one does. But I know that whatever comes, we'll face it better if we're rested than if we're burned out. We'll face it better connected than isolated. We'll face it better grateful than resentful. We'll face it better whole than fragmented.

This book is my offering—a set of principles and practices that have helped me find peace in anxious times. I share them not as prescriptions but as possibilities. Not as *the* answer but as *an* answer. Take what resonates. Leave what doesn't. Make it your own.

The world is spinning faster. Let's find stillness together.

Let's just be.

*You are allowed to seek peace in uncertain times.*
