# The chipped cup you love anyway

*A Japanese room knows that wear isn't damage — it's closeness made visible.*

## The one you reach for

There's a cup in your kitchen with a chip in the rim. A small one, near the handle. You know exactly where it is. You reach for it without looking.

You have better cups. Cups without chips. Maybe a matching set someone gave you, still boxed in a cupboard you barely open.

And still, most mornings, you pick the broken one. Nobody taught you that. It just happened, slowly, over a few hundred ordinary days.

## Saved for someone else

We're told the flawed thing should be replaced. A chip means the cup has failed at being a cup. Somewhere there's a whole one, and buying it would settle the matter.

So most of us keep two grades of belongings. The good plates for company. The everyday plates for us. As if our own Tuesdays deserved the seconds.

And the good ones wait in the dark, unchipped and unused. Perfect. A little cold. Loved in theory only.

There's a strange loneliness in a house where all the best things are being saved for somebody who hasn't arrived yet.

## What the Japanese room knows

There's an old understanding in Japan — wabi-sabi — that finds beauty in things that are worn, irregular, and marked by use. A tea bowl with a crackled glaze. A wooden tray smoothed pale by decades of hands. A paper screen patched where the cat got through.

This isn't about tolerating shabbiness. It's a different idea of what makes something worth keeping. The Japanese room doesn't ask the cup to stay new. It asks the cup to stay close.

Perfection, in this view, isn't a finish line. It's not even the point. The point is the relationship — between you and the object, between the object and the ordinary days it has survived alongside you. A cup that has never been used has had no life. A cup with a chip has had several.

## A record, not a ruin

But the chip isn't damage. It's a record. The morning you were half awake. The sink that was too full. The day you set it down hard because you were late.

You don't love the cup because it's imperfect. That's too clever. You love it because you used it — and using anything long enough leaves marks on it.

Wear is what closeness looks like from the outside. The worn stair. The soft spot on the couch. The handle of a door your hand has already learned by heart.

The mark is where the love shows. It isn't a flaw in the thing. It's the shape of all the mornings you've spent together.

## Keep using it

So here's the practice, and it's small. Keep using it. Don't retire it to a shelf where it becomes precious. It only became yours by being handled.

And notice what you reach for. Not what you'd photograph — what your hand goes to when nobody's watching. That's a surprisingly short list, and it's the true one.

Then let the good ones out of the cupboard. Drink from the nice cup on a Wednesday, for no reason. Let it earn a chip of its own.

There's nothing to buy here. You already own the things. You just have to stop protecting them from your own life.

## Keeping it close

A comfortable home isn't full of new things. It's full of used ones. Faded, softened, slightly wrong in ways only you would notice, and completely yours.

Nothing you love stays perfect. That was never on offer. The only real choice is between keeping something beautiful and keeping it close.

Keep it close. Pour the coffee. Hold the warm side. The chip sits under your thumb, where it always has.
