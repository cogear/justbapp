# An evening without a screen

*A room with nothing on turns out to have more in it than you expected.*

## The grey gap

Think back to last night. Not what you did — what you remember. For a lot of us there's a soft grey gap where three or four hours used to be.

The evening wasn't bad. Nothing went wrong. It just didn't leave anything behind. No image, no story, no particular feeling. It passed like weather you slept through.

We didn't spend it badly. We spent it somewhere else, and came back with nothing in our pockets.

This is the strange thing about the modern evening. It's not that we're doing something wrong. It's that we're doing something invisible. Hours go somewhere, and we can't quite say where. The night was full, but it wasn't full of anything that stayed.

## Very good company

A screen is very good company. It never runs out, never gets tired, never asks anything of you. It fills a room so completely that you forget rooms have their own weather. The light it gives off is steady and flat. The sound it makes never varies in temperature. It doesn't take the evening from you. You hand it over, a few minutes at a time, and then it's gone.

This isn't a lecture. Screens aren't the enemy, and going without one doesn't make you better than anyone. It's simpler than that. They're so easy that nothing else gets a turn.

And the things that make an evening feel warm and long — hands, voices, the sound of your own house — those need a turn. They need time and silence to surface. A screen doesn't drown them out exactly. It just fills the space they would have occupied, the way a loud conversation at the next table fills a café. You don't notice what you can't hear.

The Danish word for the warm feeling of a winter evening is *hygge*, and part of what makes it work is that it requires a kind of stillness. Not emptiness. Stillness — the kind where the room itself becomes part of the experience. You can't get there if something is always talking.

## The first twenty minutes

So try it once. One evening, put the screen in another room. Not off, not face-down on the table. In a different room, with a door between you. Distance matters. Willpower doesn't. If the screen is on the table, it's the most interesting thing in the room, and you'll keep glancing at it. If it's in another room, it becomes someone else's problem.

The first twenty minutes are strange. There's a small itch, like a word you can't remember. Your hands will go looking for something to hold. Let them look. That itch isn't a problem to solve. It's just the quiet arriving. It's the room warming up.

Then something odd happens. The evening gets long again. Not slow — long. There turns out to be much more of it than you thought. Time, which had been compressed into scrolling segments, opens back up. A single hour becomes a spacious thing. You notice this most in the small tasks: making tea, folding a towel, looking out the window. Each one takes exactly as long as it takes, and somehow that's longer and richer than you remember.

You'll start hearing the house. The fridge. A neighbour's door. The wind checking the windows. Ordinary sounds that were always there, underneath the noise.

## Leave the gap

Don't make a plan. That's the usual mistake — trading one filled evening for another one, just as busy. You take the screen away and replace it with a project, a list, a structured activity. Leave the gap. See what walks into it. An evening doesn't have to be filled to be good.

Usually it's your hands that find something first. A sock to mend. A deck of cards. A drawer that needs sorting. Dough that needs kneading. Work that goes nowhere in particular and takes exactly as long as it takes. Nothing important. Nothing you'd write down. But your hands know what they want to do when they're not holding a screen, and they'll find it if you give them a chance.

And people talk differently in a room with nothing on. Slower. Sideways. They wander off the point and come back. They finish their thoughts. There's nowhere to look that's more interesting than the other person, so you look at them. Conversation finds its own depth when there's nothing competing for attention.

You'll probably go to bed earlier than you meant to, tired in a way that feels earned.

## One evening the screen doesn't get

You don't need to do this every night. Once a week is plenty. This isn't a rule to fail at, and nobody's keeping score.

But keep one evening the screen doesn't get. Not as discipline. As a room in your week that nobody else is filling for you. A space that belongs to the house and the hands and the slow conversation and the ordinary sounds.

Try it once, this week. Pick a night with nothing in it. Put the thing in another room, and let the evening be as long as it turns out to be.

You're allowed to have an evening you'll remember tomorrow.
