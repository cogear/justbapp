# Your house rules: a personal comfort doctrine

*A short list of what's already true in your home — written down so you can stop rediscovering it.*

## The rules nobody wrote

Every home that feels good has rules. Nobody wrote them down. Nobody voted on them. But they're there, and everyone who lives there knows them.

The lamps go on at four. Shoes come off at the door. Nobody eats standing at the counter. Sunday morning is slow, and we don't answer the phone.

You already have a few of these. Habits that hardened into law. You've just never read them back to yourself, so you can't tell which ones are serving you and which ones you inherited.

This is the thing about unwritten rules: they run whether they work or not. The lamp at four — that one earns its keep. But maybe the rule about keeping the kitchen spotless before you sit down is just your mother's anxiety, still living in your hands. You never questioned it because you never said it out loud.

## Someone else's house

Most of us are also running rules we never chose. Ones we picked up from a parent's house. From an old roommate. From a room in a photograph that looked like the kind of life we wanted.

Some of those fit. Keep them. But some have been quietly making your evenings worse for years, and you never noticed, because you never had to name them.

Nothing in a house is more stubborn than a habit no one has questioned. The way you arrange the living room. The hour you start thinking about dinner. Whether the television is on by default. These feel like personality. They're often just residue — a setup that worked for someone else, in a different house, in a different decade, that you absorbed without trying.

Naming them is the first step. Not to judge, just to see. Oh, that's a rule. Whose was it? Does it still work here?

## Observations, not aspirations

A personal comfort doctrine sounds like a big thing. It isn't. It's a short list of what's actually true in your house — the conditions that reliably make an evening feel like yours.

And you don't invent it. You notice it.

Think of one evening this week that felt good. Not impressive. Not productive. Just good. Now work backwards. What was on? What was off? What did you not do?

Maybe the kitchen light was off and the small lamp was on. Maybe you sat down to eat. Maybe the phone was in the other room. Maybe nobody turned on the overhead light, and the room felt like a room instead of a workspace.

That's your material. The rules aren't aspirations — they're not about who you wish you were. They're observations, written down so you stop having to rediscover them every night from scratch.

A good rule makes the evening easier, not busier. If it feels like a task, it isn't a comfort rule. It's just another thing on the list.

## Writing them down

Keep them small and plain.

The big light stays off after dinner. We eat sitting down. The phone charges in the kitchen overnight.

Specific enough that you can follow them when you're tired — which is exactly when you'll need them. Vague rules dissolve the moment life gets heavy. "Be more relaxed" is not a rule. "Lamps only after six" is a rule.

Write three to five. No more. A doctrine you can't remember isn't one. Put it on paper, in your own handwriting. Tape it inside a cupboard door where only you will see it. Not a frame on the wall — this isn't décor. It's a note.

Your rules cover guests too, and that's a good thing. Not as policing — as hospitality with a shape. This is how we do it here. Coats go there. Shoes off if you like. Someone will hand you something warm. When a home has clear, gentle rhythms, guests relax faster. They don't have to guess. The house tells them where they stand, and the standing is easy.

Keep the rules loose enough to retire. Cross one out when it stops being true. A winter rule doesn't have to survive July. The doctrine is alive — it changes when you change.

## Yours to keep

There's no inspection coming. Nobody is going to arrive and check whether your comfort makes sense to them. Comfort isn't a style you perform for visitors. It's what happens when a room agrees with you — when the setup fits the way you actually want to live, not the way you think you should.

Some nights you'll break every rule you wrote. That's allowed too. The list isn't a test. It's a note from you, to you, about what works. Break it freely. Come back to it freely.

Your house. Your hours. Your rules. Written by the only person who has to live in them — and the only person who knows, in the quiet of a good evening, what those rules actually are.
