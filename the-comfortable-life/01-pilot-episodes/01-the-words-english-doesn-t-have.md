# The words English doesn't have

*Some feelings are universal. The names for them aren't — and that gap matters more than you'd think.*

## A feeling with no name

There's a feeling you've had. A room, one lamp, someone you like, nothing happening. You went home and tried to describe it to someone. And the words came out wrong.

English handed you "nice." Maybe "cozy." Neither one was it. You could feel the thing, full and real, but the language gave you a thimble to carry it in.

Here's the strange part: other languages have a word for exactly that feeling. Several do. English never got one.

## Thin words

We have "comfortable." But comfortable is what a mattress is. It's what a doctor asks you about. It describes furniture and waiting rooms better than it describes an evening with the people you love. The word has a clinical ring to it — flat, functional, a checkbox.

And "cozy" has been sold to us. It's on candle labels now. On blanket tags. A word that used to mean something got hired out as advertising. You see it in a store window and your eye slides past it. It doesn't point at the feeling anymore. It points at a product.

So we end up pointing. Gesturing at something almost everyone has felt and nobody can hold. And a feeling you can't name is easy to lose track of. It comes and goes, and you don't really know what made it arrive or what made it leave. You just notice, sometimes, that it was here. And isn't.

## Five rooms, five words

The Danes say *hygge*. Low light, easy company, no agenda. But listen to how they use it — it's less a mood that arrives than something people *do*. Candles lit on a Tuesday, for no occasion at all. An ordinary evening made slightly deliberate. The Danes know something we tend to forget: comfort is a practice, not a purchase. You don't acquire hygge. You make it, on purpose, with almost nothing.

In Norway, *koselig*. The word carries the cold inside it. You don't get the feeling without the weather — the wind, the wool, the door shutting behind you. Norwegians know that cozy begins with cold. The warmth means more because you earned your way in from something. Without the contrast, it's just temperature.

The Dutch say *gezelligheid*, and they mean people. A packed table, everyone talking over each other. A perfect empty room can't be gezellig. There has to be someone in it — ideally several someones, ideally a little too many. The Dutch know that comfort is social before it is soft. The togetherness is the comfort. The rest is scenery.

German gives us *Gemütlichkeit*. Warmth with edges. A place with a shape to it, where the day is over because you decided it was, and the door agrees. There's a boundary built into the word — a sense that this space is *this* space, and the outside has been politely but firmly closed off. The Germans know that comfort needs boundaries. Not walls, exactly. But a clear edge. A here-and-not-there.

And in Japan, *omotenashi* — care so exact it disappears. The cushion square to the mat. The cup set down at the right second. You feel looked after and never see the hand. The Japanese know that comfort is attention. Not grand gestures. Just someone who noticed, quietly, what you needed before you had to ask.

## A word is a container

None of these translate cleanly. That's the interesting part. A word like that isn't decoration. It's a container. It tells a whole country what to look for, and what to bother protecting.

Once you can name a feeling, you start noticing it. You notice when a room has it. You notice when it's missing, and you can guess why — too bright, too rushed, too alone. The word gives you a handle. Something to pick the feeling up with, turn it over, understand its shape.

You can't tend what you can't name. And comfort is something worth tending.

## Borrow the words

You already know this feeling. A kitchen at eleven at night. A car with rain on the roof. Somebody's back step at dusk, no plans, no phone out. A porch in the evening with the day finally letting go of its heat.

You just never had a word for it. So it stayed an accident — something that happened to you occasionally, instead of something you might have made on purpose.

So borrow theirs. Not the candles, not the blankets — the *words*. Not the aesthetic, not the stuff you can buy. The language. The attention the language makes possible. The words will show you what to look for. And once you're looking, you start to see how much of it is already right there, waiting to be noticed and tended.

That's the first step. Not buying anything. Not changing anything yet. Just giving a name to the thing you've been chasing without knowing it.
