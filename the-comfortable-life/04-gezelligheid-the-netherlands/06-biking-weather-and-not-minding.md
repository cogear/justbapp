# Biking, weather, and not minding

*What the Dutch know about comfort: it's built by showing up, not by waiting for the weather to cooperate.*

## The Bridge in the Rain

It's raining in Utrecht. Not dramatic rain. The flat grey kind that's been going since Tuesday and has no plans to stop. And the bridge over the canal is full of bicycles.

No technical jackets. No special gear. A man in a wool coat that will take two days to dry. A woman holding an umbrella in one hand and the handlebars in the other. Nobody looks brave.

They're not making a point about hardiness. They're going somewhere. Somebody is expecting them.

That last part matters more than anything else in this picture. The bicycles, the rain, the wool coat — none of it is about the weather. It's about the room at the other end. The table. The person pouring the second cup. The Dutch word *gezelligheid* gets translated as "coziness" sometimes, but it's really about this: the feeling of arriving where you're expected, where the room is warm and someone was in the middle of a story.

## Weather as a Veto

Where I grew up, weather was a veto. You'd check the forecast the night before and watch that little cloud icon decide whether the evening was still happening.

And when it rained, the plan didn't get cancelled. It just quietly stopped being likely. Nobody had to say anything. It went soft, and then it was gone.

A whole winter can pass like that. Not because going out was hard. Because it was never quite easy enough.

There's a particular kind of comfort we build around this avoidance. The blanket, the streaming service, the delivery app. It's real comfort. But it's comfort that costs you the other thing — the room with people in it. The table. The story you arrived in the middle of.

## Niet Zeuren

The Dutch have a phrase for this. *Niet zeuren.* Don't whine. Written down it sounds stern. In practice it's said with affection, usually while someone is handing you a towel.

It doesn't mean the rain is pleasant. It means the rain isn't the subject. You're wet, you'll dry, the room is warm, sit down. Somebody was in the middle of a story.

Not minding isn't the same as not feeling it. Your fingers are genuinely cold. The wind on the open stretch is genuinely miserable. You just don't build a case out of it. You don't let it become the whole evening.

Weather is a fact. It isn't a verdict on your evening, or a message about how your life is going. It's just what's happening outside. The Dutch seem to have figured out something that a lot of us miss: if you wait for the weather to be pleasant before you live your life, you will wait a very long time.

## Arriving Damp

Here's what the bike actually gives you. Twelve minutes of weather on your face, and then a door.

The coat goes over the back of a chair and starts to steam. Your hair is flat and there's nothing to be done about it. Your hands are cold enough to really feel the cup.

And nobody remarks on any of it. No one says you must be freezing. Someone hooks a chair over with their foot and keeps talking. That's the entire ceremony. You were expected, and now you're here.

Because the thing that would have spoiled the night was never the rain. It was staying in and calling it sensible.

This is what gezelligheid is really about. Not a curated atmosphere. Not the right candles or the right light. It's the fact of being together, in a room, with the weather doing whatever it's doing outside. Comfort, in this tradition, is social before it's soft. The warmth comes from the people, not the upholstery.

## Go Anyway

You are not going to be handed a season of perfect evenings. You'll be handed ordinary Tuesdays with weather in them.

So go anyway. Wear the coat you already own. Let your shoes get dark at the toes. Arrive damp and slightly windblown and sit down where the people are.

The point was never to stay dry. The point was to get there.
