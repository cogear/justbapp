# Borrel: drinks, bitterballen, and no agenda

*A Dutch ritual teaches us that comfort doesn't need an occasion — just a time, a few people, and something salty on a plate.*

## Shall we have a borrel

Around five in the afternoon, in a lot of Dutch places, something small happens. Somebody says: *shall we have a borrel?* And that is the entire plan.

A drink. A bowl of something fried. Mustard on a little plate. Nobody is celebrating anything. Nobody prepared remarks.

The word comes from a verb. *Borrelen* — to have a quick drink, together. In Dutch it is something you do, not something you throw. That distinction matters more than it sounds. A party is a production. A borrel is a pause.

## Why we stopped gathering

Most of us need a reason to gather. A birthday. A goodbye. A holiday. Something big enough to justify the trouble.

And because there's a reason, there's a standard. The house should be clean. The food should be real food. Somebody should probably make a toast. Maybe a playlist.

So we wait for the occasion that's big enough. And the months pass, and the people we love most become people we see three times a year.

This is one of the quiet tragedies of busy lives. Not that we don't want to see each other. We do. But we've built a world where gathering requires a threshold — a bar high enough that clearing it feels like an event rather than a Tuesday. The Dutch have a different idea. They lower the bar until anyone can step over it without thinking.

## A pause with people in it

A borrel isn't an event. It's a pause with other people in it. An hour where the day has stopped being work and hasn't yet turned into evening. That in-between space is where a certain kind of comfort lives.

The snacks are deliberately unimpressive. Fried croquettes. Cheese with mustard. Nuts poured straight from the bag. Nothing anyone had to cook. Nothing anyone can be judged for. That lack of ambition is the point. When the food is casual, the conversation can be casual. Nobody is performing hospitality. Nobody is receiving it formally.

Everything sits in the middle. You reach across each other. Somebody takes the last bitterbal, thinks better of it, and cuts it in half. Sharing off one plate does something a plated meal can't. It dissolves the boundary between host and guest. Everyone is just there, elbow to elbow, reaching for the same things.

## The lower the bar, the more often you clear it

And that's the real trick. The lower the bar, the more often you can clear it. A gathering that costs nothing to host can happen on a Tuesday. It can happen next week. It can happen because someone said five words at 4:47.

No occasion. No agenda. Just an hour with the edge taken off the day.

The Dutch have a word for the feeling this creates — *gezelligheid*. It's been translated a hundred ways: cozy, convivial, warm, belonging. But the closest meaning might be *the comfort of being together without effort*. It's not the softness of a blanket. It's the ease of people who have stopped trying to impress each other and can simply be in the same room.

## How to do it badly

So: pick a time. Say five. Tell three people. Don't clean the rooms nobody is going to walk into. This last part is important. The instinct to tidy is the instinct to perform, and performing kills the thing.

Put out one thing. Whatever's already in the cupboard. Cut it into small pieces and set it down where people are standing. That's most of the work, honestly. If you spent more than ten minutes preparing, you've already overshot.

Stay on your feet if you can. Standing keeps things moving. People drift, conversations stay short and easy, and nobody gets trapped in a chair next to someone they've run out of things to say to. A chair asks for a commitment. Standing only asks that you be present.

Then let it end. A borrel doesn't have to become dinner. Somebody glances at the clock, coats come off the chairs, and everyone goes home. Nothing was decided. Nothing was resolved. That's what success looks like.

## You just need a time

There's a kind of comfort that only comes from people you didn't have to impress. It doesn't arrive at big occasions. It shows up on ordinary evenings, in rooms that weren't tidied first.

It's made of an hour, a small glass, something salty, and no purpose at all. Which means you don't have to wait for anything. You don't need a reason to gather people.

You just need a time. Say five. See who comes.
