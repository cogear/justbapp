# The screened porch as your Stube

*In a hot climate, the room you keep ignoring is the one that holds your comfort.*

There's a room in your house that isn't really in your house. Screened on three sides. Concrete floor. A ceiling fan that wobbles off-center. It's probably where the bikes live, and the bag of potting soil, and the cooler you never emptied.

In most houses here, that room is storage. Somewhere you pass through carrying groceries in, hauling trash out. A place you never stop.

I want to make the case that it's the most important room you've got.

## What the Alps knew

In the German-speaking Alps, there's a room called the Stube. Wood-paneled, usually. One tiled stove in the corner that throws heat into the walls and holds it there for hours. In an old farmhouse, the Stube was the only room anybody bothered to heat.

So everything happened there. Meals, mending, homework, arguments, dozing. Not because it was beautiful. Because it was the one room the weather couldn't get into.

And it had a door. A real one, with a latch. You entered the Stube. You didn't just wander into it on the way somewhere else. The door made it a destination.

There's something important in that. A room isn't just walls and a roof. A room is a place you choose to be. The Stube worked because people treated it like it mattered — like the center of the house, which it was.

## The question turns around

We don't have a cold problem here. We have the opposite one. For half the year, outside is not a place you want to be after about ten in the morning. The sun runs everything. The glare, the humidity, the still heavy air — that's our winter.

So the question turns around. In the Alps, they asked: which room can the cold not reach? Here, we ask: which room can the heat and glare not reach, once the sun finally lets go of the day?

That's when the screened porch becomes the best room in the house. Evening. The breeze picks up. The light goes soft. The air moves again.

The screen is the whole trick. It's a wall that lets the air through. You get outside — the sound of it, the moving air, the smell after rain — without paying what outside usually costs. That isn't a compromise. It's a room the Alps never had. Half in, half out. And the half that's out is the half you came for.

## Furnish it like a room

So furnish it like a room. Not patio furniture — that stuff is designed to be looked at and sat in briefly. An actual chair. The kind you'd sit in for an hour without shifting around. Something your body settles into.

A lamp. Run an extension cord if that's what it takes. A small table at elbow height, because a room is a place where you can set a glass down. Something under your feet — a rug, a mat, whatever softens the concrete. A light you can turn low when the evening deepens.

Then take out everything that isn't for sitting. The bikes. The bag of soil. The box you keep meaning to deal with. A room can't be a room and a hallway at the same time. It has to commit to being one thing.

Then use that door on purpose. Not passing through — arriving. Same chair, same hour, most evenings. Repetition is most of what turns a space into a room. The Stube wasn't special because it was decorated. It was special because people kept showing up there, every night, and the room absorbed that.

## Bring people out there

The Stube was never a room for one. It was where the household gathered because it was the one warm place. There's a lesson in that for us too.

Two chairs, angled slightly toward each other. Close enough that nobody has to raise their voice. That's all it takes. The porch becomes the place where the day gets talked over. Where someone sits with a drink and the conversation goes where it goes.

You don't have to build any of this. The room already exists. It's on the back of your house right now, holding your bicycles and a bag of potting soil, waiting to be promoted.

It doesn't need a stove. It needs a chair, a lamp, and someone who decides to go sit down.
