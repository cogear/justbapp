# Keyif — Turkey: idle pleasure, unapologetic

*Some cultures don't just permit idleness — they name it, claim it, and refuse to apologize for it.*

## the man on the stool

Somewhere along the water in Istanbul, right now, a man is sitting on a plastic stool with a small glass of tea. He has been there an hour. He is not waiting for anyone.

He is not on a break from something. He is not resting up for tomorrow. There is no errand waiting at the other end of this hour. He is doing the thing itself.

And if you asked him what the thing is, he would have a word for it. Not *nothing.* A word with some dignity in it.

That word is keyif.

## rest with a job to do

Most of us couldn't sit there. Not because we don't have the hour — most days we could find one. Because we can't seem to have one without a reason attached to it.

So we give rest a job. We call it recharging. Recovery. Self-care, which somehow sounds like maintenance on a vehicle. We say we've *earned* it, as if the other option were stealing.

But a pleasure that has to justify itself has stopped being a pleasure. It's become a task with better lighting.

We have built a world where doing nothing requires a case. You need to be tired enough. You need to be far enough behind to deserve a pause. You need to frame it as investment in future productivity, or wellness, or mental health — something with a measurable return. Rest that is merely pleasant, with no outcome attached, feels suspicious. Frivolous. A little embarrassing.

## what keyif means

Turkish has a word for the other way of doing this. Keyif. Dictionaries offer pleasure, delight, good spirits. All true. All a little thin.

Keyif is closer to a mood you step into and then stay inside. The tea. The shade. The sound of the water. The particular ease of nothing at all being asked of you.

And it's a verb. *Keyif yapmak* — to do keyif. You can say it out loud. You can tell people plainly that this is where you'll be, and for how long.

That's the part that stops me. Not that people idle — everyone idles, everywhere. That here it has a name, and the name arrives with no apology stitched to it.

In Turkish culture, keyif is not a gap between obligations. It is not the leftover time after the real things are done. It is one of the real things. You schedule around it. You protect it. You don't explain why you need it because nobody asks.

## what it runs on

Look at what it runs on. A small glass of tea. A stool somebody carried out of a shop. A view of whatever happens to pass by. Keyif was never for sale.

Which means it isn't waiting on anything. Not a better chair. Not a free weekend. Not the version of your life where you've finally finished everything.

What it asks for is a decision. Sit facing something that moves — water, a street, branches. Put the phone somewhere else, not face-down beside you, because face-down is still here, still humming. Then stay longer than seems reasonable.

The first minutes will itch. Your mind will hand you errands, one at a time, to see whether you'll take them. Don't. Somewhere past that, the hour opens up.

## what this culture knows

Turkey knows something most of us have forgotten: idle pleasure doesn't need a reason. It doesn't need to be earned. It doesn't need to produce anything. It just needs to be taken.

The comfort here is not in the stool or the tea — those are almost nothing. The comfort is in the permission. The cultural agreement that sitting with no purpose is not a failure of ambition. It is a legitimate way to spend a piece of your life.

## no reason required

You don't have to be exhausted to deserve an hour like this. You don't have to have finished anything first.

The warm evening. The glass in your hand. The time with nothing in it. That isn't the prize at the end of your life's work. That is some of your actual life, happening.

Take it in plain sight. Take it without explaining. Nobody is collecting reasons at the door.
