# Why tending calms

*A few quiet minutes with something alive that asks almost nothing of you.*

## The Plant on the Sill

There's a plant on my windowsill that I check every morning. I don't do much for it. I look at it. I press a finger into the soil. Then I go on with my day.

It's a pothos. Not rare. Not thriving, especially. Just there, doing its slow thing on the sill above the sink.

It's not a hobby. It's not a project. It's about four minutes that reliably leave me steadier than I was, and for a long time I couldn't tell you why. I can now, mostly. It has to do with the kind of attention a living thing asks for, and how different that is from the kind of attention everything else asks for.

## Everything Answers Back

Most of what we pay attention to pays us back right away. A screen answers the second you touch it. A message arrives. A number goes up. Everything is quick, and quick is loud.

So attention gets trained short. We check, we refresh, we measure. Even resting starts to feel like something we're supposed to be doing well.

A plant will not participate in any of that. It has no notifications. It will not tell you how you're doing. You can stare at it for a minute and it will give you nothing back — no feedback, no confirmation that you've shown up, no little reward for caring.

That sounds ungrateful, but I think it's the whole point.

## A Slower Clock

A plant grows at its own speed, which is slow, and mostly invisible. You can't hurry it. You can't ask how it's doing and get an answer today.

So tending puts you on a slower clock. Water. Light. A small turn toward the window. Then waiting. And the waiting isn't the boring part between the useful parts. It's most of what tending is.

There's no version of this where you get ahead. You can't water a week's worth in advance. You can't batch it. You can't optimize it into something shorter. The plant takes today's small amount of care, and then it would like you to leave it alone.

Care without a receipt. You give something small, and you don't learn for weeks whether it mattered. Somehow that's a relief.

I think it's a relief because so much of daily life runs on instant feedback and visible results. It's exhausting, even when it's going well. Tending a plant is the opposite. You do a small thing. You trust it. You come back later.

## What the Hands Do

And this is work your hands already know. Fingers into soil. A leaf turned over to look underneath. Dust wiped off with a thumb. Nothing to master. Nothing to perform.

For those few minutes, your attention points outward. Not at yourself, not at a screen. At something alive that isn't thinking about you at all, and never will.

That's the quiet part. Not emptying your mind — that never worked for me anyway. Just handing it something simple and outside itself to hold on to. A small, physical task with a living thing at the center of it. Your hands know what to do. Your mind can rest in that.

## Never Finished

I've kept some plants a long time. I've lost others. The losing is part of it, and it doesn't mean you're bad at this. Things die on windowsills. They do. You learn not to take it personally, and if you can't quite manage that, you learn to start again with something else.

And nothing here ever gets finished. A plant is never done. Neither is the tending. There is no completion, no final product, no moment where you can say *there, that's finished* and set it down. It just keeps going, or it doesn't.

I think that's the reason it calms. It asks nothing of you except that you come back. Not that you come back better. Not that you've learned something since yesterday. Just that you show up again, do the small thing, and let the rest be slow.

Water it. Turn it toward the light. Come back tomorrow. That's the whole of it.
