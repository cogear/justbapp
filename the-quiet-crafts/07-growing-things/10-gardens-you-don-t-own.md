# Gardens you don't own

*A garden isn't a deed. It's a habit of noticing what's growing around you and showing up for it.*

## The tree on my street

There's a tree on my street I don't own. Thin trunk, a metal grate around the roots, a square of bare dirt about the size of a doormat. I pass it four times a day.

Someone planted it before I moved here. Nobody in particular looks after it. Last August, when the heat came and the leaves went papery at the edges, I started carrying water down in a plastic jug.

It's a small thing. The jug is an old vinegar bottle with the label mostly peeled off. The walk from my kitchen to the tree takes about forty seconds. But something shifted in me the first time I crouched down and poured water onto that square of dirt. I wasn't a gardener. I didn't own anything. But I was tending something, and that felt different from anything I'd done in a long while.

## Waiting for land

There's a story many of us carry that says tending requires a deed. A garden means land. Land means money, or at least a lease with a clause that lets you dig a hole.

So people wait. They keep a list in their head of what they'd grow someday, in a yard they don't have yet, in a house they haven't found. The list gets longer. The years pass. The longing curdles into something that feels like patience but is really just postponement.

The waiting list is a comfortable place to live. It's safe. You never have to test whether you'd actually enjoy getting your hands dirty, because the conditions for finding out never quite arrive.

And meanwhile there's green everywhere around them going thirsty. Pots on shared stairwells with dust an inch thick on the leaves. A strip of dirt by the bins, half litter, half nettles, waiting for anyone at all. A tree pit on the corner with cigarette ends pressed into the soil like ugly seeds.

The world is full of small, neglected growing things. They don't care who owns them.

## Attention, not property

Tending isn't ownership. It's attention, and repetition, and coming back. Noticing what changed since the last time you looked. None of that requires paperwork.

When you strip away the ownership, what's left is the actual practice. The showing up. The looking closely. The learning what happens to a particular plant in a particular patch of ground through the seasons. You can do that anywhere. You can do it with someone else's window box.

And there's a particular quiet in caring for something you have no claim to. Nothing to show anyone. No before-and-after photos for social media. No plan for it at all. Just water, and a Tuesday evening, and your hands getting cold. The work is its own purpose. Nothing is being built. Nothing is being optimised.

## Where to put your hands

Start with what's near you. Water a neighbour's plants while they're away, and do it properly. Finger in the soil first — if it's damp an inch down, skip that pot and move to the next one. Water slowly, until it runs out the bottom. Turn the pots a quarter so the light gets round to all sides. This is real tending. Your neighbour may never know you did more than pour and walk away.

There's nearly always a plant in a shared hallway that belongs to nobody. A sad peace lily in a municipal pot. A spider plant someone abandoned when they moved out. Wipe the dust off the leaves with a damp cloth, one leaf at a time. No one will ever mention it. That's part of the appeal.

Community plots, if you have the patience for the waiting list. Volunteer mornings in a park, pulling bindweed alongside strangers who also don't own it. The tree pit on your road, cleared of crisp packets and bottle caps by hand.

Or nothing so organised. Learn one hedge on the way to the shop. Same hedge, every week, through a whole year. Watch how it changes. Notice when the buds break, when the berries form, when the leaves curl. Your eyes are doing the tending.

## Not yours, never will be

Here's the part that takes some getting used to. You may not be there when it flowers. Someone else will pick the fruit, or nobody will, and it'll drop and go soft on the pavement and feed whatever's underneath.

That's fine. That's most of gardening, honestly. The work outlasts the person doing it, and there's a strange relief in that. You're not the point. The plant isn't the point either. The tending is.

When you tend something you don't own, you're participating in a very old pattern. Someone before you planted. Someone after you will water. You're a link, not a destination. The tree on my street will outlive my time on this block. I'll move away eventually. Someone else will carry the jug.

So fill a jug. Go and look after something that isn't yours and never will be. Then walk home and have your dinner. You don't need to own a thing to care for it. You just need to notice it, and keep showing up.
