# Herbs on a windowsill

*A pot of mint that never makes it into dinner, and why that's exactly the point.*

## The pot by the sink

There's a pot of mint on my kitchen windowsill. It has never once made it into a meal.

But I brush my hand across it every time I pass the sink. Ten times a day, maybe. And every time, my hands smell like something green.

That's the whole return on the pot. Not dinner. Not a harvest. Just a smell I can reach with the back of my hand.

I used to think this was a failure. A plant should justify itself, shouldn't it? It should become something. A meal, a garnish, at the very least a story about self-sufficiency. But the mint just sits there, growing quietly behind the faucet, and my hand finds it the way it finds the light switch — without thinking.

## Sold as a supply

Herbs get sold to us as a supply. Basil for the pasta. Rosemary for the chicken. A tiny farm on your sill, quietly paying for itself.

So we buy the plastic-sleeved basil, use four leaves, and watch the rest go black in a week. And we decide we're bad at this. We tell ourselves we don't have a green thumb. We say it with the same flat voice we use when a recipe goes wrong or a gym membership goes unused.

You're not bad at it. You were handed the wrong reason.

Yield is a terrible reason to keep a plant on a windowsill. The sill is too small, the light is too thin, and one pot will never keep up with an appetite. If you're cooking seriously, you'll burn through a windowsill of basil in one dinner. And then the plant looks sad, and you feel worse.

The whole transaction gets framed around what the plant produces for you. But the best part of a windowsill herb isn't what it gives you to eat. It's what it gives you to touch.

## For touching

Here's a different reason. Herbs are the only plants you're meant to touch. Not just water and admire. Touch. Pinch. Bruise between your fingers.

Rosemary smells like almost nothing until you rub it. The oils live just under the surface, waiting for friction. Mint gives up nothing to the air and everything to a thumb. Basil bruises, and the whole kitchen knows — that sharp green sweetness blooming out of nowhere because you pressed a leaf between your fingers.

A windowsill of herbs is a row of small switches. You walk past, you flip one, and the room changes for a second. Not the lighting. Not the temperature. Just the air. Just your attention, redirected through your nose to something growing and alive.

Herbs are for touching. That's the whole reframe. Once you let go of the harvest, the plant becomes something else entirely. A scent machine. A small daily interruption. A living thing that asks nothing of you except to be noticed with your hands.

## Three pots

So: three pots. Not twelve. Not a greenhouse. Three.

Mint, because it's nearly impossible to kill. It will forgive you for forgetting, for overwatering, for underwatering. It grows like it has somewhere to be and no particular rush to get there.

Rosemary, because it smells like the inside of a warm afternoon. Rub a sprig between your fingers and you're suddenly standing somewhere Mediterranean and slow, even if you're really just waiting for the kettle to boil.

And one you simply like the look of. Not because it's useful. Not because a cookbook told you. Because the shape of the leaves makes you happy when you see it in the morning.

Pinch the tops. Not for the plant's sake, though it helps them bush out. Pinch them so your fingers learn the difference between thyme and oregano with your eyes shut. So you build a small vocabulary in your hands.

Turn the pots a quarter whenever you notice them leaning toward the glass — they're chasing the light, and a small rotation keeps them honest. Push a finger into the dirt before you reach for water. If it's damp an inch down, walk away. That's the entire maintenance list.

And when the basil finally goes leggy, flowers, and stops being any use at all — that's fine. It smelled good for a season. That was the point. Some plants are annuals. They do their thing and they're done. You don't owe them grief.

Some evening you'll tear one leaf into a glass of water, and it'll taste like almost nothing. Do it anyway. That's the plant and your day meeting each other. The faint green taste, the faint effort, the small ceremony of it.

## An arm's length

You don't have to earn the sill. A leaf in a glass of water counts. A pinch that goes nowhere but your fingertips counts. Nobody is weighing the harvest.

There's a green thing growing an arm's length from where you stand every morning. You touch it on the way past. It gives you one second of somewhere else. A second that smells like summer, or like rain on dry stone, or like nothing you can name.

That's enough. That's the whole craft. Keeping something alive, near you, on purpose — and letting it be useless.

The quiet of it sneaks up on you. One day you realize your hand goes to the rosemary before your brain does. That you've been turning the pots without noticing. That the windowsill smells like something, and so do your fingers, and so the morning starts with a small green thread that connects you to the world outside the glass.

It doesn't feed you. It doesn't save you money. It doesn't make you better at anything.

But it's there, growing, within reach. And that's something.
