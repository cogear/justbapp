# Container gardening

*You don't need land to grow something. You need a pot, some dirt, and a reason to go outside.*

## A bucket of dirt

There's a pot on my back step with nothing much in it. Some grass I didn't plant. A bit of thyme that's gone woody and stopped being useful. I look at it most days.

Technically, it's a garden. It's also a bucket of dirt on a step. Both of those are true, and neither one has ever bothered me.

If you've ever thought you couldn't grow things because you don't have anywhere to grow them — a balcony, a yard, a patch of soil to call your own — this is the answer. You need a pot. Any pot. A bucket with holes drilled in the bottom. A yogurt container. A cracked ceramic thing you almost threw out.

The word "garden" sounds like it belongs to someone with property and a plan. But a garden is just a place where you pay attention to something growing. That can happen in a coffee can.

## Walls are a kindness

Open ground is endless. You dig one bed and there's more bed behind it. Roots run off where they like. Weeds arrive from three gardens over. The work expands in every direction and never quite finishes.

A pot has walls. What's inside it is all there is to tend. You can see the whole of it from where you're standing. The edge of the pot is the edge of the task.

That edge isn't a limitation. It's a kindness. Nothing in a pot can get away from you.

Six containers you can actually reach are worth more to your hands than half an acre you can only stand at the edge of and worry about. A garden that fits in your arms is a garden you can know completely. You'll notice the first new leaf. You'll catch the dryness before anything wilts. The scale makes attention possible.

## Filling it

Start with the hole in the bottom. Everything else follows from it. Water has to be able to leave, or the roots sit in it and drown, slowly, out of sight. If there's no hole, make one. If the pot is too thick to drill, use it as a cachepot — a decorative shell with a cheaper plastic pot sitting inside it, lifted on a few pebbles so it doesn't sit in standing water.

Then you fill it. Use your hands if you like the feel of it — the cool of the soil, the grit under your nails. There's something in the temperature of fresh potting mix that tells your body what season you're in, even when your head has lost track. Stop about an inch below the rim. That gap is for water — without it, you'll overflow every time and wash soil onto the step.

Press it down with your palms. Firm, not packed. You want it to hold together and still let air through. Your hands learn the difference after two or three pots. It's one of those things you can't read your way into. You just have to feel it wrong once or twice.

Then lift it. It's heavier than you expected. Soil and water weigh more than they look like they should. That weight in your arms is the thing you just made.

## The shuffling

A container asks something small of you, often. There's no deep ground under it to draw on, so it dries faster. You check with a finger, an inch down. Not a schedule. A finger. The soil will tell you.

And you can move it. That's the part I didn't expect to love. A pot sulking in the shade can be picked up and carried into the sun. Try that with a flower bed. Something not thriving by the wall can be walked to the middle of the step. A tender thing can come inside when the forecast drops.

So you shuffle them. A little further from the wall in July. Back under the eaves when the wind turns. Nobody's watching. Nobody needs to. It's between you and the plant, and the plant isn't keeping score.

And when you group them close, they shelter each other. Damper air between the leaves. Wind breaks against the outer pots before it reaches the inner ones. Three pots stop being three pots and start being a place — a little microclimate you built without thinking about the word microclimate.

## Permission

None of this has to produce anything. A pot of grass is allowed. A pot with one flower in it that lasted nine days is allowed. A pot where you tried chives and they survived but you never actually cut them and ate them — that's fine too.

What you're really doing is going outside twice a day to crouch down and look at something at the level of your knees. That's the practice. The plant is just what makes you come.

A container, some soil, a little water, somewhere to put it. That's a garden. It doesn't have to be more than that.
