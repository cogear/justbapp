# Soil, plainly

*A bag of soil, hands in it, and the quiet permission to begin before you understand anything.*

## The bag by the back door

There's a bag of soil in a lot of houses that never gets opened. Folded over at the top, sitting by the back door. Or in the shed, or on a balcony. Waiting until you understand it properly.

Because soil feels like something you should understand before you're allowed to touch it. Like there's a test. Like someone's going to check.

So let's not wait for that. Here it is, plainly.

## The homework problem

Walk into a garden centre and there are twelve bags lined up. All of them dirt. All of them insisting they're different from each other. The labels are loud. Coir. Perlite. Loam for this, ericaceous for that. pH this, pH the other. You came for soil. You left with homework.

And homework is where a lot of plants die. Not from neglect. From waiting. Waiting for the right mix, the right knowledge, the right Saturday when you've finally read enough. The plant sits in its plastic pot by the window and the weeks go by.

There's no exam. The plant doesn't check your credentials. It doesn't need you to have a framework or a system. It needs you to begin.

The truth is that people have grown things in whatever was underfoot for a very long time. Long before the bags, long before the words. They watched what happened, adjusted, and kept going. That's most of it. Attention and a willingness to keep going.

## What soil actually does

So here's what soil is for. Three things, mostly.

It holds the plant up. That's the obvious one.

It holds water. That's the one everyone knows.

And — this is the one people miss — it holds air. That's most of the job.

Roots breathe. We don't tend to think of them that way, but they do. When soil packs down hard and stays soaked, the roots aren't drowning in water so much as suffocating for want of air. Nothing you can see is happening above the surface. The plant just slowly stops. Goes quiet. Loses its colour. You might think it needs more water, give it more water, and make it worse.

That's why the good stuff is loose and crumbly and full of gaps. It takes a drink, keeps some, and lets the rest run out the bottom. It holds and it lets go, both at once.

There's a little food in there too. Less than the bag suggests. Enough for a while. It gets used up, which is why old soil goes tired — grey, compacted, spent. Not broken, just finished with what it had to give.

## Hands in it

So. Hands. No gloves, not for this part.

Take a handful and squeeze. It should hold its shape for a second — a brief, soft clump — then break apart when you open your fingers. If it won't hold at all, it's too dry and loose. If it stays in a hard lump, it's too heavy and wet. You'll feel which it is. Your hand already knows how to run this test. Nobody had to teach you, and nobody needs to.

Then smell it. Good soil smells like the floor of a wood after rain. That particular dark, clean, slightly sweet scent. Sour or stale is a different smell, and you'll know it without being told. Something slightly off. Something tired. Everyone can tell the difference. You knew it before you read this.

Every so often — a year, maybe two — tip a plant out and look at what's underneath. Roots circling round and round, soil gone grey and solid, the pot full of root and not much else. Break it up. Give it fresh. Tuck it back in. That's repotting, and it's gentler than it sounds.

If the soil keeps setting hard between waterings, mix in a handful of something gritty. Sand, small bark, those white specks from the garden centre. Not a recipe. Just more gaps. More air. That's the whole principle — keep the gaps in.

## What the label can't give you

You don't need the vocabulary. Nobody's going to ask you for it. Not the plant, not the soil, not anyone whose opinion matters.

You need to be willing to put your hands in it. That's the part that costs nothing and teaches the most. There's something in the doing that a bag label can't give you. The weight of it, cool in your palm. The way it crumbles or holds. Dirt under your nails for a day or two afterwards, a quiet reminder that you did something real.

Soil is the plainest thing in the world. Loose, damp, dark, a bit alive. Get some. Open the bag. Put your hands in and feel what's there. You already know more than you think.
