# Propagation: more from what you already have

*A jar of water, a cutting, and the strange satisfaction of making something from almost nothing.*

## A jar on the sill

There's a jar on my windowsill with a piece of plant in it. No pot. No soil. Just a cut stem in water, and a few pale roots reaching down into the glass.

It came from a plant in the corner of the same room. A stem I snipped with kitchen scissors on a Tuesday, for no particular reason.

I didn't buy it. I didn't order it online or drive anywhere to get it. I made it — which is a strange word for something I mostly just left alone.

But that's what propagation is. You take one thing and, through almost no effort of your own, it becomes two. The plant does the work. You just provide the water and the window.

## Going out to get more

When we want more green in a room, our first instinct is usually to go out and acquire it. A shop, a shelf, a plastic pot with a price sticker still stuck to the rim.

That's fine. There's nothing wrong with buying a plant. But there's another way, and it's older, and it costs nothing. You take a plant you already have, and you ask it for a piece of itself.

Most of the plants we keep indoors are absurdly generous. Cut them in the right place and they don't diminish. They double. You end up with the same plant you started with, plus a new one, plus the quiet satisfaction of having done something with your hands that resulted in a living thing.

There aren't many activities that offer that.

## Ninety seconds

The whole procedure takes about ninety seconds. Maybe less if you know where you're cutting.

Find a stem with a few leaves on it. Look for the small bump where a leaf meets the stem — that little node is where roots want to come from. Cut just below it.

Strip the lowest leaf so that part of the stem can sit in water without rotting. Put the stem in a clean glass of water, somewhere with light but not hard, direct sun. Change the water when you remember — every few days, or whenever it looks cloudy.

That's it. You're done. There's nothing left to do but glance at it now and then.

Pothos, tradescantia, mint, spider plants, most succulents — all of these will root in water without any encouragement. If you're not sure whether a plant will take, try it anyway. The worst outcome is a stem that softens and goes in the bin. Nothing lost.

## On its own schedule

Roots come on their own schedule. Some in a week. Some in a month. Some never — and you only find that out slowly, by watching the stem go soft and translucent at the base.

You can't hurry it. There's no version of this where paying more attention makes it go faster. No app helps. No technique shortcuts it. Which is, I think, part of why it calms the hands and the mind.

What you get instead is a small daily glance. A checking-in that asks nothing of you. The white thread that wasn't there yesterday. A root tip curling against the glass like a comma.

And the object of all this is not a garden. It's not a project or a goal. It's a jar of water with something living in it, on a sill, doing exactly what it does. You made it, and then you let it be.

## Giving it away

Eventually you have more plants than you planned to have. Cuttings in jam jars, in mugs, in the glass you can't find a lid for. Lined up along every surface that gets decent light.

You don't need them. That's the good part. Nobody needs eleven pothos. Nobody needs a forest of spider plant babies. So you give them away — to a neighbour, to your sister, to the person at work who mentioned their flat felt dark and empty.

All of it came from one plant you already owned. You didn't add anything from outside. You just paid attention to where the plant was already trying to go, and gave it somewhere to do that.

More, from what you already have. Not by buying. Not by building. Just by noticing what was already there, and making room for it to continue.

The quietest kind of making there is.
