# Seeds, patience, and failure

*A seed is almost nothing, and that's exactly the point.*

## Almost nothing

Tip a packet of seeds into your palm. You'll look down and wonder whether someone has sold you dust. Marigold seeds look like tiny splinters. Lettuce seeds are like grains of sand someone dropped on the floor. Tomato seeds are small enough to lose in the crease of your hand.

Something that small holding a whole plant seems unlikely. But you press them into damp soil anyway. You cover them over. You water gently. And then — nothing.

That's the part nobody warns you about. Growing things starts with a long stretch of nothing. Days of it. A week, maybe two. The tray sits there looking exactly like a tray of dirt, because that is precisely what it is.

We don't have many experiences left that begin this way. Most things in our lives show us something immediately — a loading bar, a confirmation email, a first paragraph. Seeds don't. They keep their own counsel, underground, in the dark, and they don't owe you a sign.

## A tray of dirt

For a week you look at a tray of dirt. You look in the morning with your coffee. You look again before bed. The soil looks exactly the same as it did yesterday, and the day before that.

There's nothing to do. You can't hurry a seed. You can't check on progress without digging it up and ruining the whole thing. The work is happening somewhere you can't see, on a timeline you didn't set.

This is uncomfortable at first. We've been trained to think that if we're not doing something, nothing is happening. But the seed is doing plenty. It's taking in water, splitting its coat, sending a root downward before it ever sends anything up. It just doesn't perform for an audience.

The seed keeps its own calendar. Some come up in four days. Some take three weeks. Some were never going to come up at all. You find that out by waiting — the only way there is.

## Most of them won't

Then some come up. Not all. Rarely most. You'll get a patch of green and four empty cells of bare soil sitting alongside them, and there's no explanation you will ever find that feels satisfying.

Here's the thing that takes a while to understand. In a seed packet, failure isn't a mistake. It's the arithmetic. A single plant in the wild scatters thousands of seeds so that a handful can land somewhere suitable, survive, and carry on. You're working inside those odds, not against them. The packet in your hand is a small piece of that ancient strategy.

And some come up and then die anyway. Too wet. Too cold. Knocked over by a cat. You didn't do it wrong, exactly. You can do everything right and still lose a few. That's not a moral failing or a lesson you were supposed to learn. It just goes that way sometimes.

If you can sit with that — the bare cells next to the green ones, the seedling that flopped over for no reason — you're learning something seeds have always known. Life is generous and indifferent at the same time.

## Sow more than you need

So sow more than you need. Twice what you want. Seeds cost almost nothing, and sowing plenty takes the whole enterprise out of the realm of pass and fail.

Write the date on a stick and push it in. Not to keep score — just so you know whether it's been six days or sixteen, so you stop asking the tray for news it doesn't have. The tray has no news. It will tell you when it tells you.

Then leave it alone. Go and do the dishes. Read a book. Walk around the block. A seed doesn't need your attention, only your patience — and patience, with seeds, mostly looks like being somewhere else.

This is the quiet practice. Not the sowing, which takes five minutes. Not the watering, which takes thirty seconds. The practice is the leaving alone. The trusting that something is happening where you can't see it, on a schedule you don't control, whether you watch or not.

## One green hook

And then one morning there it is. A green hook pushing up through the surface, bent double, still carrying the seed case on its back like a tiny helmet it hasn't figured out how to remove yet.

Nothing you did made that happen. You gave it somewhere to do it — damp soil, a little warmth, a bit of light. The rest was already in the seed. The rest was always in the seed.

It might never come to anything. It doesn't have to be food, or beautiful, or a gift for someone. It doesn't need a purpose beyond the fact of it. It's a plant you started from a speck. That's enough for it to be.

Patience isn't a virtue you earn by growing things. It's just the pace of the thing. You can match it, or you can grow nothing at all. The seed doesn't care which you choose. But if you choose to match it — to slow down to the speed of something that takes its own time — you might find that the tray of dirt was never really about the plants.

It was about learning to wait without flinching. To do a small thing and step back. To let something unfold at its own pace, in its own way, without your help. That's a practice you can carry into the rest of a life. The seeds just make it plain.
