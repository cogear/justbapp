# The tyranny of the side hustle

*On the kindest, heaviest sentence in the English language — and what it costs you.*

## The kindest sentence

Someone picks up the thing you made. Turns it over in their hands, checks the underside, the way people do when they're genuinely looking. And then they say it.

You could sell these.

They mean it so well. It's how we've been taught to praise things — by imagining a price for them. A compliment dressed up as a business plan. And for a second it lands beautifully, because someone saw what you made and thought it had value.

But something in you goes quiet when you hear it. And you're not always sure why.

## Someone else in the room

Here's why. The moment that sentence is spoken, the thing has a price. You don't have to accept it. You don't have to list it anywhere, open an account, print a label, or buy shipping supplies. But the question was asked. And questions like that don't unask themselves.

The next time you sit down to work, there's someone else in the room. A customer. An audience. A voice comparing your uneven edges to the ones you've seen on a screen — made by someone doing this forty hours a week, with better tools and a ring light.

And the quiet you came for is gone.

You're not making anymore. You're producing. It sounds like a small difference. It changes the whole hour. Your hands are still moving, but the quality of attention shifts. You're thinking about consistency now. About whether the next one will be good enough. About what people will say when they see it.

## Earning its keep

We've built a world where a hobby needs a business plan. Where a quiet evening has to earn its keep. Where every skill gets asked, sooner or later, what it's worth — and the only answer we seem to accept is a number.

So we monetize the one thing that was ours. The bread. The drawing. The wobbly bowl. The scarf that took three weeks and has a dropped stitch you decided to leave in because it looked like it belonged there. And then we wonder why we don't want to do it anymore.

I've watched people fall out of love with their own hands this way. Not because the work got harder — it didn't. Because it got a job. Something you did for relief became something that owed you a return. Something that once held no expectations suddenly held nothing but.

The tragedy isn't that you might fail at selling. The tragedy is that you stopped coming to the table for yourself.

## Nothing to pay back

Here's the sentence I'd like to hand back to you. Some things don't have to pay for themselves.

Your hands are allowed to spend an hour on something with no margin, no market, nobody waiting for it. That isn't wasted time. That's what the hour was for — to be spent, not invested. To be used up by your own choosing, on your own terms, for no reason that would make sense on a spreadsheet.

When someone says *you could sell these*, you can just say thank you. You can let it be a compliment and nothing else. You don't have to agree. You don't have to carry it upstairs and think about it in bed.

## Keep one thing

Notice where the pressure sits in your body when it creeps in. Shoulders climbing. Jaw tightening. The wanting to go faster, cleaner, more like the pictures. That's the moment to slow back down. Next stitch. Next cut. Next fold.

There's an old kind of quiet in work nobody is waiting for. Not laziness. Not wasted time. Just the one part of the day that isn't answering to anybody. The one room where no one is a customer, including you.

So keep something back. One thing you make that has no price on it at all. No plan, no audience, no future. Just your hands, and the hour, and the particular silence that fills the space when you stop needing it to be anything other than what it is.
