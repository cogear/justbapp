# Sharing work that isn't good yet

*The apology comes before the object. Maybe it doesn't have to.*

You made a thing. A bowl, a sock, a wooden box with a lid that doesn't quite close. It's a little crooked, and before anyone can even look at it, you hear yourself start explaining. It isn't good yet. I'm still learning. The rim went weird on this one.

That's the sentence we lead with. We hand the thing over already apologized for, already smaller than it was. We point at the flaw before anyone else can find it, as if getting there first might soften the blow.

But the crookedness isn't really the problem. The problem is the flinch — that half-second where you decide the thing needs a defense before it even gets a look. The flinch says more than the flaw ever could.

## Where the middle goes

Almost everything you've ever seen someone make, you met after it was finished. The sanded edge. The seam pulled out and redone. The glaze that finally took. Nobody photographs the middle. Nobody posts the lopsided attempt that came before the one that worked. So the middle starts to feel like something shameful, something you're supposed to skip past on the way to the real thing.

And you learn a rule nobody actually said out loud: don't show it until it's earned being seen. Until it's good. Until it holds up. So the half-done things go in a drawer.

A drawer is a very quiet place. Nothing in there can disappoint anyone. Nothing in there is quite alive, either. The things in drawers don't get turned over in someone's hands. They don't start conversations. They just sit.

## Showing, not submitting

There's a difference between showing someone something and handing it in. Same object. Same crooked rim. Completely different act, depending on what you're asking for.

"Is this good?" asks for a verdict. "Look what I did" asks for company. You get to decide which one you're asking, and most of the time you can just… not ask the first one. You can skip the evaluation entirely and simply share that this is something you spent an evening doing.

And here's what I've noticed. If you don't cue people to judge, most of them won't. They'll turn it over. They'll ask how you did the corners. They'll run a thumb along the uneven part, not because they're inspecting it but because they're curious. That's all you wanted anyway — someone curious, not someone scoring you.

## Out, at eye level

So try it without the preface. Hand it over. Don't say what it should have been. Don't narrate the gap where it went wrong. Let them find that themselves, or not. Most people aren't looking for flaws. They're looking at the fact that a thing exists at all, and that someone they know made it.

Put the wobbly one on the shelf, at eye level, next to things you didn't make. Not hidden. Not framed either. Just out, being what it is. A crooked bowl holds soup just as well as a perfect one, and it has a better story if anyone asks.

And be a little careful who you show. Not everyone is easy with unfinished things. Some people get uncomfortable with imperfection and will rush to fix it with advice you didn't ask for. One person who's simply glad you're making something is plenty. You don't need a crowd. You don't need a jury. You need a witness, maybe two.

## Seen partway

Work that isn't good yet is still work. It's the only kind anybody has ever had at the beginning, including everyone whose finished things you admire. That bowl you saw at the craft fair, the one with the flawless glaze — there were thirty bowls before it, and most of them were rough. You just never saw those. The roughness wasn't a failure. It was the path.

And it gets easier to keep going once it's been seen. The thing stops being a secret. There's nothing left to catch you out, because you already showed it, wobble and all. The gap between what you made and what you hoped to make gets to just be a gap, not a confession.

You're not asking anyone to like it. You're letting them know this is something you do now. That's a different kind of sentence — quieter, steadier, and it doesn't need a defense.

You're allowed to be seen partway. That's where most of a life actually happens — partway through, hands still learning, with a bowl that doesn't sit flat and a lid that almost closes.
