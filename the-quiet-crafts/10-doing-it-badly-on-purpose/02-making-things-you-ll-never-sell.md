# Making things you'll never sell

*A lopsided bowl that holds nothing — and why that might be the whole point.*

## The bowl on the windowsill

There's a small clay bowl on my windowsill. Lopsided. The glaze pooled thick on one side and ran off the edge in a way that technically counts as a flaw. It doesn't hold anything. It never will. Water would seep through the hairline crack along the bottom.

Nobody would buy it. Nobody should. And that is not a problem with the bowl.

It sits there in the afternoon light. It's not for sale. It's not going anywhere. Every time I walk past it, something in me settles — not because the bowl is beautiful, but because it asks nothing of me. It just exists.

## What are you going to do with it

When you make something with your hands, someone will eventually ask that question. What are you going to do with it. And it's a kind question, usually. But underneath it sits an assumption so quiet you might not notice it: that the thing needs somewhere to go.

Think about it. Almost everything in your house arrived with a price on it. Someone made it to be sold. Someone shipped it, someone shelved it, someone bought it. We are so surrounded by objects that passed through the marketplace that a thing made for no reason at all is genuinely hard to look at. We don't have a category for it.

So we invent destinations. We say it's practice. We say maybe someday I'll give it as a gift. We say it's a learning piece. We give the object a job it never asked for, because that feels safer than saying the simpler, stranger thing: I made it because I wanted to.

## A receipt, not a product

Here's what I've come to. The making already happened. The two hours at the table, the hands learning the shape, the quiet of working slowly — that's already spent. Already had. Already yours.

The object isn't the point. It's more like a receipt. Proof that you sat down. Proof that your hands did something slow while the rest of the world moved fast.

And a receipt doesn't have to be beautiful. Nobody frames a receipt. Nobody worries about whether it's even on both sides.

Which means the bowl is free. It doesn't have to be symmetrical. It doesn't have to please anyone. It only has to exist.

Something else happens, too. The moment a thing could be sold, you start looking at it the way a stranger would — hunting for flaws, measuring weight, checking the joinery. Make it for no one, and that eye quietly closes. You see it the way you see a stone on a walk. It's just there. It's fine.

## Where they go

There's a drawer in my kitchen full of these. Wobbly spoons. A sock with a heel that came out wrong. Half a scarf I unravelled and knit again, twice. A small wooden box with a lid that doesn't quite close.

Some of them I give away. When you hand someone a thing with no price, they can't measure it against anything. There's no comparison shopping in your head. They just hold it. Sometimes they don't know what to say, and that's okay. The gift isn't the object. The gift is that someone spent slow hours making something for no reason and then thought of you.

And when one breaks, I'm not out anything. The hours were the point, and I already had those hours. I just make another one. Slightly less crooked, maybe. Or more crooked. It doesn't matter.

## Holding nothing at all

You're allowed to make something with no future. No buyer, no purpose, no use to anyone, including you. You're allowed to spend real hours on it — an evening, a weekend, a season of small attempts.

Make the useless bowl. Put it where the light hits it. Let it sit there, holding nothing at all.

Nothing to defend. Nothing to justify. Just a thing that exists because your hands were moving one quiet evening, and that was enough.
