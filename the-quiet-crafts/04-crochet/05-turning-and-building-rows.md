# Turning, and building rows

*A narrow strip of fabric that didn't exist an hour ago — and the simple act of flipping it over to keep going.*

## The end of the row

You come to the end of a row. The hook has run out of stitches to work into. In your hand is a narrow strip of fabric that didn't exist an hour ago. And now there's nowhere left to go.

So you turn around, and you go back the way you came.

That's it. That's the whole of it.

There's something almost funny about it. You've spent all this time moving in one direction, building something stitch by stitch, and the natural thing — the only thing — is to flip the whole thing over and retrace your path. Not because you made a mistake. Not because you need to fix anything. Just because that's how the fabric grows. You work into what you already made.

## Changing direction

You've been moving in one direction, stitch after stitch, until the row ended. Turning just means flipping the piece over — like turning a page — so the row you finished becomes the foundation for the next one.

Most people chain one before they start the new row, to lift the hook up to the height of the stitches. A small step up. Without it, the edge pulls tight, like a sentence that forgot to take a breath. With it, the fabric climbs evenly, one row resting on the last.

The row doesn't end. It turns.

And that's the odd, lovely thing about crochet. You never really stop. You change direction and keep going, into the thing you already made. The past row becomes the ground for the next one. You're always building on what came before, even when you're moving backward.

## Edges

Your edges will not be straight. Not for a while.

They'll pull in, or creep out, and you'll hold the thing up to the light and know something's off without knowing what. Usually it's the last stitch of the row. Easy to miss. It sits tight against the turning chain and hides there, camouflaged, easy to pass by. Miss it once a row, and your rectangle quietly becomes a triangle.

You can count. Ten stitches, then ten again. Or you can let it wander, and call the wandering part of the record — a log of the evenings you were tired, the afternoons the phone kept buzzing, the stretches where your mind was far away.

Both are fine. Nobody is grading the edges.

This is worth saying again, because it's easy to forget: the edges are yours. They don't have to be perfect to be real. A wavy edge is still an edge. A fabric that narrows is still fabric. The imperfection doesn't undo the making.

## Row after row

After a few dozen rows, the turn stops being something you decide to do.

Flip. Chain. In. Your hands handle it while your mind is somewhere else entirely. That's when the quiet shows up — not because you forced it, but because your body found a rhythm and your attention was free to drift.

Rows are honest. Each one takes exactly as long as it takes. You can't do three at once. You can't skip ahead. The only way through a row is along it, stitch by stitch, to the end. There's no shortcut, and something about that is a relief. The row asks for your presence but not your performance. It just asks you to be here, one stitch at a time.

So the strip becomes a rectangle. The rectangle grows past your knees. Some evenings you get four rows. Some evenings you get one. The fabric doesn't mind either way. It doesn't know about your schedule.

## Permission

It doesn't have to become anything.

It can be a rectangle. It can live in a drawer. It can be the thing you pull out on a hard evening, not to finish, just to add a few rows and put down again.

Turn. Work back. Turn again. An hour of that, and your shoulders sit lower than they did, and you couldn't quite say why. Something settled. Something that was clenched let go, not because you told it to, but because your hands were busy with something that didn't demand anything from you.

You are allowed to make rows that go nowhere. You are allowed to just keep turning.

The point was never the rectangle.
