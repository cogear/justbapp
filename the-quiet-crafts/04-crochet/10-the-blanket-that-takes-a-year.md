# The blanket that takes a year

*A big thing made of very small motions — and why that's enough.*

## A year is a strange thing

A year is a long time to spend on a blanket. It's also nothing at all. The year passes either way, whether or not something is growing in a basket beside your chair.

There's a blanket in someone's house right now that isn't finished. It was started in spring. It's now well past spring. And it's fine.

At some point, you may want to make something big. Something slow. Something out of all proportion to any good reason for making it. A blanket is a good candidate. So is anything that asks only for repetition and your hands.

## Late for nothing

We're used to projects with edges. A start, a finish, a due date. You complete the thing, and then you're permitted to feel something about having completed it.

So the half-done blanket becomes an accusation. You walk past it. It's still there. Still unfinished. As if yarn could fall behind schedule.

That's what makes people stop at row forty. Not boredom. Not difficulty. The quiet shame of being late for something nobody assigned.

You started it. Nobody asked you to. There is no deadline. There is no client. There is no manager waiting for a status update on your wool. And yet somehow the unfinished thing starts to feel like a task you're neglecting, a promise you're breaking — when the only person involved is you, and you never set a date.

## A companion, not a deadline

A blanket that takes a year isn't a deadline you're failing. It's a companion. It sits in its basket and asks nothing except your hands, when you happen to have them free.

You don't work on it. You visit it. Some evenings for an hour, some for the length of one song. Some weeks not at all.

It will be done when it's done. That isn't laziness or avoidance. It's the honest arithmetic of a big thing made of very small motions. A blanket is thousands of stitches. You can't rush thousands of stitches. You can only do them, one at a time, or not.

And a year of small motions adds up to something you could never have made in a weekend, no matter how much you wanted to. The weekend version would be tight and tense and done out of obligation. The year-long version has had time to breathe. So have you.

## What a year actually looks like

Here's what a year really looks like, if you let it.

In October you're quick and interested. In December you don't touch the blanket once — there are other things, and the basket gets pushed to the back of the cupboard. In February you find it again, and the yarn smells faintly of cedar and dust. You pick it up and your hands remember what to do before your mind does.

You'll see your own hands change in the work. The first rows a little tight, a little anxious. The later ones loose and even and unhurried. You can read the blanket like a journal — here's where you were worried, here's where you relaxed, here's where you finally stopped pulling so hard.

You'll run out of a colour and buy more and it won't quite match. Leave it. That subtle line is a date stamped into the fabric. That's where last summer went. That's the row you were making when the season turned.

Some of it you'll do with people in the room, half listening to a conversation. Some of it in silence, late, when the house has gone quiet. The blanket doesn't distinguish. It only gets longer. It holds all of it equally — the sociable evenings and the lonely ones and the ones where you were barely paying attention.

## No ceremony

And one ordinary night, with no ceremony at all, you'll finish a row and find there's nothing left to add.

It won't be a perfect blanket. It'll be heavy and a little crooked and warm. It holds a year of quiet evenings, and nobody but you will ever really know that.

You're allowed to make something that takes longer than a season. You're allowed to put it down for months without guilt. You're allowed to finish it, or not. The point was never the blanket. The point was the year — and the quiet thing your hands were doing while it passed.
