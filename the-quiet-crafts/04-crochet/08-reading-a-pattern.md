# Reading a pattern

*a pattern is just somebody's hands remembering what they did*

## the page

The first time you look at a crochet pattern, it doesn't look like language. It looks like a code someone forgot to translate. Letters with no vowels. Numbers in brackets. Asterisks scattered through the text like small warnings.

And there you are, holding a hook, knowing how to make a stitch — and somehow this one piece of paper has made you feel like a beginner all over again.

That feeling is worth pausing on. You learned to crochet. Your hands figured out the rhythm of it. You made something. Maybe several somethings. And now a single sheet of abbreviations has quietly suggested that none of that counts. That the real skill is somewhere on the other side of this page, and you haven't earned it yet.

That's not what the page is doing. But it's what it feels like, and that matters.

## it feels like a test

Most of us don't try. We glance at the page, decide it belongs to someone more serious than us, and go back to making the thing our hands already know how to make.

Which is fine. Truly. There is no obligation to read a pattern, ever. If you enjoy making the same square in the same colour for the rest of your life, that is a complete and dignified way to spend your evenings.

But it does mean a whole shelf of quiet afternoons stays closed to you, and there's no good reason for that.

The page isn't an exam. Nobody grades it. It's closer to a recipe card written in a hurry, in handwriting you simply haven't got used to yet. The first time you saw a friend's handwriting, it was hard to read. After a few letters, you stopped noticing.

Patterns are like that.

## somebody's hands

Because that's all a pattern is. Someone sat down with yarn, made a thing, and wrote down what their hands did — as briefly as they could — so that yours could do it too.

That's the whole story. There's no guild. No certification. No ancestor of crochet who invented the abbreviations and guards them. A person made a jumper, wrote down how, and shortened the words because doing the work was more interesting than writing about it.

sc is single crochet. ch is chain. Repeat what sits between the asterisks. Parentheses group the moves that happen together. The number at the end of a row says how many stitches you should have — a checkpoint, not a verdict.

That's nearly all of it. The rest is shorthand for repetition. And repetition is the part your hands were already good at.

You could learn most of it in the time it takes a cup of tea to go cold. The strange part is how long we avoid starting.

## one line at a time

So read one line. Only one. Work that line. Then read the next.

The whole page at once will flatten anybody. One line at a time almost never does. This is true of patterns and true of most things, honestly. We see the whole shape of a task and feel small against it. We look at one row and feel fine.

Put a sticky note under the row you're on and slide it down as you go. Tick the rows in pencil. Nobody is watching you keep your place.

Say the line out loud if it helps. It sounds ridiculous in an empty room. It also works — the words reach your hands faster when you've heard them. Your mouth and your fingers seem to coordinate more honestly when they go through the ear.

And when you lose your place — and you will lose your place — you count. Counting isn't a setback. It's just part of reading. Every reader loses their place. Every reader finds it again.

## permission

Some patterns are written badly. That isn't you failing to read. Put it down without ceremony and find another one. A poorly written pattern is not a test of your worth. It's a person who didn't write clearly.

And one day you'll understand a pattern well enough to disagree with it. To make it longer. To leave the flower off the corner. To use a heavier yarn because you want a sturdier thing. That's when you've really read it — not when you followed it perfectly, but when you heard it as a voice rather than an order.

The page is a voice, not an instruction from above. You can follow it. You can argue with it. You can close it and make whatever your hands were leaning toward anyway.

The pattern opens a door. It doesn't lock one.
