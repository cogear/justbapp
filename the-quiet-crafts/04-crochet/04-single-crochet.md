# Single crochet

*A chain, a hook, four movements, and a particular kind of quiet.*

## Four Movements

You have a chain. A row of little loops lying there, like a piece of string that learned to be orderly. Something you made and don't yet have a use for. That's fine. The use was never the point.

Now we put a stitch into it.

Single crochet is four movements. That's the whole thing. Four movements, over and over, for as long as you like. There are fancier stitches — stitches that fan out, that puff up, that make little shells and clusters. You'll meet them eventually, or you won't. Single crochet doesn't need them. It is the plainest stitch there is. Not the prettiest. Not the one that gets noticed.

It shows up in almost everything, and nobody notices it, because it's busy holding the rest together. That's a fine thing to be.

## In, Over, Through

In. The hook goes into the loop. Not through the whole thing — under the top strand, so the hook sits in the middle of the stitch like a finger through a ring. You're not piercing the yarn. You're finding the space that's already there.

Over. The yarn lays itself across the hook. Then back — you pull it through, and now there are two loops sitting on your hook, waiting. They look patient, those two loops. They are.

Over again. And through both. The two loops become one. That's a stitch. That's all a stitch has ever been.

Four movements, and there's one stitch. Then you find the next loop along, and you do the same four things. Then again. The repetition isn't something to endure on the way to the real thing. The repetition *is* the real thing.

## The First Ones Are Bad

Your first ones will be bad. Some too tight to get the hook back into. Some so loose they gape like a yawn. The edges will wander. Your tension — that word crocheters use for how tight or loose the yarn flows — will be all over the place, because your hands haven't learned the yarn yet, and the yarn hasn't learned your hands.

This is not information about you.

The hook will slip out. You'll lose count of your loops and have to look at your own hand like it belongs to a stranger. You'll make a stitch and immediately forget whether you made it. Everyone does. Every single person who has ever learned this, in all the years people have been pulling yarn through loops, has sat exactly where you are sitting.

So pull it out. The yarn doesn't remember. Wind it back onto the ball and start again. You have plenty of material and nothing is due. There's no version of this where you skip the clumsy part. The clumsy part is where the learning happens. And it's oddly pleasant, once you notice that nothing depends on it.

Nobody is grading you. Nobody is waiting for this object. The only deadline is the one you might accidentally impose, and you can put that down whenever you're ready.

## When the Hands Take Over

Somewhere around the fortieth stitch — or the sixtieth, or the hundredth, it varies — something shifts. You stop saying the words in your head. *In, over, through, through.* The chant fades. The hands just go, and you're along for it.

This is the part nobody can teach you, because it isn't knowledge. It's the body taking the job over from the mind. Your fingers find the loop before your eyes do. The hook turns at the right angle without being told. And when the mind is off duty, it gets quiet in there.

Not empty. Quiet. The way a room is quiet when someone stops talking.

The sound is small. A soft tick as the hook turns. The yarn drawing over your finger, a faint whisper of fiber against skin. You can hear rain through it. You can hear someone moving in the next room. You can hear the particular sound of your own breathing, which you usually can't, because there's too much else going on.

This is what people are looking for when they say they want to slow down. Not the absence of activity. The presence of a different kind.

## A Small Crooked Patch

What you'll have, after a while, is a small crooked patch of fabric. Uneven. Faintly trapezoidal, wider at one end than the other, because your hands were figuring it out as they went. It is not for anything. It won't become a scarf or a blanket or a gift. It is a small crooked patch of fabric, and that is what it is.

Keep it anyway.

Not as proof of progress. Not because you'll need it later. Just because your hands made it, and it exists now, and yesterday it didn't. That's enough of a reason. More than enough.

Four movements. In, over, through, and through. You can stop whenever you like, and pick it up again whenever you like. Nothing is waiting on you. The yarn will be exactly where you left it, patient as ever, ready for the next loop.
