# The chain

*A single loop pulled through another, repeated until the thinking stops.*

## A row of loops

The first thing you make in crochet is a chain.

It isn't a hat. It isn't a scarf. It isn't a dishcloth or a blanket or a gift for anyone. It's a row of loops, each one pulled through the one before it. That's all.

Nobody hangs a chain on the wall. Nobody keeps one in a drawer or photographs it for friends. And still, almost every crocheted thing that has ever existed started exactly here — one loop, then another, then another. The chain is the spine of the whole craft. Every stitch you'll ever learn sits on top of one.

So it's worth staying here a while. Not to get it right. There isn't much to get right. Just to let your hands and this hook get acquainted. The hook is a simple tool — a stick with a curve at the end — and your hands have never held one before. Give them time.

## Yarn over, pull through

You already have a loop sitting on your hook. Maybe you made a slip knot for it, or maybe someone just handed you the hook with a loop already on. Either way, that's your starting place. Everything from here is one small motion, repeated, until you decide to stop.

Point the hook at the yarn. Catch it. Pull it back through the loop that's already there. Now there's a new loop on the hook, and one chain hanging below it.

That's it. That's the whole thing. Yarn over, pull through. Yarn over, pull through.

Say it out loud the first few times if it helps. Your hands are listening — they learn from rhythm as much as from words. The chant gives them something to follow until they don't need it anymore.

Some of the loops will slide off the hook. Some will be too tight to get the hook back into. Both are normal. Pull it out and start again. The yarn doesn't mind. It has no memory of its own mistakes, and it isn't keeping score of yours.

## The hand finds its measure

The first dozen will be clumsy. The hook will snag on the yarn. The loops will come out uneven — a few pulled tight as knots, a few loose and gaping like open mouths.

That's tension. It isn't a flaw in you. It isn't something you're doing wrong. It's only how much yarn your left hand happens to be holding back, and it settles on its own, without instruction. You don't fix tension by concentrating harder. You fix it by making more chain.

Somewhere in the second hour — maybe sooner, maybe later — your fingers quietly agree on a measure and keep it. You won't notice the moment it happens. You'll just look down at some point and see that the last twenty loops all look the same. Your hands figured it out without consulting you.

This is the way of it. The body learns what the body does, and the mind is not always invited to the meeting.

## Longer than it needs to be

Look at what you've made. It lies down like a little braid — a row of small V shapes, all facing the same way. Once you can see the V's, you can count them. Counting matters later, when the chain becomes a foundation for something. Tonight it doesn't.

Make one much longer than anything could need. Twenty loops. A hundred. Keep going past the point where you're watching your hands.

Somewhere in there the room comes back. You hear the radiator. You notice you've stopped narrating to yourself — stopped checking, stopped correcting. The work has moved from your head down into your fingers. They know the motion now. They're doing it on their own, and your mind has drifted somewhere quieter.

That's the thing the chain is really for. Not the object. The stillness on the way to it.

## Let it come undone

Then, when you're done, pull the loose end. The whole thing comes undone in about two seconds. The chain unravels in a soft rush and the yarn curls back into itself, a little wavy from the experience but unharmed. Nothing is lost. You still know how.

A chain doesn't have to become anything. Most of what your hands learn, they learn on things nobody will ever keep. The practice is the point — the repetition, the patience, the particular quiet that comes from work your body knows how to do.

So make one tonight. Make it far too long. Then let the yarn go back to being yarn, and let that be enough.
