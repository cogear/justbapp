# Working in the round

*There's a different way to make things — one that never reaches an edge.*

## No Edge

Everything you've made so far had an edge. You worked to the end of the row, stopped, turned, and came back the other way. Back and forth. There's a structure to that — a sense of progress, of moving across a field and reaching the other side.

There's another way to work. You start from a ring about the size of a pea, and you go around it. Then around again. No edge to reach. Nothing to turn at.

It sounds like a small difference. In your hands it isn't. Rows have a rhythm of stopping and starting. Rounds just have a rhythm.

There's something about the absence of a turning point that changes the feel of the work. You stop anticipating the end of a row. You stop bracing for the turn. The hook keeps going, the yarn keeps feeding through, and the circle in your hands grows so slowly you might not notice until you look down and it's bigger than it was.

## Starting From Almost Nothing

Put stitches into that ring — six of them, crowded together like seats around a very small table. Then pull the tail. The hole closes. Six stitches holding hands in a circle, and the beginning disappears into itself.

If the two-finger ring feels fiddly — and it will, at first — chain four and slip the last stitch into the first. Same idea. A small closed loop to build out from.

This is the part people find strange. There's no back and forth. The work only ever goes one direction. Your hands are doing something they recognize — the same motions, the same tension, the familiar pull of yarn through loops — but the shape doesn't behave like a row. It curves. It closes. It turns back on itself before you've gotten anywhere at all.

## Adding

A circle has to grow, or it stays the size of a pea. So you add. Two stitches where there was one. Six becomes twelve. Twelve becomes eighteen. The numbers are simple, almost silly.

You'll feel it if you get it wrong. Add too few and the edges curl up into a shallow bowl. Add too many and the rim ruffles like a lettuce leaf.

Neither is a mistake. A bowl is a fine thing to make. So is a ruffle. You're learning what the numbers do to the fabric, and hands learn that faster than heads do. You can understand the math perfectly and still be surprised by what your fingers show you. That's the kind of knowing that comes from making — the kind that lives in your hands and stays there.

## Losing Your Place

The trouble with a spiral is that you lose your place. There's no seam, no obvious start. You come around and everything looks the same. The stitch you just made looks like the one next to it, and the one next to that.

So you mark it. A scrap of different-coloured yarn tucked into the first stitch of each round, moved up as you pass. A small crumb dropped so you can find your way back. It's not cheating. It's just a kindness to yourself — a way of saying, *you were here.*

And then the counting fades. Hook in, wrap, pull through. The disc turning slowly in your left hand like a wheel with nowhere to be.

This is the part nobody warns you about. How quickly an evening goes when your hands are busy and your head is allowed to be quiet. Not empty — quiet. The thoughts don't stop, but they soften. They move to the background, like music in another room. Your attention settles into the stitch, and the stitch after that, and the round after that.

## A Flat Round Thing

What you'll have, after a while, is a flat round thing. A coaster, if you want to call it that. A small mat for a cup that isn't there yet.

It doesn't have to become a hat. It doesn't have to become anything. It was a way to spend an evening going around and around, which turns out to be a good way to spend an evening.

No edges. Nowhere to turn back. Just the next stitch, and then the one after it. The thing about a circle is that it doesn't go anywhere — it just gets bigger, or it doesn't. There's something restful in that. Making something that isn't trying to reach the other side of anything.
