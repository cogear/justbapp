# Amigurumi, briefly

*A small stuffed thing, made from one long string, and why it ends up on every shelf.*

## The thing on the shelf

There is a small crocheted animal sitting on a shelf in most homes I visit. Slightly lopsided. Made by somebody's hands. Nobody can quite explain why they've kept it for eleven years. It's not beautiful in any obvious way. It doesn't match the decor. It's just there, being a little grey elephant or a cat with one ear higher than the other, and nobody touches it and nobody throws it away.

This is amigurumi. Small stuffed things, made out of one long string.

Mostly animals. Sometimes a strawberry with a face. Sometimes a dumpling with arms. It's an entire world of tiny sculpted creatures that exist for no practical reason, and we're only stepping into it for a few minutes — long enough to know it's there, long enough to maybe want to make one.

## Adding and subtracting

Amigurumi is made of spheres. Or things close enough to spheres. A head. A body. Four short stubby legs. A tail. You'd expect that to be complicated, and it is, in the way that simple things are — which is to say, not very.

It's mostly adding and subtracting, one stitch at a time.

Put a few extra stitches into a round and the fabric bulges outward. Leave a few out and it pulls back in. That's the entire geometry. Increase, work even, decrease. Your hands make a ball without ever being told what a ball is. The pattern says "six single crochets in a ring," and then "two in each stitch," and your fingers just follow along, and somewhere around round four the work stops lying flat in your lap and starts to cup itself, like a small bowl deciding to become closed.

You can feel it happening. The yarn has its own opinion about what shape it wants to take, and your job is mostly to not get in the way.

## Worked tight

Here's something that feels wrong at first: amigurumi is worked tight. Tighter than feels natural. You use a hook a size or two smaller than the yarn calls for, so the stuffing stays hidden inside and doesn't peek through the gaps. Loose crochet makes lovely drapey fabric and a leaky animal. You want stiff, dense, almost sculptural. The kind of fabric that holds its own weight.

Your hand will complain a little at first, gripping a hook that small, working that firmly. Set it down when it does. Shake your fingers out. There's no hurry inside this. Nobody is timing you.

Round after round of the same stitch, the same little circle turning in your fingers. The counting keeps the front of your mind busy and leaves the rest of you alone. That's the real gift of it, honestly — the counting. Your hands know what to do, the numbers give the thinking part of you something simple to hold, and the quiet fills in around the edges.

And for a long while it looks like nothing at all. A grey rumpled sack. A shapeless lump. You keep going because the pattern says to keep going. Then you press the stuffing in, and it sits up, and it's a rabbit. That turn never stops being funny. Every single time.

## It becomes somebody

The eyes are where it becomes somebody.

Two stitches, or two safety eyes, pushed through the fabric and fastened on. Set them a hair too close together and you get intensity. A hair too far apart and you get surprise. Slightly uneven and you get a personality you didn't plan for. Shy. Startled. Faintly disapproving. Looking at you with one eye while the other one drifts toward the window.

This is the part people apologize for. The crooked ear. The leg that came out fatter than the other one. The smile that leans. Nobody here is asking you to apologize. That's not what this is for.

The crooked ones are the ones people keep. I don't know why. They just are. The perfect ones are impressive, and then they sit in a drawer. The strange ones sit on the shelf for eleven years, and nobody can explain it, and that's fine.

## Exactly as useful as it is

A crocheted octopus is useless. Genuinely useless. It holds nothing, feeds no one, hangs on no wall, wins no awards. It sits there being an octopus you made out of string. That is its entire purpose, and it is sufficient.

And it takes an evening. Maybe two. Which means you can hold the whole finished thing in your hands before you go to bed. Start to finish, in one sitting of your life. Not many things work that way. Most things we do are ongoing, unresolved, waiting for something else to happen before they count. This counts. Tonight. In your lap.

So make the strange little creature. Give it away, or don't. Put it on a shelf and let it be exactly as useful as it is.
