# Keeping a thing for thirty years

*A wooden spoon, a wrong screw, a mismatched patch of wool — and what it means to stay with an object long enough for it to remember you.*

## Older than some friendships

There's a wooden spoon in my kitchen drawer that is older than some of my friendships. The handle has gone pale where my hand sits. The bowl is worn lopsided from stirring the same direction for years.

I didn't set out to keep it for thirty years. I just never once had a reason to throw it away.

Thirty years is not a long time for an object. It's a long time for a person to keep paying attention to one.

Most objects outlast us easily. A brick will be a brick for centuries. What's rare is the relationship — a person choosing, again and again, not to move on.

## Things that expect to leave

Most of what we own now arrives with an expiry date nobody prints on the box. Two years for the phone. Five for the kettle. A blender you can't open without destroying it.

So we stop expecting to keep things. We buy already knowing we'll replace. And something quiet goes missing — the long acquaintance. The way an object slowly learns your hands.

It's possible to go a whole life without owning a single thing that remembers you. Everything new. Everything smooth. Nothing that shows where you've been.

There's nothing wrong with new things. But when everything around you is new, you lose a particular kind of belonging — the kind that only develops through repetition and time. The way a chair creaks differently after you've sat in it for years. The way a jacket creases at the elbows in exactly your shape.

## Accumulation

A thing kept this long stops being something you use and becomes something closer to a record. Every repair is a date. Every mark is a day you'd otherwise have forgotten entirely.

The chip on the handle from the summer you moved. The screw you replaced with the wrong screw and never went back to fix. The wool darned in a colour you couldn't quite match.

None of this is damage, exactly. It's accumulation. The thing gets more particular each year. More clearly yours. Less and less like anything you could walk into a shop and buy.

This is what repair gives you that replacement never can. A mended thing carries its history visibly. A replaced thing starts blank. The scar on the wood, the odd-coloured stitch, the slightly crooked hinge — these are not flaws to hide. They are the object becoming specific. Becoming yours in a way no one else can replicate.

A thing you've kept that long doesn't only belong to you. It holds the record. It remembers you back.

## One mending at a time

You don't keep a thing for thirty years by deciding to. It happens one small mending at a time. You fix it this week. That's all. Then you fix it again in four years.

It helps to own things that can be opened. Wood, metal, leather, cloth. Screws instead of glue. A shape simple enough that you can see how it's held together.

Objects designed to be sealed teach us that repair is someone else's job. Objects designed to be opened invite us back in. The hinge you can unscrew, the seam you can restitch, the surface you can sand — these are small permissions to return.

And it helps not to upgrade. There is nearly always a better version available. There is almost never a reason.

There will come a point where mending costs more than replacing. Do it anyway, if you can. That's the whole hinge of it — choosing to stay with the thing.

## What time feels like

The strange part is what this does to time. You can't feel thirty years pass. But you can hold a handle worn smooth by your own grip and understand it.

The spoon is nothing. It cost less than a coffee. It's cracked near the neck and I'll probably glue it this winter, badly, like last time.

But it's been in every kitchen I've ever cooked in. That isn't sentiment. That's just what happens when you don't throw something away.

You don't have to feel anything grand about it. You just keep using it. You fix it when it breaks. You put it back in the drawer. And one day you notice the handle has changed colour where your hand rests, and you realize the spoon has been quietly keeping count of the years all along.
