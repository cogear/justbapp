# Sharpening

*A dull edge doesn't announce itself — and neither does the quiet that comes from fixing it.*

## The Knife You Stopped Noticing

There's a knife in your kitchen that has been getting duller for years. So slowly you never noticed. You just started pressing harder, and harder, and called it normal.

A dull edge doesn't announce itself. It just makes everything slightly more work. The tomato crushes instead of slicing. And you blame the tomato.

Most of us throw it out eventually. Buy a new one. It never occurs to us that nothing is broken. The edge is just tired.

This is how a lot of things go. Not just knives. Something drifts, slowly, and we adapt around it. We press harder. We call the difficulty normal. And then one day we decide the thing itself is the problem, when really it just needs attention.

## Taking Away, Not Adding

Sharpening is a strange kind of repair, because you don't add anything. You take metal away. A whisker of it, less than you can see, until the two sides of the edge meet again in a line.

There's something worth noticing in that. We're used to repair meaning replacement — new parts, new tools, new purchases. But sharpening asks for less, not more. You remove what's in the way. That's the whole job.

A stone. A little water. Ten or fifteen minutes at the kitchen table. That's the whole apparatus. There's no upgrade path here, nothing else to buy. Nothing thrown out. An edge, returned to itself.

The stone needs soaking first. You put it in a bowl and watch the bubbles come up out of it. And then you wait, because it isn't ready yet. Even the tool needs patience before the work begins.

## The Same Small Motion

The hard part is the angle. The same shallow angle, pass after pass, while your arm quietly wants to drift. Early on, it will drift. That's fine. Everyone's does.

The sound tells you before your eyes do. A dry scrape means you've lost it. When it's right there's a soft, even hiss, and grey slurry gathers under the steel.

After a while you stop counting passes. Your shoulder takes over. Fifteen minutes go by and you have not thought about anything — just water, stone, steel, and one small motion, repeated.

This is the part that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't done it. The repetition isn't boring. It's the point. Your hands are doing something they understand, something older than language, and your mind gets to go quiet. Not blank. Just quiet. The way a room gets quiet when you stop making noise in it.

There's no ambition in it. Nobody is watching. No one will ever ask how sharp your knife is. You get good at it anyway, quietly, for no reason at all.

## The Burr

You check with your thumb, across the edge, never along it. There's a faint catch when it's ready. A burr — a curl of metal so small it's more a feeling than a thing.

Sharp is something you feel before you can see it. Nobody can hand you that. Your hands learn it themselves, one blade at a time.

Then the last passes, lighter and lighter, almost polite. You move to the finer stone. And at the end, a sheet of paper falls open at the touch of it.

## Reason Enough

None of this makes you more productive. It just means dinner is quieter. Less pressing down, less force, less fighting. The work stops arguing with you.

And you kept something. A knife somebody in your family used for years and years, still working, because every few months a person sits down with a stone. That's not thrift. It's a kind of loyalty.

You don't need a better reason than that. You can sharpen the knife because you like the sound it makes. That's reason enough.

The world will tell you that making things with your hands needs a purpose — a product, a skill, a market. But some things are worth doing just for the fifteen minutes of quiet they give you. The knife gets sharp. You get still. Nobody needs to know.
