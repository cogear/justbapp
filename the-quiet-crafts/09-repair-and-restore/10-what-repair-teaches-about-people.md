# What repair teaches about people

*A broken chair and a frayed friendship have more in common than you'd think — if you're willing to slow down long enough to notice.*

## Before you fix it

The first thing you do with a broken thing is not fix it.

You look at it. You turn it over under the light. You find where it actually gave way — which is almost never where you thought.

Nobody teaches you this. You learn it by ruining something once. By forcing a piece that wanted to be lifted. By snapping a clip that only needed a little warmth first. By cracking a housing that would have slid off if you'd waited ten seconds and breathed.

After enough of that, you slow down.

And then the slowing down starts following you around. Out of the workshop, into rooms with people in them. Into conversations that feel stuck. Into moments where your first impulse is to push and your body says, wait.

That's the quiet gift of working with your hands. The lessons don't stay on the bench.

## Broken. Done. Next.

Out in the world, we're fast. A thing stops working, we replace it. That's the reflex we were handed. It's cheap, it's easy, and it mostly works — on things.

But a reflex doesn't stay in its lane.

Someone lets us down and there's the same small click somewhere in the chest. *Broken. Done. Next.* The evaluation takes half a second. The verdict arrives before the story does.

I won't pretend that's always the wrong answer. Sometimes it's the only honest one. Some things need to end, and pretending otherwise just prolongs the snapping.

But it's worth noticing how fast it arrives. How little looking it does first. How often the click sounds before we've turned anything over under the light.

## One loose joint

At the bench you learn that most breaks are small.

A whole chair unusable because of one loose joint. A lamp dead because of one tired wire. A drawer that won't slide because of one warped runner. Rarely the whole thing. Almost always one thing.

You learn that the visible break is rarely the whole story, either. Something was working loose for months before it gave. A joint creaked. A wire frayed a thread at a time. The moment it snapped was the last part, not the cause.

And you learn that force is nearly never the tool.

Warmth, patience, the right angle, a bit of waiting. If it won't come, you're pulling the wrong way. If you pull harder, you'll break what you're trying to save.

That one has saved me more conversations than I can count.

## People are not objects

Now the honest part. People are not objects. You can't open somebody up, find the tired wire, and set them back on the shelf working. The metaphor only carries so far, and then it drops you.

Repair doesn't teach you to fix people.

It teaches you how to stay near something that isn't working yet, without needing it resolved by dinner. Or by the end of the week. Or by the end of the year, if you're being honest.

It teaches you that a mended thing isn't a lesser thing. A chair with a reglued joint holds weight the same as it did before. A scarf with darned elbows keeps you warm. But it also carries a history it didn't have before — a place where someone paused, looked closely, and decided this was worth the trouble. You were there for that. The object remembers, even if no one else does.

And it teaches the harder one. Some things do go to the bin. Not everything can be saved. Not everything should be. You carry them there slowly, and you carry them knowing exactly what you tried. That knowing is the difference between discarding and giving up.

## A pace, not a philosophy

So that's what repair gave me.

Not a philosophy. Nothing you could put on a wall or stitch onto a pillow. A pace.

Look first. Ask where it actually gave way. Don't force it. Accept that mending takes longer than replacing, and that the longer way is not wasted time. The longer way is the whole point — it's where the looking happens, where the patience grows, where you learn the difference between something broken and something finished.

Then set the tools down and go be a person among people. A little slower than before. A little more willing to turn things over under the light before deciding what they are.

That's the whole of it. No system, no framework, no five steps. Just a pace you carry with you, learned at a bench, useful everywhere else.
