# Kintsugi: the golden seam

*A cup breaks. You could throw it away, or you could spend three weeks putting it back together.*

A cup slips out of your hand. That sound — bright, and then final. Then the sweeping up, the careful hunt for the small pieces under the table. Most of us have done this. Most of us reach for the bin.

There's a Japanese repair that goes the other way. You keep the pieces. You put them back together. And then you make the break the most visible thing about the bowl.

Kintsugi. Golden joinery. A crack filled, and then lined in gold.

## Mostly glue

Underneath the gold, it's mostly glue. Lacquer from a tree, mixed with rice flour into a putty about the thickness of soft clay. Nothing exotic. Nothing clever. The materials are humble, and so are the tools. A sliver of bamboo. Some tape. A blade for scraping. Sandpaper. A soft cloth for the gold.

You work the putty into the break with the bamboo. Press the pieces together. Bind them with tape while they set. Your hands go sticky and dark, and it doesn't come off with water. There's something honest about that — the way the material leaves its mark on you before you leave your mark on it.

You'll get some of it wrong. A piece seated a fraction proud. A gap you don't notice until it's set hard. You can take it back down and try again, or you can leave it. The bowl doesn't care. It's not waiting for perfection.

## A bowl in a box

Then you wait. Lacquer doesn't dry the way paint dries. It cures — slowly, and only in damp air. So the cup goes into a box with a wet cloth, and the box goes on a shelf.

Days. Sometimes weeks. There is nothing you can do to hurry it. You can't check on it too often. You can't speed it up with heat or fan or willpower. This is most of kintsugi — a bowl in a box, and you, elsewhere, living your life.

It's a strange thing, being in the middle of something you can't touch. You've committed to the repair, and now the repair is happening without you. The work continues in the dark, and your part is simply to not interfere.

When you open it, you scrape back what's proud. Sand the seam flat. Fill the low spots. Close the box again. Three or four rounds of this before any gold comes near it. Each time, the box closes. Each time, you walk away.

## Very little gold

The gold comes last, and there's very little of it. A thin line of fresh lacquer along the seam, then powder dusted on with something soft, barely touching. A fingertip of velvet. That's all.

You burnish it with your own finger. The warmth of your skin brings a dull shine up from the powder. And there it is — the break, still a break, now the brightest thing on the cup.

It won't be a perfect line. Yours will be thick in one place and thin in another. It will be exactly as steady as your hands were that day. Maybe you were tired. Maybe the light was bad. Maybe you were thinking about something else. The seam records all of it. That isn't a flaw in the method. That's the method.

## Before it means anything

People like to make this mean something about you. Your cracks, your scars, your beautiful damage. It's a lovely thing to say, and it isn't wrong. There's truth in it. But before it means anything about you, it's a job. Glue, sanding, waiting, a brush.

And that's the part I'd keep. Not the lesson. The afternoon. The smell of the lacquer. The tape. The small scrape of the blade against the seam. The particular quiet of doing one thing with your hands while the rest of the world does whatever it's doing.

The philosophy lands differently when your hands have actually been in the putty. When you've sat with the bowl in the box and wondered if you should peek. When you've sanded something almost flat and then sanded it a little too far. The metaphor is nicer from a distance. Up close, it's just work — slow, repetitive, patient work that asks for nothing but your attention.

It holds tea again, if you want it to. Or it sits on a shelf holding nothing at all. Both are fine. You didn't mend it to make it useful.

You mended it because it was broken, and you had hands.
