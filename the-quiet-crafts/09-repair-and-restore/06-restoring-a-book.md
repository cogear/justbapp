# Restoring a book

*A broken book on your shelf isn't a problem to solve. It's an invitation to sit down and make something whole again.*

## The book that won't close

There's probably a book on your shelf that doesn't close properly anymore. The spine is cracked white. Pages have gone loose at the front, and one has come away entirely, sitting at a funny angle like a tooth knocked sideways.

You've been meaning to deal with it for a couple of years. Which usually means throwing it out. Or just not looking at that part of the shelf. It becomes furniture you step around.

There's another option. It takes an evening or two, and nothing about it is difficult. You can put the book back together yourself.

## Same words, better glue

A new copy costs almost nothing. Same words, better glue, delivered by Thursday. This is the sensible answer, and it's why most broken books get quietly replaced. We live in a world where replacement is effortless and repair is the strange choice.

But the new copy isn't the book you read on a train years ago. It doesn't have your pencil in the margin, or the crease where you fell asleep holding it, or the small stain from the cup of tea that went cold beside you. The words are the same. The object isn't. And the object is what broke.

There's something worth noticing here. We talk about books as if they were only their content — just words, transferable between copies. But you don't keep a broken book because the words are rare. You keep it because the object carries something the words alone don't. A history of being held.

## A patient material

You need less than you'd think. Wheat starch paste, cooked thin in a small jar. Japanese tissue, so fine you can read through it. A bone folder. Thread. Maybe a weight — a few heavy books will do.

The paste is the part that teaches you. It goes on wet, it dries slow, and there is nothing you can do to hurry it along. You lay the book under a weight, and you leave the room, and you let it be.

Paper is a patient material. It will take a repair, but only at its own speed. An afternoon isn't enough. A week, sometimes. You learn quickly that rushing doesn't make it dry faster. It just makes it warp.

This is one of the quiet gifts of repair work. It asks you to match the pace of the material, not the other way around. The paste doesn't care about your schedule. The book doesn't care about your schedule. And after a while, you stop caring about your schedule too.

## Forty times through

Books come apart in sections. Little folded booklets, sewn together — that's all a book has ever been. Once you see it, you can't unsee it. Every hardcover on your shelf is a small stack of folded paper held by thread and goodwill.

You pierce the folds with an awl. Then linen thread and a curved needle, and you go through, and back, and through. The stitch is simple. You'll do it forty times.

Somewhere in the middle of that, your hands take over and your head goes quiet. Not empty. Just occupied by something small enough to hold all of you. The repetition becomes the point. Not a stitch toward a finished product, but forty small acts of attention, one after another, each one the same as the last.

Nobody needs you to do this. That's part of why it's restful. There's no deadline on a broken book, and no one checking the stitch. No one will inspect your repair. The only witness is you and the book.

## Back on the shelf

The finished book won't look new. There's a seam down the spine, a shade lighter than the cloth. It's slightly thicker than it was. It closes. That's the whole standard.

Then you put it back on the shelf. Maybe you'll never read it again. That's allowed. You weren't mending it to read it. You mended it because it was in pieces and you had thread.

A thing you mended stays mended in a way a thing you bought never is. Your hands know that book now. The small ridge of paste under the tissue. The tension of the thread. The book on the shelf is no longer just something you own. It's something you've been inside of, in a quiet way, with your hands.

That's what repair gives you. Not a perfect object, but a relationship with one.
