# Fixing a bicycle

*A small noise turns into a quiet practice of listening, slowing down, and learning a machine with your hands.*

## The tick

Somewhere on the way home, your bicycle starts making a noise. A small tick. Once every turn of the pedals, so quiet you're not sure you heard it.

It doesn't get worse. It doesn't go away. You ride with it for a month, and every single day it says the same small thing.

It's a small thing to carry. But you notice you've started riding differently. Bracing a little. Waiting for the tick instead of just riding. The noise has become part of the trip, and not in a good way. Your body has learned to expect it.

## Somebody else's job

Most of us do one of two things. We take it somewhere. Or we ignore it until something actually stops working, and then we take it somewhere.

Not out of laziness. We've been trained to think this way. Machines have gotten quiet about themselves. Sealed panels, hidden screws, the polite suggestion that whatever's inside is not your business. Phones you can't open. Cars you can't peer into. Appliances with no user-serviceable parts, as the label says.

So we assume a bicycle is one of those. That there's a body of knowledge we missed somewhere, and it's too late to catch up. A guild we weren't invited into.

It isn't like that.

## Nothing is hidden

Turn it upside down on the kitchen floor and the whole machine is right there. Chain, cog, cable, pad, rim. Nothing behind a cover. Nothing you can't reach with your hands.

Turn the crank slower than feels useful, and put your ear near it. Somewhere in that circle, the tick happens. Do it enough times and you'll know exactly where.

It isn't cleverness. It's patience, mostly. The bike is telling you. You only have to go slowly enough to hear it.

There's a particular calm in a machine simple enough to understand all of. Everything does one job. Everything connects to the next thing. You can follow it with a finger. The pedal turns the crank, the crank turns the chain, the chain turns the cog, the cog turns the wheel. No mysteries. Just a circle you can see the whole of.

This is rare now. Most of the objects in our lives are opaque to us. A bicycle is not.

## Rag, water, grease

The work itself is small and repetitive. A rag folded around the chain, pulled through link by link. The rag turns black. The chain turns quiet. There's something meditative about it — the same motion, over and over, until the thing you're holding tells you it's done.

An inner tube held under water in a washing-up bowl, section by section, until a thin thread of bubbles gives away the hole. That moment is genuinely satisfying. A tiny trail of air rising from one spot in the rubber. You found it. It was there the whole time.

Grease settles into the creases of your knuckles and stays there a day or two. Your hands look like they did something. They did.

You'll use the wrong tool at first. You'll tighten something too much, or not enough. This is how everyone learns a machine. By feel, slowly, with a few mistakes that don't matter much. Nobody is watching. Nobody is grading you. The bike doesn't care that you used a butter knife. It only cares that the bolt is tight.

## Silent

Then you turn it right side up and spin the wheel and listen, and there's nothing. Just the soft whirr of a thing running the way it was built to run.

You ride it the next morning and it's silent. No one will ever know. There's no photograph of this. Nothing to show anybody. Nothing to sell, nothing to post, nothing to prove.

And a bicycle you fixed feels different underneath you. Not better, exactly. Known. You know what that ticking was, and where it lived, and how it stopped. You know the chain and the cog and the cable because your fingers were there. The machine has become a little less separate from you.

That's the whole reward. It's enough.

The quiet that comes from this isn't dramatic. It's the quiet of a small thing resolved by your own hands, in your own kitchen, on an ordinary evening. And then carried, silently, into the morning.
