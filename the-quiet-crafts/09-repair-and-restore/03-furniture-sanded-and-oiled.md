# Furniture, sanded and oiled

*A dull chair, a Saturday, and the kind of quiet that only comes from work your hands already know.*

## Something gone dull

There's a piece of wood in your house that has gone dull. You know the one. A chair, maybe a small table, the arm of something you rest your hand on without thinking. Not broken. Just tired.

Water rings from mugs you didn't bother to use a coaster for. A grey film built up where hands have held it for years. You've stopped seeing it, mostly. It's furniture. It holds up cups.

You could leave it exactly as it is. That would be fine. Or you could give it a Saturday, a sheet of sandpaper, and a small can of oil.

This is not a project. Nobody is waiting on it. Nothing is being upgraded. You're just giving a piece of wood some of your afternoon, and seeing what happens.

## Coarse to fine

Start coarse. Eighty grit feels rough as a paving stone under your fingers. Then one-twenty. Then one-eighty. The numbers climb as the paper gets kinder, and you move through them in order.

Wrap the paper round a block so your hand doesn't dig grooves into the surface. Follow the grain. Go across it and the wood will tell on you later, in the light, when you see the scratches you left behind.

Your arm learns the motion in about ten minutes. After that, your mind can go wherever it wants. The hand keeps working without you. This is the part that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't felt it — the way a repetitive physical task frees something in your mind that thinking alone never seems to reach.

There is no fast way to do this. You can press harder, but you'll only leave marks you have to sand out again later. The wood sets the pace, and the pace is slow. That's not an obstacle. That's the whole point.

## Not new

Run your hand across it now and you can feel the difference before you can see it. Bare wood. Pale and slightly chalky, like it's holding its breath.

You're not making it new. New isn't on offer. The mill that cut this board is probably gone, and so is whoever first oiled it. What you're doing is something older and quieter than restoration.

The dents stay. The gouge from something dropped years before you got here. The pale scoop where a thousand hands pushed the chair back from the table. Sand those away and you'd be sanding away the whole reason to bother. The marks are the life of the thing.

Not new. Just cared for. There's a whole different feeling living inside that sentence.

## The wood comes back

Then the oil. This is the part worth waiting for. You pour a little on a rag, wipe it on, and the wood comes back.

The grain darkens and deepens, and suddenly there are rivers in it you never noticed. Lines and curls and small patterns that were there the whole time, just waiting under the grey. Ten seconds of work for something that looks like a small magic trick.

Then you wait. Wipe off whatever didn't soak in. Leave it overnight. Do it again tomorrow, and maybe the day after that. Oil doesn't hurry, and you can't make it.

Lay the rag out flat to dry, never balled up — oily rags can heat themselves as they cure. A small piece of knowledge you'll now carry for the rest of your life, passed along the same way someone probably passed it to the person who taught you.

## Back in the room

Carry it back to the room it lives in. Someone will sit on it without noticing a thing. That is the correct response.

Your hands will smell faintly of linseed for a couple of days. You'll catch the surface in low light and feel something quiet. Something calmer than pride.

Someone might ask what you did with your weekend. Sanded a chair. It will sound like nothing at all. It was not nothing.

Nothing was achieved. A chair that worked fine still works fine. And you spent an afternoon with a piece of wood, doing something your hands turned out to already know.
