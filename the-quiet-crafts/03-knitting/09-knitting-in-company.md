# Knitting in company

*A quiet way to be near someone when talking feels like too much.*

## Two chairs, one lamp

There's a kind of company that doesn't ask much of you. Two people in one room. One of them knitting, the other reading. Nobody saying anything in particular. The lamp on. The evening going by.

From the outside it looks like nothing is happening. No one is entertaining anyone. But I think it's one of the better things a room can hold.

Knitting is a solitary thing, mostly. Quiet work your own hands do. But it does something unusual when you bring it near other people. It changes what being together costs you.

## Facing each other

Most of the ways we're taught to be together involve facing each other. Talking. Filling the gaps. Someone asks how you've been, and you hear yourself giving an answer that's a little bit of a performance.

That's fine sometimes. But it's work. And when you're tired, that work is often the reason you say no. No to the dinner. No to the phone call. No to seeing anybody at all.

So you stay home. And you're not lonely, exactly. But you're not with anyone either. Both of those options cost you something. You can spend your energy performing company, or you can spend your evening without it.

There's a third option, though. It's just harder to see because nothing in our culture points you toward it.

## Sideways

Hands change the arithmetic. When your hands have a job, the pressure to be interesting drops right off. You have somewhere to look — down, at the needles — and the silence stops feeling like a hole you have to fill.

The conversation goes sideways instead of straight across. And sideways is easier. It's why hard things get said in cars, and in kitchens while somebody's chopping onions. It's why people open up on walks, not across formal dining tables. Nobody's watching your face while you look for the words. You can stop mid-sentence to count stitches, and nobody minds, and then you can carry on.

Side by side. Not face to face. The talking that happens when your hands are busy tends to be more honest and less polished. You're not composing. You're just saying things as they come, the way you would to yourself, except someone is there to hear.

## A table with hands on it

Somewhere near you, most likely, a handful of people meet up to knit. A room at the back of a library. A café in the quiet hour. Someone's front room with too many chairs in it.

What happens there is: not much. Someone asks what you're making. You say a dishcloth. Nobody is impressed, and nobody needs to be, and honestly that's the whole appeal of it.

The woman who has knitted for forty years sits next to the man on his third row, and it isn't a lesson. It's a table with hands on it. Help gets offered when it's wanted, and not as instruction, just as a hand reaching over. Sometimes you watch someone else's fingers for a minute and figure out what yours were doing wrong. Sometimes you don't. It doesn't matter.

And out in the world, people will talk to you. If you knit on a train, in a waiting room, on a park bench, someone will stop. Someone's grandmother knitted. Someone's aunt. Someone remembers a blanket they still have. They stop to tell you about her, and you let them. Your hands gave them a doorway, and they walked through it.

## Permission

There's older company in it too. Every stitch you make, somebody once showed somebody else, hand over hand, back further than anyone can trace. You're doing what they did. The gesture repeats. Yarn over, pull through. A motion that has crossed centuries and oceans, and here it is in your lap on a Tuesday.

You don't have to join anything. You can knit alone for years and be perfectly well. Plenty of people do, and that's a whole life's worth of pleasure.

But if you're lonely and talking feels like too much just now — take your knitting. Sit down next to somebody. Let your hands carry the part you can't. You don't need to explain. The needles explain. And the quiet that settles around you both will say more than you think.
