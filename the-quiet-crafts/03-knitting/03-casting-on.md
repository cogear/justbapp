# Casting on

*The first row of knitting is the one you make from nothing — and the one that teaches you the most about starting.*

## The Row You Make From Nothing

Before you can knit anything, you have to get the yarn onto the needle. That's not knitting yet. That's just getting the yarn there. And it's the part that stops most people.

It has a name. Casting on. It's the only part of knitting that starts from nothing at all.

Everything after this works with stitches that already exist. You move them along, you change them, you pass them from one needle to the other. But casting on is the row you make out of air. There's nothing on the needle, and then there's something. A line of loops where a moment ago there was just a loose strand of yarn.

That transition — from nothing to something — is where a lot of people put the needles down and never pick them back up. The first row feels awkward and uncertain in a way that the rest of knitting, even when it's difficult, doesn't. Because once stitches exist, your hands have something to work with. They have a path to follow. Casting on has no path. You're laying the path down as you go.

## Fighting With String

The first thing you'll learn is a slip knot. A loop that tightens when you pull the tail. Your hands will make it wrong three or four times before they make it right. Three or four is the normal number. Not a sign that knitting isn't for you. Not evidence that you lack some quality that real knitters have. Just the number of tries it takes before your fingers understand what they're being asked to do.

Then you make loops, one after another, and most people make them too tight. You pull the yarn snug because snug feels careful. Careful feels like you're doing it right. And then the needle won't slide. The loops grip the metal like they're holding on for dear life, and every small movement becomes a struggle.

There's a stretch here where nothing feels like a craft. It feels like fighting with string in your lap while your shoulders climb toward your ears. You might think you're doing something wrong. You are — but only in the sense that everyone does it wrong first. Everyone's hands are clumsy with this. Nobody's hands know it yet. Yours are exactly on schedule.

## A Loop On A Stick

Here's what helps. A stitch is not a knot. It's a loop of yarn lying over a stick. Nothing holds it there except the stick. Tip the needle down and the loops would slide off into your hand. They're not attached. They're not committed. They're resting.

Which means you can take the whole row out with one pull. The yarn straightens. It doesn't crease, doesn't remember, doesn't hold anything against you. The strand goes back to being a strand, and you're free to start again as if nothing happened.

So you haven't committed to anything. You've arranged some yarn on metal. That's all casting on has ever been. When you understand this — really understand it, not just as an idea but as a feeling in your hands — the tension changes. Not just in the yarn. In you.

## Twenty, Then Twenty Again

So do it badly, on purpose. Cast on twenty stitches. Count them, touching each one with your finger so you're sure. Then slide the needle out and let the whole row fall back into a plain strand of yarn.

Do it again. And again. Somewhere around the fourth or fifth time, your hands find a rhythm. Not because you've gotten better, but because you stopped watching yourself do it. The way you stop watching your feet when you walk. The body learns the thing by doing the thing, and the mind gets out of the way.

Aim for loose enough that each loop travels along the needle without you pushing it. That's the whole standard. It slides, or it sticks. If it sticks, pull it out and try again. You lose nothing. The yarn doesn't mind.

## Nowhere Else To Be

Some evenings, casting on is the only thing you do. You sit down, you make a row of loops, you count them, you pull it out, you make it again. The tea goes cold beside you. An hour passes and you have nothing to show for it. No scarf. No square. No proof that you spent your time well.

That isn't a wasted evening. Nothing has to come of it. The row you made and unmade was still an hour your hands spent doing quiet, ordinary work. An hour where your attention was on something small and physical instead of scattered across the day's usual noise.

Cast on. Count. Start over if you want to. The yarn is patient. It has nowhere else to be.
