# Collage without art school

*You already have everything you need — a box of scraps, a glue stick, and an hour that belongs to no one.*

## The Box

There's a box somewhere in your house. Maybe it's an actual box. Maybe it's a drawer, or a folder tucked behind the cookbooks. Old magazines. A ticket stub from a film you can't remember the plot of. The brown paper a bunch of flowers came wrapped in, too nice to recycle. A postcard from someone you no longer write to.

You kept all of it without deciding why. That's fine. You don't have to justify the keeping.

That's the whole material list. That, and a glue stick, and an hour nobody's asking you about.

You don't need to know what you're making. That's not a shortcut or a beginner's compromise. That's the method itself. Not knowing is where this begins.

## The Word Next To It

The trouble with collage isn't the scissors. It's the word standing next to it. Art. That word arrives with baggage — an imagined room full of people who know more than you do, who learned things in buildings you didn't enter, who'd look at your page and quietly catalog what's wrong with it.

So we hesitate. We assume there's a composition we should understand. Colour theory. A reason the red piece belongs in that corner and not the other one. Some hidden grammar of placement that everyone else apparently studied.

We assume the finished thing is supposed to mean something. To communicate. To reveal.

It doesn't have to do any of that. Nobody is coming to look at it. There's no critique at the end of your kitchen table. No grade. No audience. The page belongs to you and the hour you gave it, and that's the entire arrangement.

## You Already Know How

Here's what collage actually is. Moving pieces of paper around on a flat surface until they sit right. That's the whole craft.

And your hands have been practising it for years, without calling it anything.

You've arranged plates on a shelf and then swapped two of them. Shuffled photographs across a table until the order felt true. Slid a chair two inches to the left because two inches mattered, and you couldn't have explained why if someone asked. You just knew. Same skill. Same quiet certainty.

You'll know when it's right, and you'll know without a vocabulary for it. Something in your chest loosens when the pieces stop arguing with each other. The page settles. You weren't taught that feeling. You arrived with it.

That's the only standard here. Not good. Not clever. Not even finished. Just — the arguing stopped.

## The Long Fiddling

So tip the box out. Spread everything where your eye can travel over it without reaching. Then pick up whatever you keep coming back to, and set it down on a page.

Add one more piece beside it. Move that piece. Move it again. Turn it upside down. Let it hang off the edge — paper doesn't have to stay inside a border it never asked for. Cover half of it with something else and see what happens. Pull it back. Try the blue scrap instead. There's no wrong move here, only moves you haven't tried yet.

None of this costs anything, because nothing is stuck down yet. You can sit in the undecided part as long as you want. Most people rush it — reaching for the glue before the arrangement has settled, because commitment feels like progress. Don't. The undecided part is the craft.

The glue comes last, and much later than you'd think. The arranging is where the quiet lives — that long, unhurried fiddling before anything is committed. Your hands are busy. Your mind goes soft and slow. This is the point.

## Uselessly Yours

And when you're finished — or when you decide you're finished, which is a different thing — you'll have a piece of paper with other paper stuck to it. It won't hang in a gallery. It won't be useful to anyone. It won't solve a problem or demonstrate a skill worth listing.

Good. That's allowed. That's the whole idea.

Slide it into a drawer. Or the fridge door, if you're fond of it. Then next week, tip the box out and do it again — badly, happily, with no plan at all. The box doesn't care. The glue stick doesn't care. The hour is still yours.

You gave time to your hands, your scraps, and nobody's opinion. That was the whole thing. That was it.
