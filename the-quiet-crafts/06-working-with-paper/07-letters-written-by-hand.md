# Letters, written by hand

*The slowest kind of writing, and maybe the most honest.*

## The one you kept

Somewhere in your house there's probably a letter you kept. Not an email — a letter. You knew the handwriting before you read a single word.

You didn't keep it because it said anything important. Most letters don't say anything important. You kept it because somebody's hand moved across paper for you, and you could see that it had. The pressure changed mid-word. A line tilted uphill. There was a crossing-out they left because there was no undo.

Nobody prints an email. Nobody saves a text. But paper a person touched — we hold on to that, without ever quite deciding to.

## Fast words

We send more words to each other than any people in history. Hundreds a day, typed with our thumbs, delivered in a second, forgotten by evening. Most of them deserve to be. That's what messages are for — the quick coordinate, the confirmation, the tiny ping that says *I'm still here.*

Nothing you thumb out at a red light is going to sit in a drawer for thirty years.

A letter is slower than the person writing it. That's the whole of it. It's the one kind of writing that can't keep up with your own thoughts. And that lag, that slight drag between thinking and ink — that's where something quiet happens.

## The hand trails behind

Pick up a pen and you'll feel it. Your hand trails behind your head. You get halfway through a sentence, decide you'd rather say it differently, and there it is — already written, already down there on the page.

So you finish the sentence. Crooked, slightly wrong, honest in a way that typing never quite manages. The backspace key has quietly cost us something — not just accuracy, but the trace of a person figuring it out as they go.

And then your body settles in. The scratch of the pen. The drag of your wrist across the page. The small pause at the end of a line while you find the next word. Your breathing slows without being asked to. This is what making with your hands does, even when the thing you're making is just a sentence about the weather.

## One page

Write about nothing. The weather. What you ate. Something the cat has started doing. Letters aren't for news — news travels faster than the post ever will. Letters are for the sound of someone being present, taking their time, not rushing.

One page. When the page runs out, stop, even mid-thought. Sign it. Don't read it back hunting for mistakes. The mistakes are the proof that a person was here, not a machine.

You don't need good paper. A pad from the drawer and a pen that works. The nice stationery tends to sit unopened for years, waiting for a letter worthy of it. Use the ordinary stuff. The ordinariness is part of the point.

Address the envelope by hand as well. Find a stamp. Walk it to the postbox yourself. That walk is part of it — the last stretch of the making, when the thing leaves your hands and goes out into the world on its own.

## Sent

Then it's gone. You can't unsend it. You can't see whether it was opened. It travels for days somewhere out there, with none of you attached to it. There's something freeing about that — a thing you made, released completely, belonging to no one's notification system.

Maybe one comes back. Maybe it doesn't. That isn't a failure. You didn't write it to get something — you gave an hour of your hand to someone you like. That was the whole errand.

So write to somebody this week. About nothing much. In your own imperfect handwriting. Fold it, seal it, and let it go.

And if a letter comes back to you someday — don't be surprised if you keep it.
