# Zines and very small print runs

*One sheet of paper, folded a few times, becomes a book nobody asked for — and that's exactly right.*

## one sheet

Take one sheet of paper. Printer paper, nothing special. The kind you already have in a drawer somewhere.

Fold it in half. Then in half again. Then one more time. Open it up. Eight little rectangles, creased and waiting. One cut down the middle fold, a few folds back on themselves, and you have a book. Eight pages. No glue. No thread. No special tools. One sheet of paper that was hiding a book the whole time.

People call these zines. The name matters less than the fact of it. You can make one in a minute, and then spend a whole week deciding what goes inside.

There's something quietly astonishing about this. A single flat sheet, ordinary in every way, becomes a structure. A container. Something with a front and a back and pages in between. You don't build it so much as reveal it. The book was already there. You just had to fold the right way.

## a lot of weight

Somewhere along the way, putting words or pictures on paper came to mean publishing. And publishing means an audience. A number. Metrics. Someone out there deciding whether it was worth doing, whether it was good enough, whether you had the right to put it into the world.

So we don't make the small thing. We think about who'd want it. Where it would go. Whether it measures up. We imagine the judgment before we've even picked up a pencil. And the sheet of paper stays in the drawer. Uncut. Unfolded.

That's a lot of weight for eight tiny pages to carry.

The irony is that zines started as the opposite of all that. They were homemade, rough, xeroxed, handed to friends. Nobody was trying to reach a market. Someone just wanted to make a thing about something they noticed, and make a few copies, and that was enough. Somewhere between then and now, even the smallest acts of making got pulled into the gravity of audience and output. Even a folded piece of paper has to justify itself now.

But it doesn't. Not here.

## make five

Here's the way out of that weight. Make five copies. Not fifty. Not five hundred. Five.

Five is not an audience. Five is not a market. Five is barely more than one. There's nothing to measure and nobody to impress. You can't optimize a run of five. You can't build a brand on five. The number is too small to take seriously, which is exactly why it works.

At five copies, the making is the whole thing. The folding. The smell of a copy shop on a tuesday afternoon. The stack squared up between your palms. Then doing it again, exactly the same way, watching your hands get a little more sure with each one.

You're allowed to make something nobody will read. The copies aren't for anyone. They're just proof that your hands were somewhere for an hour, doing something that asked for patience and attention and nothing else.

## fold, cut, staple

Pick something small and specific. Every door on your street. The sounds your house makes at night. Nine ways to hold a cup. The birds you saw this week, in order. It doesn't have to mean anything. Small and true is better than big and important.

Fill the eight pages. Pencil, then ink. Or just pencil. Or leave one blank — a blank page is allowed. It's your eight pages. One of them can be a pause.

Then take the flat sheet somewhere with a copier and ask for five. That's the only part that requires anyone else.

Then the part your hands will remember. Fold. Cut. Fold. Crease with a thumbnail. Staple. Trim the edge. Do it four more times. By the third one, your hands stop asking questions. They know what comes next. The body learns the sequence, and the thinking mind gets to rest.

There's a particular sound a stapler makes going through folded paper. Dull, and final, and done. Not much else in a day ends that cleanly.

## a stack of five

Five small books about door colors, or night sounds, or the nine ways to hold a cup. Nobody asked for them. Nobody needs them. That's most of what makes them good.

Keep them in a drawer. Leave one on a bus seat. Hand one to a neighbour without explaining. Or don't. A stack of five is fine as a stack of five. It doesn't need a destination to be complete.

The print run was never the point. It was the folding, and the counting, and the quiet of a job your hands could finish before dinner. Something made, held, set down. Done.
