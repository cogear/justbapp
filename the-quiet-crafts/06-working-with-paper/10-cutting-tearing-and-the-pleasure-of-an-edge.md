# Cutting, tearing, and the pleasure of an edge

*Two ways to make an edge, and what your hands already know about both.*

## Two sounds

Start with something you don't need. A piece of junk mail, a page from a magazine you've already read. Tear it in half — but slowly. Not one quick rip. Let your thumbs move together and pull a little at a time.

Listen.

That sound is thousands of fibers letting go of each other. You can hear them go.

Now take another piece and cut it with scissors. A completely different sound. A different edge, too. Same paper, same hands, same afternoon.

Your fingers know the difference before your head does. The cut edge is cool and definite. The torn edge is softer, a little wild, showing the pale fuzzy inside of the paper that the mill pressed smooth and hid away from you.

Both are edges. Neither is better. But they aren't the same, and pretending they are would be a kind of lie.

## Decided and shared

A cut edge is a decision made once. Straight, exact, a little cold. It says: here, and no further. It looks intended because it was. The blade goes through and leaves a line that belongs entirely to you.

A torn edge is different. You start it, but the paper finishes it. The tear wanders a bit. It follows the grain, the fiber, the hidden structure inside the sheet. You give up some control and you get a line you could never have drawn.

That's the trade. One edge is all yours. The other you make together with the paper.

Sometimes you want the clean one — the certainty of it, the simple satisfaction of a straight line that ends exactly where you meant it to. Sometimes the rough one is the only thing that feels right, because it's honest about what paper is, and because your hands made something they can't fully explain.

There's no rule. There's only which one you feel like today, and that might change tomorrow.

## Rhythm and resistance

Cutting has a rhythm. Open the scissors, close them, feed the paper forward with your other hand. Go slow and the blades whisper. Rush and they chatter, and the line goes crooked — not because you failed, but because the pace was wrong for the tool.

Tearing has a rhythm too. Thumbs close together. Short pulls, an inch at a time. Pull from too far away and the paper tears where it wants instead of where you meant. Stay close and the line follows you.

Keep your fingers back from the blade. The whole thing gets quieter when you aren't slightly afraid of the edge. A sharp blade on firm paper is calm and precise. A dull one on limp paper is frustrating and chattery. Your hands know the difference, and they adjust before you think about it.

That's the part I keep coming back to. However you do it — cutting or tearing, scissors or fingers — your hands find the pace before your mind agrees to it. They settle into the rhythm on their own. You just have to let them.

## Cut nothing in particular

So sit down with paper you don't care about. Envelopes, a magazine, a page from a book you'll never finish. And cut.

Not shapes of anything. Strips. Squares. Long slow curves that go nowhere at all. Then tear a few, just to feel the difference in your thumbs.

Notice the ones you like. Some edges will feel good and you won't be able to say why. That's allowed. You don't need a reason. Keep going.

Let them pile up on the table beside you. Don't glue them to anything. Don't turn the pile into a project. It isn't a draft of something — it's just the pile. The pile is fine.

## The edge was enough

At the end you'll have a small heap of paper that's useful to nobody. Sweep it into your palm and drop it in the recycling. Notice that you're steadier than when you sat down. Not transformed, not improved. Just steadier.

There's a real pleasure in a clean edge. A different one in a rough edge. Neither has to become something to have been worth the half hour.

The edge was the point. The cutting was the point. Your hands were busy and quiet, and the afternoon went somewhere gentle.
