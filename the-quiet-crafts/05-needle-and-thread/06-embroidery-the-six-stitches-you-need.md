# Embroidery: the six stitches you need

*A handful of movements that can carry you for decades — and the permission to stop there.*

## The whole stitch dictionary is not your problem

There are hundreds of embroidery stitches. Thousands, if you count all the regional ones with their own names and half-names and variations-on-variations. You need six.

Not six to get started before you learn the real ones. Six. You could work with these for forty years and never reach the end of what they can do. Nobody is going to test you on the rest. There's no level you graduate to, no certification, no moment where someone taps your shoulder and says *now you may learn the bullion knot.*

The abundance of a stitch dictionary is generous in spirit but paralyzing in practice. It suggests that embroidery is vast and requires a map. It isn't, and it doesn't. It's a handful of ways to move a needle through cloth.

## Why people buy the hoop and then don't touch it

Open a stitch dictionary and you'll find page after page of diagrams. Arrows. Numbered dots. Little dotted lines showing where the needle enters and exits. It reads like an exam you forgot to study for.

So the hoop gets bought, and the thread gets bought, and the little pack of needles gets bought, and it all goes in a drawer. Not because embroidery is hard. Because it looks like homework. And embroidery gets filed alongside the things you'd need a class for, a teacher for, a weekend workshop and a certificate.

Embroidery isn't a curriculum. Six stitches do almost everything you'll ever see in any embroidered cloth, in any museum, in any market. That's the entire secret. Not a secret, really — just something that doesn't sell books.

## Line, curve, fill, dot, rope, petal

Here they are.

**Backstitch** makes a line. Solid, unbroken, good for outlines and for words. It's the stitch you'll use to write your name on something, or to draw the edge of a leaf.

**Stem stitch** makes a line that bends. With backstitch, a curve looks like a series of small straight decisions — because it is. Stem stitch turns a corner without the corners. It flows.

**Satin stitch** fills a shape. Thread laid down side by side, row after row, until the cloth underneath disappears and the surface turns glossy. This is the stitch that makes embroidery look like painting — slow, patient painting with a single hair.

**French knot** makes a dot. One dot. That's all it does. It only does that, and it is the most useful thing in the box. Eyes, seeds, stamens, stars, texture, a field of scattered rain.

**Chain stitch** makes a rope — loops holding hands down the length of a line. It has a spine to it, a raised quality. You can feel it under your thumb.

**Lazy daisy** is one chain stitch left open on its own. A single petal. Five of them in a circle and you've got a flower. It is the stitch that taught every embroiderer that a flower is just a decision repeated.

Line. Curve. Fill. Dot. Rope. Petal. That's the vocabulary. Everything else you'll ever see — feather stitch, herringbone, cretan, chevron, coral — is one of these six held differently, bent at a different angle, or given someone's name.

## Rows, not pictures

Cut a square of plain cotton and put it in a hoop. Then practice the six in rows. Not on a picture — just rows, across the cloth, the way you'd practice letters on lined paper before you tried to write a sentence.

Your first backstitch will wander. The stitches will be uneven — some tight, some loose, some angled where they shouldn't be. The twentieth row will be even, and you won't be able to say what changed. Your hands are learning something they can't explain to you, and don't need to.

Somewhere in the third row, the thinking stops. Down, up, pull. Down, up, pull. The needle goes where you already decided it would go. There's nothing left to work out. Your body knows the movement before you do, and your mind drifts somewhere else — to the evening light, to a conversation you had earlier, to nothing at all.

This is the quiet the whole practice exists to reach. Not the finished cloth. The quiet.

## A page of nonsense

When the square is full you'll have a page of nonsense. Lines going nowhere. A field of dots that don't spell anything. A flower with too many petals leaning over to one side like it got tired.

Keep it anyway. Not as proof of progress, not as a before-picture for some later comparison. It's just the first cloth your hands ever talked on. Pin it up somewhere. It doesn't have to become anything.

Six stitches. A scrap of cotton. An evening with nowhere else to be. That's the whole of it.
