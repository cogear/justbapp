# Threading, knotting, the running stitch

*A few small motions that ask nothing of you except your hands.*

There is a small moment at the beginning of sewing that nobody warns you about. You hold a needle up to the light, and you try to put a piece of thread through a hole you can barely see.

It takes a few tries. It always takes a few tries.

That isn't a sign you're bad at this. That is simply what threading a needle is — for everyone, every time. So let's start there. Three things: getting the thread in, tying the knot, and the one stitch that does most of the work.

## Getting the thread in

Cut the thread at an angle, with scissors, not your teeth. A clean diagonal end goes through the eye. A frayed, chewed end does not.

Wet it if you like. Press it flat between your thumb and finger until it becomes a thin blade. Then bring the needle to the thread, not the thread to the needle. Your hand is steadier that way — you're moving the smaller, lighter thing toward the thing that's already anchored.

Hold it against something pale — your palm, a sheet of paper. The eye shows up dark against the light. And then it goes through, and you feel unreasonably pleased about it.

There's no trick that removes the fiddling. There are only small things that make it shorter. A sharp cut. A flattened end. A pale background. That's all. The rest is patience, which is also part of the practice.

## A knot at the end

Now the knot. Pull the thread through until one end is longer than the other. About a forearm's length of working thread. Longer than that and it tangles on itself, which is frustrating in a particular way that makes you want to stop.

Wrap the end twice around your fingertip. Roll it off with your thumb. It gathers into a small tight bundle, and you pull it down to the end of the thread. That's your knot.

You will make hundreds of these. The first ones will be lumpy and too big, and the thread will twist in a direction you didn't intend. Eventually your fingers will tie one while you're looking somewhere else entirely, and you won't remember doing it. The knot becomes something your hands just know, like snapping your fingers or turning a doorknob. You don't get there by trying. You get there by repeating.

## Up, down, up

The running stitch is the simplest thing there is. Up through the fabric. Down through the fabric. Up again. That's all it is. That's the whole stitch.

Try to keep them even. Same length on top, same length underneath. Not perfect — even. You get evenness by doing it a lot, not by trying harder. This is worth noticing, because it's true of most things done by hand. The improvement comes from repetition, not from concentration. You can't think your way into a neat stitch. You can only stitch your way into a neat stitch.

After a while your hand finds a rhythm, and the rhythm takes over. In and out. The needle rocking gently between your fingers. Your breath slows down to match it without asking you first. This is the quiet that people who work with their hands keep talking about — not silence, but a kind of settling. Your body knows what to do, and your mind, for once, doesn't need to supervise.

If a stitch goes crooked, leave it. Pulling it out costs more than the crooked stitch does. The fabric will have a hole where the needle went in, and that hole doesn't close back up. A crooked stitch in a practice rag is just evidence that you were here, learning something.

## Lines on a rag

Practice on a scrap. Not a project, not something you need. A square of old sheet, a bit of a worn shirt. Stitch a line across it. Then another line. Then another.

It will look like nothing. A few uneven lines on a rag. And that is exactly right.

You're not making a thing. You're teaching your hands a motion they'll keep. Thread, knot, in and out. Three small motions. They'll be waiting for you whenever you want them — tonight, next week, next month. Your hands won't forget.

That's the thing nobody tells you about working with your hands. The knowledge lives in your fingers, not in your memory. You don't have to review it or rehearse it. You just pick up the needle, and there it is, still there, still yours.
