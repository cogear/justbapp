# Darning a sock

*A hole in a sock, a needle, half an hour that wasn't going anywhere anyway.*

## The hole

You notice it before you see it. A soft spot under your heel, then a give, then the strange feeling of your bare foot against the inside of your shoe. You take the sock off later and there it is — a small wound in the fabric, fraying at the edges.

The sensible thing is to throw it out. Socks are cheap. They come in packs of six. This one cost less than a coffee and has already outlived its reasonable life.

We're going to fix it anyway. Not to save money. Not to make a point about sustainability. Because it's a decent thing to do with your hands on a Tuesday evening, and the alternative was another half hour of scrolling.

## The arithmetic nobody wins

Let's be honest about this. You'll spend thirty minutes threading a needle and weaving wool across a gap that's smaller than a coin. You'll use a length of yarn that probably cost something. And the sock will wear through somewhere else inside a year, maybe two.

If that sounds like a waste of time, it's worth asking what the thirty minutes was otherwise going to be. Most of my evenings don't survive that question. They dissolve into something I can't quite name and didn't particularly enjoy.

There's no version of this where you come out ahead. That's what makes it restful. Nothing is riding on it. The sock doesn't care how good you are. Nobody is grading your stitches. The whole thing is weightless in a way that very little of adult life is.

## What you need

Almost nothing. A needle. Some wool or thread — darning wool if you have it, embroidery thread if you don't, even regular sewing thread doubled up if that's what's in the house. And something round to stretch the sock over.

A darning mushroom is the proper tool. You probably don't have one. A tennis ball works. An orange. The base of a small jar. A lightbulb, if you're careful. Anything that sits under the hole and gives it a gentle dome to rest against.

Thread the needle. Don't tie a knot — a knot is a lump, and a lump under your heel will annoy you for the rest of the sock's life. Instead, anchor with a few small stitches in the good fabric around the hole. Leave a tail. It'll felt into place soon enough.

## Over, under

Then you build. Rows of thread across the hole, one way, like the rungs of a ladder. Leave a small gap between each row — about the width of the thread itself. When you've covered the hole in one direction, turn and go across those rows, weaving over and under, over and under.

You're making a small piece of cloth where there wasn't one. That's all darning is. Not a mystery. Not a craft you have to study. Just threads going across, and threads going through.

If you lose your place, the threads show you where you are. Nothing in this asks you to remember anything. You look down and the work tells you what comes next.

## What the hands know

This part takes a while. Over, under, over, under. Your hands learn the rhythm in the first minute and then they don't need much from you. Your eyes stay on the wool. Your mind goes wherever it goes.

That's the part I'd come back for, even if the sock vanished. Twenty quiet minutes where the only decision left is over, or under. The world outside is full of decisions that matter and have consequences and ripple outward. This one doesn't. Over, under. Over, under.

It's fine if the darn is ugly. Mine always are — lumpy little patches, slightly the wrong colour, the tension uneven because I always pull too tight on the first few rows. It's fine if this is the only thing you make all month. The skill will come or it won't. The hole will be closed either way.

## Back in the drawer

The finished darn will not be invisible. It'll sit under your heel where nobody will ever see it, and when you put the sock on next week you'll feel it for a moment — a slight ridge, a small newness under your foot — and then you'll forget it was ever mended.

That's the whole of it. A small hole, closed by hand, for no good reason at all.

The sock goes back in the drawer. Nothing happens. Nobody claps. And somehow that feels like the point — that you did something small and quiet and useless, and the world didn't need it, and it was still worth doing.
