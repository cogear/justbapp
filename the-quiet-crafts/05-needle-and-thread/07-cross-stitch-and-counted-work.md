# Cross stitch and counted work

*some kinds of sewing take the deciding off your hands — and that's exactly the relief you need.*

## a grid that's already made

There's a kind of sewing where you don't have to decide anything.

The fabric is a grid. The holes are already there. You count, and you put the needle where the counting says.

Cross stitch. Counted work. One small shape, made over and over, until something appears.

Which sounds mechanical. And it is, in the same way that breathing is mechanical, or walking. The repetition isn't the obstacle. The repetition is the thing you came for.

It's one of the most peaceful things I know how to do with my hands.

## tired from deciding

Think about how much of your day is choosing. What to say in the email. What to eat. Which of four perfectly fine options to go with. Most of them have no right answer. You still have to pick one, and then live with the picking.

By evening, you're often not tired from effort. You're tired from deciding. Your mind has been making tiny judgments all day long, and now it's spent.

Counted work takes the deciding away. Not by making it simple — by moving the choices somewhere else. Onto a chart, made by somebody else, or by you, on some other day when you felt like making decisions. The chart is a plan you don't have to think about now. You just follow it.

That removal is the gift. Not the pattern, not the finished picture. The fact that for a little while, you don't have to choose anything at all.

## reading, not drawing

The cloth is woven so the holes sit in even rows. Graph paper you can feel with your fingers. After a while your hands find the next hole without your eyes going along.

And the stitch is one stitch. Up through one corner, down through the corner across from it. A small X. Then another, exactly the same, next to it. That's the whole vocabulary.

Each square on the chart is a colour. Each colour is a thread. You aren't drawing anything. You're reading, and your hands are writing it down.

There's something about that arrangement — your eyes reading, your hands writing — that splits your attention in a useful way. Your mind is occupied just enough. Not gripped, not strained. Held, gently, by a task that needs both halves of you but asks very little of either.

## five, six, seven

You count under your breath. Five, six, seven. It sounds silly until you notice that the counting has crowded out everything else you were carrying around. The thing someone said at lunch. The list of things you forgot to do. The worry that has no solution and no shape. All of it gets quietly shouldered aside by five, six, seven.

Sometimes you lose the count. You find out twenty stitches later, when one row won't line up. You can pick it out, or you can leave it. Both are honest answers. Nobody is coming to check.

An hour can go by like this. Then another. Not lost — just quiet. The count is small enough to hold, and big enough that nothing else fits in beside it.

That's the particular quality of counted work. It fills a certain size of space in your mind. Not all of it. Not the way a screen does. Just enough that the rest of you can settle down around it.

## the evidence

And at the end there's a thing. Small, blocky, made of squares. A strawberry. A house with a crooked roof. A word in a font that doesn't quite match anything. Something nobody in your life needs.

You don't have to frame it. You don't have to give it to anyone or explain what it's for. It can live in a drawer for years and lose nothing at all.

Because the picture was never the point. The counting was the point. The picture is just what's left over — the evidence that for a while, your hands knew exactly what to do, and you let them do it.

That's all it is. That's all it needs to be.
