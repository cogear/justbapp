# A mending basket instead of a donation bag

*A place where the things you're not done with can wait for you.*

## The bag by the door

There's a bag by your door. Or in the back of a closet. The one you put clothes in when you don't quite know what else to do with them. It's been there a while.

A shirt missing one button. Jeans worn thin at the pocket. A dress with a seam that quietly let go somewhere in the wash. Nothing really wrong with any of it. Just not whole.

You probably know the feeling. You hold something up, see the small flaw, and feel a flicker of something — not quite guilt, not quite decision. Just a vague sense that you should do something about it. And then the thing goes in the bag, because the bag is where things go when you don't have the energy to think about them right now.

I want to offer you a different container. Not a bag that leaves. A basket that stays.

## What the bag decides

The donation bag is for things you've already decided about. It closes at the top. It goes in the car. It leaves, and you feel a little lighter, and that's real. Letting go is fine. Sometimes it's exactly right.

But some of what ends up in that bag isn't finished with you. It didn't need replacing. It needed ten minutes and a needle, on a night you didn't have ten minutes.

That's what the bag does, quietly. It turns every small flaw into a choice between perfect and gone. And most of the time, we choose gone, because it feels simpler. But simplicity and finality aren't always the same thing.

There's a difference between something you've genuinely outgrown and something that just needs a moment of your attention. The bag doesn't leave room for that distinction. It treats all of it the same — as excess, as clutter, as something already behind you.

## Open at the top

So instead: a basket. Shallow, open at the top, big enough for four or five things. You can see everything in it. Nothing is hidden. Nothing has been decided yet.

And this part matters more than the basket itself. It is not a to-do list. Nothing in it is late. Nothing in it is owed to anyone.

Things sit there until you feel like picking one up. A week. A month. A whole season. The shirt doesn't mind waiting. It has nowhere to be.

This is the part that takes practice. We're so trained to see a small pile of things as a backlog — evidence of something we should be doing and aren't. But a basket isn't a backlog. It's just a place where things rest until you're ready. The readiness is the whole thing. Without it, the mending is just another chore you're behind on.

## Where it lives

Put the basket near the chair you actually sit in. Not the spare room. Not a high shelf. Somewhere your hand passes it in the ordinary course of a day. If you have to go looking for it, you won't. That's not a flaw in you. That's just how attention works.

Keep what you need inside it. A few needles, thread in three or four colors, small scissors. So there's nothing to hunt for when the mood arrives — because the mood is often brief. You feel it, and then it passes, and if you had to gather supplies first, the moment is already gone.

Then some evening, take one thing out. Sit with it while something plays in the background. Get the button on, or get halfway. Put it back either way. No one's counting.

The basket doesn't empty. Things go in, things come out, the level stays about the same. That's not failure. That's just how a household works. Clothes get worn. Seams give way. Buttons disappear in the wash. The basket isn't trying to solve that. It's just catching what falls, gently, until you have a free hand.

## Allowed to wait

You'll wear the mended shirt out into the world and nobody will notice a thing. That's the whole reward, and it's bigger than it sounds. Something you kept, quietly, with your own hands. A small act of care that didn't require a trip, a purchase, or a decision about whether the thing deserved to exist anymore.

Nobody is measuring the basket. It isn't a project, or a habit you have to keep. It's just a place where your things are allowed to wait for you.

You don't have to be finished with something to stop deciding about it. You can simply set it down where you'll see it, and let it be there. And some evening when the light is low and your hands want something to do, you can pick it up again.
