# Buttons and hems

*A shirt in the back of your closet is waiting for ten minutes of your time.*

## One button

There's a shirt in the back of your closet you actually like. You haven't worn it in a year. Not because it's worn out. Because of one button.

Or a pair of trousers that sit an inch too long. You cuff them. You've been cuffing them for two years now. It works, mostly.

These are the two smallest repairs there are. And they quietly decide whether your clothes stay in your life or leave it.

It's a strange kind of loss. Nothing dramatic. No rip, no stain, no moment of failure. Just a button that came off and rolled under the radiator, and a pair of trousers that never quite looked right. The garment doesn't break. It just slips out of rotation. It becomes the thing you pass over when you're getting dressed, the thing you'll deal with later, and later never comes.

## Too small to be urgent

This is the strange thing about small repairs. It isn't that we can't do them. It's that they never rise high enough on the list to get done. Too small to be urgent. Too dull to be interesting.

So the shirt waits. The trousers wait. And slowly, without any decision being made, a perfectly good garment turns into a question. Keep it, or let it go.

And you already know it's a ten-minute job. That's almost the problem. Ten minutes is very easy to postpone forever. It doesn't demand your attention. It doesn't hurt anything by sitting there. So it sits there. And the closet fills up with small unfinished things, each one taking up space not because it's broken, but because it's almost fixed.

There's something else going on too. We've absorbed this idea that mending is something you do when you have to, something for people who can't afford to replace things. That's not true, and it was never true. Mending is what people have always done with clothes, because clothes were made to be kept. A button coming off isn't a sign of failure. It's just what happens to buttons. They're designed to come off eventually. They're the part of the shirt that takes all the strain.

## Ten minutes

So let's just do it. Find the button tin, or the little paper packet that came with the shirt. Or a button that's close enough. Close enough is fine. Match the thread if you can. If you can't, dark thread on dark cloth disappears well enough.

Thread doubled. A tiny stitch to anchor it. Then up through one hole, down through the next. Six or seven passes. Slip a pin underneath, so the button sits with a little room to move.

Pull the pin out. Wind the loose thread around the stem a few times. Knot it against the cloth, snip. That's it. That's the whole craft.

Ten minutes. And then it isn't a question anymore. It's just a shirt.

The pin under the button is the one step people skip, and it's the one that matters. Without it, the button sits flat against the fabric, and there's no room for the cloth of the shirt to fit underneath when you button it. The button pulls. The thread strains. It comes off again in a month. That little gap, that bit of space between the button and the fabric, is the whole secret. It's a small thing that makes the repair last.

## The long way around

A hem is the other kind of work. Not ten minutes — maybe an hour, in a chair, with the garment across your knees. And nothing about it is difficult.

Turn the raw edge under once. Turn it under again. Press the fold flat with your thumbnail, pin it, and then begin — catching one thread of the outside fabric, then a bite of the fold. Over and over.

Small, even, forgettable stitches. You'll be bad at it for the first twenty. Then your hands take over, and you can stop watching so hard.

This is the part people don't expect. A long hem is pleasant. The radio on. The same motion, again and again, going somewhere at the speed of about an inch a minute.

There's a particular quiet that settles in when you're doing this. Not silence — you can have music, or a conversation, or the sounds from the window. But a kind of quiet inside. The part of your mind that's usually scanning, planning, worrying, has something to do. Something small and rhythmic. It's the same quiet that comes from kneading bread, or weeding a garden bed, or sanding a piece of wood. Your body knows the motion, and your mind can rest.

## Nobody notices

When you wear it, nobody will say anything. There's nothing to see. The hem hangs straight. The button holds.

Your button might be a slightly different white than the other five. Leave it. You'll be the only person who ever knows it's there.

This isn't thrift, and it isn't a skill you're building toward anything. It's just a quiet half hour, and afterwards, a shirt you can wear.

That mismatched button is a kind of signature, if you want it to be. Proof that a human hand was here. That someone sat down and did the small unglamorous thing that nobody asked for. The shirt is better for it, not because the repair is invisible, but because it's cared for.

Do it on a Sunday afternoon. Not to be productive. Just because the light is good, and your hands would like something small to hold.
