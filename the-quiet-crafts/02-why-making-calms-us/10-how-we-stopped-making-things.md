# How we stopped making things

*We didn't lose anything dramatic. We just stopped giving our hands somewhere to go.*

## The photograph

There's a photograph in a lot of families. Someone's grandmother, or a great-aunt, sitting in a chair. And in her hands, something half-made. Nobody remembers what it was.

She wasn't crafting. She wasn't taking something up. She was just a person whose hands had somewhere to go in the evening. That was normal. That was most people.

Somewhere along the way, our hands got quiet.

## A hundred reasonable choices

It wasn't one big thing. It was a hundred small conveniences, each one perfectly reasonable. A sweater that cost less than the wool. A chair that arrived in a box, in two days, already finished.

So we stopped. Not out of laziness. Out of arithmetic. Why give six evenings to something you could have by Thursday, cheaper, and better made than you'd manage anyway?

And it worked. Things are plentiful now, and cheap, and mostly fine. People have warm coats. That isn't a small thing, and I'm not going to pretend it is. Mass production solved real problems. People who would have gone cold now have coats. Children who would have gone without shoes now have shoes.

But the knowing went with it. Two generations, and the small skills that used to pass across a kitchen table just didn't get passed. Not lost, exactly. Just never handed over. A grandmother who could knit without looking didn't have a granddaughter sitting beside her long enough to learn. The wool didn't come out of the drawer. The drawer stayed shut, and then the muscle memory in those hands went with the woman when she went.

## The six evenings

Something left with the making, and it took a long while to notice what. It wasn't the sweater. We had sweaters. We had more sweaters than ever.

It was the six evenings. The hours where the hands were occupied and the mind was allowed to wander around loose, and nothing in the room needed a decision from you.

There's a particular quality to that kind of attention. Your hands are doing something that matters enough to keep you seated but not so much that it asks for your full mind. The body is busy. The thoughts can drift. Something in you settles without being told to settle.

We bought that time back, all six evenings of it, and then weren't sure what it was for. So we filled it with looking at things. And the hands stayed empty.

Ask someone to name one thing they made with their hands this year. Watch the pause. That pause is new. Her great-grandmother wouldn't have needed it. She might have said "bread" or "socks" or "the garden fence" without thinking, because the question would have been strange. What do you mean, made? What else would I have been doing?

## This isn't nostalgia

This isn't a plea to go backward. Nobody needs to churn butter to prove a point. Grinding your own flour will not make you a better person, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something.

The old making wasn't charming. Much of it was work nobody chose. You mended because there was one coat. Necessity is a hard teacher and it never asks whether you're in the mood. There's nothing romantic about stitching a patch by lamplight because the cold is coming and there's no money for a new coat. People who lived that way were often glad to stop.

Here's what's different now. Nothing depends on it. You can make something with nobody waiting for it, nobody needing it to be good, and no rent riding on whether it turns out. That's not how it used to be. That's a new kind of space.

## Somewhere to go

Which is a strange sort of freedom, when you think about it. We might be the first people who get to make things for no reason at all.

And your hands haven't forgotten. Not the skill — the skill takes time, and it will be clumsy for a while. But the wanting is still there. Give them thread, or clay, or wood, and watch. Something in the body recognizes the invitation. The fingers slow down. The shoulders drop. The breathing changes without anyone telling it to.

You don't have to be good at it. You don't have to finish. You don't have to show anyone. You just have to give your hands somewhere to go.
