# Both hands, moving: the body's part in it

*When both your hands are truly busy, something settles — and it isn't your mind doing the settling.*

## Full Hands

Notice what your hands are doing right now. Probably not much. One of them is holding a phone, maybe. The other is free, resting somewhere, waiting for nothing in particular.

This is how most of our days go. Hands in a kind of low idle. Not doing nothing, exactly, but not doing anything that asks much of them either.

Now think back to the last time both of your hands were busy at once. Truly busy. Holding, turning, pressing, pulling. Not typing — that's mostly fingers, and fast ones at that. I mean both hands engaged in something that asks for your whole hand. Your palm. Your grip. The weight of your arm.

Something settles in a person when that's true. It's hard to explain and easy to feel. And here's the part that might surprise you: it isn't happening in your head at all.

## From the Neck Up

Most of what we call thinking happens from the neck up. The body sits underneath it, still, waiting to be told something. Shoulders creeping forward. Hands idle, or half-idle, which is somehow worse.

And when something's bothering us, we try to think our way out of it. We reason. We rehearse. We replay conversations and imagine new ones. We stay in the same small room as the trouble, walking around and around the same furniture.

Meanwhile the body has been standing there the whole time, holding something useful. Nobody thought to ask it.

We treat the body like a stand for the head. Something to carry it from room to room. But the body has its own way of knowing, its own kind of attention. And it has been waiting.

## One Holds, One Works

Here's what I notice about working with your hands. Almost none of it can be done with one hand. One hand holds while the other works. One steadies, the other moves. They trade jobs without discussing it.

Dough needs a palm and a heel. A needle needs one hand to push and one to receive. Wood has to be held still before it will let you cut it. Yarn needs tension from one side and movement from the other.

So both arms are in it. And the shoulders. And the breath, which slows down to match whatever pace your hands have decided on. There isn't much of you left over to worry with.

That's the body's part in it. Not a metaphor. Just a smaller share of you available for spinning.

## Knowledge Below Language

And your hands know things you couldn't say out loud if you tried. How much pressure a seam will take. When clay has gone too dry. How a blade wants to travel with the grain instead of across it.

That knowledge arrives slowly, over many evenings, and then it stays. It lives somewhere below language. Which means you can't hurry it — and you can't really lose it either.

And while your hands are working, they don't need your commentary. You can set the commentary down on the table beside you. It'll still be there later, if you want it.

This is the part that's hard to explain to someone who hasn't felt it. The quiet that comes isn't an absence. It's a different kind of conversation — one that doesn't need words. Your hands are talking to the material, and the material is talking back, and you get to listen in. The anxious narrator in your head, the one who narrates everything, quiets down because there's already something happening. Something that doesn't need narration.

## Something to Press Against

Pay attention to the resistance. The push-back of the material — dough, wood, wool, wire. That's the part the body seems to like most. Something firm to press against that isn't you.

We spend so much of the day pressing against ourselves. Our own thoughts, our own worries, our own expectations. The body wants something outside of itself to meet. Something that pushes back honestly. Not a screen, which takes whatever you give it without resistance. Not a task, which just ends and is done. Something with weight and texture and a bit of refusal in it.

It doesn't have to be good. It doesn't have to be for anything. It only has to be a job that takes two hands and a bit of your weight.

So pick the thing up. Let your hands go first. The rest of you will catch up.
