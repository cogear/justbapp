# Why screens don't count

*A screen can hold your eyes for hours and never once ask your hands for anything.*

## The nearest thing

Your hands know where the phone is without looking. Mine do too. That isn't a character flaw or a discipline problem. It's just the nearest thing within reach at the end of a long day.

You sit down on the couch. Your body is tired. The phone is right there, in your pocket or on the armrest, and picking it up takes almost no effort at all. That's the thing — it asks almost nothing of you. That's why it wins.

There's nothing wrong with it. But an hour later, your hands feel like they've been nowhere. Something in you was busy the whole time, and almost none of it was your body. Your eyes moved. Your thumb swiped. Your jaw tightened maybe, and you didn't notice until you put the phone down and tried to relax it.

This is about one specific question, and it's the question underneath all the quiet crafts: why does an evening of making leave you settled, and an evening of scrolling leave you thin?

## Nothing pushes back

Start with the obvious thing nobody mentions. A screen is flat. Same weight all evening. Same temperature. Same smooth glass surface under your thumb, whether you're reading a poem or an argument, whether it's noon or midnight.

Nothing pushes back. That's the core of it.

Wool pulls against the needle. You feel the slight tug with every stitch, and your hands adjust without you thinking about it. Wood resists the blade, then gives, and in that resistance it tells you which way the grain runs. Dough clings to your fingers until, suddenly, it doesn't — there's a shift, and your hands know it before your mind does.

Glass tells you nothing. It's the same under your hand at midnight as it was at noon. Your fingers have no work to do, so they stay half-asleep while the rest of you races. That's the mismatch. Your eyes are flooded with input and your hands are doing almost nothing, and that split leaves you feeling strange. Not rested. Not calm. Just spent.

## A material with opinions

The quiet in making doesn't come from what you're looking at. It comes from a small negotiation between your hands and a material that has opinions.

Your fingertips are sending back a hundred reports a second. Pressure. Texture. Warmth. Give. Slight resistance that means the wood is dry, or the yarn is the right tension, or the clay is ready to move. That's a real job, and it takes up real room in your attention. There's no space left over for the loop of worry — the one that plays the same sentence from work again and again, the one that replays a conversation you wish had gone differently.

The material fills your hands with information, and the worry has nowhere to sit.

Glass has no grain. There's nothing there for your hands to learn. Your fingers were built to read the world through touch, and a screen gives them almost nothing to read.

## Tired isn't done

And then there's how it ends, or doesn't.

A row finishes. A bowl gets its last coat. A spoon comes off the knife smooth enough to stop. There's a moment when something is complete, and your body knows it. You set the thing down. You look at it. You're done, and you feel done — a specific, clean kind of satisfaction that has nothing to do with pride or accomplishment. It's just the feeling of finishing.

The screen doesn't finish. It was built not to. There's always one more post, one more article, one more refresh. So you stop when you're tired instead of when you're done, and those two feel very different in the body. Stopping from exhaustion leaves a residue. Stopping because something is complete leaves you settled.

In the morning, if you scrolled all evening, nothing sits on the table. Nothing shows where three hours of your one life went. If you made something — even something small, even something useless — it's there. Your hands have a witness.

## Something to hold

None of this is an argument against your phone. It's just noticing what it can't do. It can hold your eyes for hours and never once ask your hands for anything.

So tonight, before the glass, five minutes of something with a texture. Peel an apple in one long strip. Sand a scrap of wood. Wind a ball of yarn. Knead a small piece of dough. Fold a cloth slowly.

Not to accomplish anything. Not to improve yourself. Just to let your hands do something a screen has never once asked of them.

They've been waiting all day for something to hold.
