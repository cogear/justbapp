# Flow, and why it isn't productivity

*A quiet argument for losing time on purpose, with no outcome to show for it.*

## Two Hours, Gone

You know the feeling, even if you've never had a name for it.

You looked up and two hours were gone. The tea went cold beside you. Nobody asked you to do this. You did it anyway.

There's a word for that. Flow. It's a good word — accurate, even — but somewhere along the way it got borrowed by people who wanted to sell you a better Tuesday. A sharper morning. A more optimized you.

Something in you settled while you weren't looking. That's what I want to talk about. And about how badly we've learned to describe it.

Because here's the thing: we've been taught to treat flow like a tool. A lever you pull to get more done. And that framing, more than anything else, is what makes flow so hard to find.

## When Flow Got a Job

Somewhere along the way, flow became something you were supposed to engineer.

Get into your flow state. Protect your deep work. Find the zone, and get more out of the morning than you did yesterday.

But the moment flow has a job to do, it stops turning up. You can't concentrate very hard on not watching the clock. The watching is the thing that ruins it.

This is the paradox nobody mentions in the productivity podcasts. Flow is deeply allergic to being used. The second you pick it up as a strategy — the second you think, *I'm going to get into the zone and really knock this out* — you've already stepped outside of it. You've become the manager of the experience rather than the person inside it.

And flow doesn't work for managers. It only shows up for participants.

That's the whole trick, really. Flow isn't a method for getting somewhere. It's what happens in the gap when you've stopped trying to get anywhere at all.

## Just Hard Enough

Here's what it actually is, as far as I can tell.

The work is just hard enough to hold you. Not so easy your mind wanders off. Not so hard that your shoulders come up around your ears.

Your hands know roughly what comes next, so the thinking part of you isn't needed. And that voice — the one that narrates everything, that says *this is crooked, that's wrong, what are you doing with your life* — goes quiet. Not silenced. Not defeated. Just uninterested. Like it wandered into another room and found something else to poke at.

There's a particular quality to that silence. It's not the silence of meditation, where you're aware of the effort. It's the silence of being genuinely occupied. Something has moved into the room where the worrying usually sits, and the worrying can't get comfortable, so it leaves.

You're not resting, exactly. You're not achieving anything either. You're occupied in the old sense of the word — filled, taken up, inhabited.

## How You Know

The tell is simple.

When you stop, you don't feel like you got something done. You feel like you were somewhere, and now you're back.

And the thing in your hands might be lopsided. Too small. Not much use to anybody. Flow doesn't mind. It was never really about the object.

This is also why it can't be scheduled, or tracked, or improved. Anything you measure, you're standing outside of. And standing outside of it is the one place flow won't meet you.

Ask someone in it how it's going and they'll blink at you. There's no report to give. There's just the next stitch, the next pass of the blade, the next row. The question itself feels slightly absurd — like asking a river how the flowing is going.

We're so accustomed to narrating our experiences that we forget some experiences don't have a narration. They just have a rhythm.

## The Same Chair

So don't chase it.

Sit down with something small and repeated that your hands half-remember. Set it up so it's easy to begin — the tools out, the materials close, nothing between you and the first motion but a breath. Then give it forty minutes with nothing to prove.

Some evenings it comes and takes the whole night with it. You look up and the light has changed and you can't remember deciding anything for hours.

Some evenings you just work. A little bored, a little fidgety, checking the time. The stitches are fine but nothing special. You put it down early.

That's a fine evening too. It's the same chair. The same practice. The showing up is the thing — not the feeling that arrives sometimes when you do.

The hours you lose this way aren't lost. They're some of the only ones you ever fully spend.
