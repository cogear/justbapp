# Tea as ceremony

*A ceremony hidden in the thing you've already done a thousand times without noticing.*

## A thousand cups

You've made tea a thousand times. Kettle on. Bag in the cup. Water over the top. Two minutes of your life you would struggle to describe, because you weren't really there for them.

There's a ceremony hidden in that. Not the formal kind, with whisks and kneeling and years of study. The kind that asks nothing of you except that you show up for it.

Same leaves. Same water. Same chipped cup. The only difference is whether you were in the room while it happened.

## Tea as fuel

Almost every cup of tea I've made, I've made while doing something else. Reading with one hand. Answering a message. Standing at the counter already three steps into the rest of the afternoon.

Which is how you end up with a mug going cold behind your laptop. Made, forgotten, reheated — which never works — and finally poured down the sink.

Tea as fuel. A delivery system for warmth and caffeine. There's nothing wrong with that. It's just not anything. It passes through you without leaving much behind.

## What ceremony actually is

Ceremony sounds like it needs equipment. A special pot, a tatami mat, somebody who knows the right way. It doesn't. It needs a sequence, and your attention inside it.

That's all a ceremony is. The same small steps, in the same order, done on purpose. The order matters because you decided it matters. Nobody else has to agree.

So: warm the pot first. Swirl hot water around the inside, pour it out. It barely changes the tea. That's part of why I do it — it's for me, not for the tea.

None of it makes better tea, really. That's the freedom in it. When the point isn't the result, the doing gets to be the point.

This is what's easy to miss about ceremony. We think of it as something added, something extra we have to bring in from outside. But the steps are already there. You were always going to boil water, pour it, wait, drink. Ceremony is just choosing to be present for the parts you usually skip past.

## You can't hurry water

The kettle takes as long as it takes. You cannot hurry water.

So I stand there. One hand flat on the counter. Listening to it climb — that low roar building, then a softer sound, then the click. Not waiting for it, exactly. Just there while it happens.

This is the part that feels hardest. Not the pouring or the steeping, but the standing still. The body wants to wander. The mind is already in the next room. But water has its own pace, and part of the practice is letting it keep that pace.

Then the steeping. Three minutes where the only thing asked of you is to leave it alone. No stirring. No lifting the lid to check. The water is doing the work. You don't have to help it. You just have to not interfere.

Then the pour. The spoon set down on the saucer. The same cup you always use, because you always use it.

## Six minutes

And then you drink it. Standing at the window, or sitting down properly, holding the cup with both hands because it's warm and your hands are glad of it.

Nothing was produced. You'll be thirsty again within the hour. There's a cup to rinse, and you'll rinse it.

But for six or seven minutes you did one thing, and you were there for the whole of it. That's the ceremony. It's small, and it's available again tomorrow.

The cup doesn't care whether you were present for it. The tea doesn't know the difference. That's what makes it safe to practice on. Nothing is at stake. You can't get it wrong. You can only be there, or not — and you can try again tomorrow, with the same kettle and the same chipped cup and the same ordinary six minutes.
