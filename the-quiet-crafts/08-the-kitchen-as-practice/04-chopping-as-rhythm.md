# Chopping as rhythm

*Steel meeting wood — a thousand-year-old sound that asks nothing of you but attention.*

Somewhere right now, in a kitchen you'll never see, someone is making a sound that hasn't changed in a thousand years. Steel meeting wood. A pause. Then again. Then again.

It isn't music. It's closer to a metronome — the plainest kind of time-keeping there is, and one of the oldest. A knife. A board. A pile of something that needs to be smaller than it is. That's the whole instrument. Most kitchens already have one.

## The part before

Most of us treat chopping as the part before the cooking. The toll you pay at the gate. Ten minutes of standing between you and the part that feels like actually making something.

So we hurry. We've all seen the videos — hands blurring, blade a flicker, an onion gone in four seconds. We hold ourselves against that invisible standard and come up short, every time.

The food lands in the pan the same either way. But our shoulders are up near our ears, and we've spent the whole time somewhere else — already at the stove, already at the table, already done.

## A tempo you can hold

Here's the turn. Chopping isn't a task with a finish line hanging over it. It's a rhythm. And the thing about a rhythm is that it has a tempo you can actually hold.

So find the speed where the sound stays even. Not fast. Even. Listen for the gaps between the cuts and keep them the same length. Your hands will find it before your head does. This is something the body knows that the mind doesn't — that steadiness is not the same thing as speed, and that an even pace feels different from a rushed one. Not just in the hands. In the chest.

Give it a few minutes and something loosens. You're not thinking about the onion anymore. You're not thinking much at all. You're keeping time.

## Where the rhythm lives

Set it up so the rhythm has somewhere to live. A board that doesn't slide. A knife sharp enough to do its own work. A bowl for the scraps so you're not stopping to clear space every thirty seconds. These aren't professional tricks. They're just small kindnesses for the version of you that's about to stand there and do one thing for a while.

Then the small motions, the ones your body will learn without being told. Knuckles curled, fingertips back. The tip of the blade staying near the wood, rocking more than chopping. Same arc, same sound, over and over. There's a reason this grip has been passed down for centuries — not because it's elegant, but because it lets your hand forget it's working.

The pieces will not match. Some fat, some slivers. Let them. Nobody is grading the carrots, and the pot has never once complained.

And when you notice your mind has wandered off somewhere far from the kitchen — good. That's the rhythm doing exactly what it does. It gives you a place to come back to. The sound is the anchor. The motion is the anchor. You drift, you return, you keep chopping.

## More than you need

Some nights I chop more than the recipe asks for. Not to prep, not to save time later. Just because I wasn't ready to stop.

There's not much left in a day that asks so little of you. Nothing to submit. Nobody watching. No product at the end, no skill to level up, no one to be impressed. Just attention, a steady hand, and a sound that comes back every second or so.

You are allowed to go slowly. You are allowed to like the sound. You are allowed to make the pile bigger than you need.

The next time you pick up a knife, try this: don't think about the meal yet. Don't think about the clock. Just listen for the sound, and find the pace where it goes even. Stay there as long as you want. The cooking will wait.
