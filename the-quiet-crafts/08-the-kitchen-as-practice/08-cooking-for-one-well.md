# Cooking for one, well

*The quiet practice of feeding yourself like you count.*

## Standing at the counter

There's a meal that happens when nobody's watching. You're standing at the counter with the fridge door still open, scanning the shelves like something will appear. Crackers. Cereal at nine at night. Something eaten cold, straight from the container it came in.

I'm not here to call that a failure. Some nights that's exactly the right dinner. You're tired, the day was long, and a handful of almonds standing at the counter is honest food.

But I want to talk about the other nights. The ones where you're hungry, and it's still early enough, and you could make something. And you don't. Not because you can't. Because it's just you.

## Not worth the trouble

Somewhere along the way we picked up the idea that cooking is something you do for other people. That effort needs a witness. That a real dinner, made for yourself alone, is a lot of trouble for one.

So we skip it. Not because we're always tired, though we usually are. Because it doesn't feel worth doing. One person doesn't seem like enough reason.

There's a strange math in it. We'll spend an hour on dinner for four without blinking. But twenty minutes on dinner for one feels like fuss. Like it's too much for just you.

Where does that come from? Maybe from growing up in kitchens where someone cooked because people were coming. Maybe from restaurants, where cooking is a performance. Maybe just from the quiet assumption that feeding yourself is maintenance, and feeding others is care.

But you are someone worth cooking for.

## Enough reason

You're the person you feed most often. Thousands of meals over a lifetime, and almost every one of them is yours. That's not a small account to keep.

And there's a freedom in cooking for one that cooking for a table never has. Nobody to please. No preferences to work around, no allergies to remember, no timing to coordinate. You can make exactly what you want, exactly how you like it.

It can be odd. Four ingredients. Too much garlic. A plate of roasted vegetables with a runny egg and too much salt. Nobody's grading it. A meal that would embarrass you in front of company can be perfect on a Tuesday.

One is enough reason.

## At the scale of one

So cook the small amount properly. Not a family recipe sadly halved, standing in the kitchen feeling the absence of the other three portions. A single portion, built from the ground up for one.

Use the small pan. The one that feels almost too small, like it's meant for something unserious. It's not. It's the right size.

Learn a few things at the scale of one, and cook them until your hands know them. A single egg scrambled slowly with butter. A small bowl of pasta with olive oil and lemon. A piece of fish in a pan with salt. Toast with whatever you have. Keep it simple and keep it yours.

Then put it on a plate. Not because plates are virtuous, or because real meals require real dishes. A plate just says: this is a meal, and you're the one having it. Eating from the cooking vessel, standing up, is fine sometimes. But a plate makes it a small event instead of a non-event.

Sit down. At a table, if you have one. Phone face down. Ten minutes of eating something you made with your own hands. That's the whole thing. It doesn't need to be more than that.

## The repetition is the quiet part

Do it more than once. The same three dishes, over and over, until you stop reading recipes and just cook. When your hands know the motions — how the oil sounds when it's ready, how the garlic looks before it burns, how long your stove takes to get where you want it — something settles.

This is what the kitchen gives you when you stay with it. Not a skill you can display. Not something to photograph. A particular kind of quiet that comes from work your body knows how to do without asking your mind to manage it.

The repetition is the quiet part. The same small steps, the same pan, the same Tuesday. Nothing new, nothing impressive. Just the steady rhythm of making something warm for yourself, again.

## An ordinary kindness

None of this will make you better at anything. It won't show up anywhere. Nobody's going to know you stood at the stove for twenty minutes on a Wednesday and made yourself something warm.

But the kitchen gets quiet in a good way when you're cooking for just yourself. No timing to manage, nobody waiting, nothing to get right for anyone. Just a small pan, the smell of it, and your own steady attention.

It's an ordinary kindness you can hand yourself most nights of the week. Set the table anyway. One place is a place.
