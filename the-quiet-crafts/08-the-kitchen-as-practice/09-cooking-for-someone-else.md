# Cooking for someone else

*The oldest kindness isn't complicated — it's just paying attention to a person and handing over what you made.*

## Somebody's coming over

Somebody's coming over on Thursday. And the first thing you feel — before the pleasure of it — is a small tightening. A little dread you'd rather not admit to.

Because somewhere along the way, cooking for another person stopped being feeding them and became showing them something. Proving you're the kind of person who can host. Proving you have good taste, or a clean enough kitchen, or a recipe up your sleeve that's worth the drive across town.

That's a shame. Because underneath the dread, you were about to do one of the oldest, plainest kindnesses people do for each other. Not a performance. A meal.

## Being graded

Watch how it escalates. Three courses instead of one. A recipe you've never tried, chosen last night at eleven o'clock, scrolling on your phone in bed. A timeline that only works if nothing goes wrong. And something always goes wrong — the onions take longer than they should, the pan isn't hot enough, you realize you're out of the one thing the recipe said was essential.

So by the time they knock, you're behind. Hot. Apologizing before they've taken their coat off. You spend the evening at the stove with your back to the room. They spend it waiting for you to arrive.

The food comes out fine. It usually does. But nobody quite got fed. You were being graded all night, and you were the only one holding the pen.

We do this to ourselves. We turn a quiet act of care into a test we've written for ourselves and then panic about failing.

## Different jobs

So here's the turn. Nobody sat down at your table hoping to be impressed. They came hoping to be looked after for an hour or two. Those are different jobs.

Impressing someone is hard. It requires novelty, skill, a bit of luck. Looking after someone isn't complicated. It's usually the thing you already know how to cook. Made a little slower, with a particular person in mind.

Attention is the ingredient. The rest is just groceries.

You remember she doesn't like fennel. You keep the chili on the table instead of in the pot so he can decide. You cut the bread the way he cuts it at home. Small things. That's the whole craft of it.

The craft isn't in the complexity of what you make. It's in the particularity. You cooked *this* for *them*. You thought about their mouth, their preferences, the way they hold a fork. That thinking is the practice.

## How to do it

So: cook something your hands already know. Something you could make while holding a conversation. Leave your attention free for the person instead of the recipe page.

Start early. Let it sit. Food that waits well — a braise, a pot of beans, a soup, a stew — is food that lets you sit down too. And sitting down is most of what they came for. They didn't come to watch you cook. They came to be near you while you both eat.

Then set it down without a speech. Don't list what went wrong. Don't apologize for the bread being a little dark on top. Don't explain that you meant to get the other kind of salt. Just: here, I made this, eat while it's hot.

And don't wait for the compliment. It's nice when it comes. It isn't the point. The point already happened — at the stove, an hour ago, when you were thinking about them.

## Hand it over

There's a very old quiet in feeding somebody. Older than recipes, older than restaurants, older than cookbooks and cooking shows. Your hands doing plain work on purpose, for a person you're glad to know.

You don't have to be a good cook to be good at this. You have to pay attention, and then hand it over, and then let it be enough.

Somebody's coming over Thursday. Make the thing you always make.
