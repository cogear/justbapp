# Cooking without a deadline

*The kind of cooking where nothing is due and no one is waiting.*

## Seven o'clock

Most of the cooking most of us do has a deadline. Someone is hungry. Something is due. The clock on the stove isn't decoration — it's a countdown.

Timer on the phone. Oven heating while you're still in your coat. Food as the last obstacle standing between you and the end of the day.

I want to talk about the other kind of cooking. The kind where nothing is due. Where you walk into the kitchen on a slow afternoon and no one at all is waiting for a plate.

This isn't about getting better at cooking. It's not about meal prep or weeknight efficiency or becoming someone who posts their dinners online. It's about what happens to a kitchen when you take the urgency out of it.

## Feeding the clock

When there's a deadline, the food becomes a task. And you're not really in the room. You're a half hour ahead of yourself, already standing at the moment the plate lands on the table.

So you chop faster than you'd like. You read the recipe the way you read an exit sign — for the shortest path out. And when it's over, you can't remember a single minute of it. You ate. You cleaned. You don't know what happened.

That's not the cooking's fault. Cooking is one of the oldest, most forgiving things a person can do with an hour. The problem is the clock we brought in with us — the one that says this has to be done, and done soon, so we can move on to whatever is next.

The kitchen absorbs that urgency. The onions get chopped rough. The pan gets too hot because you didn't want to wait. You're present in the room but absent from the moment, because the moment you're actually living in is the one where everyone is already eating.

## Nothing is due

Take the clock out and something shifts. The work stops being the thing in the way of dinner. The work becomes the reason you're standing there at all.

Your hands slow to the speed they actually want to move at. You notice the sound the pan makes when the heat is right — a low, patient hum. You taste something halfway through, just to know what halfway tastes like. You stir not because the recipe says to but because you want to feel the weight of the spoon against the bottom of the pot.

Nothing is due. No one is waiting. That's the entire instruction, and it's harder to follow than it sounds.

Most of us have forgotten what it feels like to do something with our hands without a reason behind it. We've been trained to justify the time. But the kitchen is one of the few places left where the body still knows what to do — peel, stir, chop, wait — and the knowing is enough.

## How to start

If you want to try it, start here. Eat something first. Cook when you're not hungry. Hunger is a deadline with a body attached — it makes you hurry without ever telling you it's hurrying you.

Then choose something small and slow. Not a whole meal — one thing. Something that takes longer than it strictly needs to. A pot of beans. A loaf of bread. A single onion, caramelized past the point of reason. And then stand there while it takes that long.

Stir when it doesn't need stirring. Taste as you go. Wipe the counter in the middle for no reason at all. Let the whole thing be far too much effort for the size of what you end up with.

You're not building a skill. You're not working toward anything. You're just letting your hands do what they already know how to do, at the pace they want to do it.

## Permission

You might end up eating it standing at the counter. You might end up with something that isn't very good. Both are fine. Neither one was the point.

The point was the hour. Your hands busy with something warm. Your mind quiet in a way it rarely gets to be. Nothing at all to show anyone afterward.

The stove will wait as long as you want it to. Almost nothing else in your life does.
