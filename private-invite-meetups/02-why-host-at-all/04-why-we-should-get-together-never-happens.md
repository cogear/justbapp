# Why "we should get together" never happens

*The sentence is sincere. The sentence is also the reason nothing happens.*

## The sidewalk goodbye

Someone hugs you goodbye on the sidewalk and says, we should get together. And you say, yes, we really should. And here's the thing worth noticing: you both mean it. Completely. Nobody in that moment is lying.

And then nothing happens.

Not out of coldness. Not because anyone stopped caring. The weeks just close over it, quietly, the way water closes over a stone. You think about them sometimes. You assume they think about you. Neither of you does anything about it, and eventually the gap gets wide enough that reaching across it starts to feel like a big deal, which makes it even less likely that anyone will.

## A sentence with no owner

That sentence has no owner. It's addressed to both of you, which means it's addressed to nobody.

It never lands on a calendar. Never joins a list. No one wakes up on a Tuesday and finds it waiting for them.

It isn't a plan. It's a feeling wearing the shape of a plan. A kind way to end a conversation. It does its job right there on the sidewalk, and then it evaporates, and both of you walk away feeling like something was arranged.

Nothing was arranged.

Eight months later you run into them at the market. You're genuinely glad. You say the same sentence. You both mean it again. Some friendships spend their entire lives inside that loop — warm, real, and permanently stalled.

## Vague is safe

Here's the part we don't say out loud. Vague is comfortable.

If you never name a day, they can never tell you they're busy. Nothing gets refused, because nothing was actually offered. That sentence can't be turned down. That's its appeal.

It also can't come true.

Safety and possibility are sitting on opposite sides of the same door. So somebody has to trade one for the other. Somebody has to pick a square on the calendar, say it out loud, and let it hang in the air for a second without knowing the answer.

That small exposure is the whole cost. It feels bigger than it is. A lot bigger. It can feel like you're asking for something, or being presumptuous, or risking a small rejection that will somehow reveal that the friendship was thinner than you thought.

But it's not any of those things. It's the difference between a warm feeling and an evening that actually exists.

## Name the day

The fix is unglamorous. A day, a time, a place.

Thursday the fourteenth, my kitchen, seven o'clock, I'm making far too much pasta.

That's it. That's the entire technique.

Notice what changed. It isn't warmer. It isn't more heartfelt than the sidewalk version. It's just answerable. Now they can say yes, or no, or not that week, what about the one after.

Sometimes it's a no. A no isn't a failed invitation. A no is a real answer, which is more than that old sentence ever managed to give you in eight months. A no is information. It means you can stop wondering and move on to the next person, or try again next month. The vague sentence gives you nothing to work with. A no gives you something.

And you don't need a reason. No birthday, no news, no theme. No occasion to justify it. I'd like to see you is a complete reason, and it always has been.

## What gets in the way

Most people hold back from naming a day because they think hosting means performing. They imagine a clean house, a planned menu, good lighting, conversation that flows. They imagine they need to be the kind of person who is naturally good at this.

But hosting isn't a personality. It's a craft. You learn it by doing it badly a few times and noticing that nobody minded.

The house doesn't have to be clean. The food doesn't have to impress. Far too much pasta is not just acceptable — it's ideal. It says: I'm not trying to do something here, I'm just feeding you. Frequency beats ambition every time. One small ordinary evening matters more than one elaborate one nobody has the energy to repeat.

And here's the thing about reciprocity. People worry about keeping score. Did I have them over last, or did they have me? Is it my turn? But scorekeeping is what kills reciprocity, not what sustains it. When someone hosts you, you don't owe them a proportional evening. You just owe them the same willingness to pick a day when the time comes. The point isn't balance. The point is that the door keeps swinging open from both sides.

## On the other side

Most of the people you miss are not far away. They're not angry with you. They're not waiting for an apology or an explanation.

They're on the other side of one sentence nobody ever finished.

You can be the one who finishes it. Not because you're the organised friend, or the natural host, or the one with the nice apartment. Just because you noticed that someone has to, and nobody had.

So don't say we should get together.

Say Thursday.
