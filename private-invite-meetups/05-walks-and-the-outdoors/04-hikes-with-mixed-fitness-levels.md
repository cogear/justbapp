# Hikes with mixed fitness levels

*Why the hardest part of a group hike isn't the hill — and how a host can fix it without making it weird.*

## The back of the group

On any group hike, somebody ends up at the back. They know it. They can feel the gap opening up ahead of them, and they can hear their own breathing get loud.

And the hard part isn't the hill. It's cresting it and finding everyone already rested, sitting on rocks, being patient about you.

Nobody meant to do that. That's the strange thing. It's a kindness that lands like a verdict.

The people up ahead aren't thoughtless. They stopped because they didn't know what else to do. They waited because they didn't want to abandon anyone. And yet somehow, the waiting itself becomes the thing that stings — because it turns a shared walk into a visible ranking of who's holding everyone up.

The person at the back already knows they're last. They don't need the rocks and the rest to confirm it.

## Easy hike, little loop

It usually starts with the invitation. We say: easy hike, little loop, nothing crazy. We mean it warmly. But warm and vague is still vague, and vague is what does the damage.

Because someone with bad knees, or a new baby, or a body that just doesn't do hills, can't make a decision with that. So they come and they hope. Around the second climb, hoping stops working.

They won't tell you. They'll thank you at the trailhead and mean it. And then next time you ask, they'll be busy, and you'll never know why.

This is the part that quietly breaks group hiking. Not the terrain, not the weather, not the logistics. It's the slow erosion of your circle, one vague invite at a time. People don't drop out dramatically. They just become "busy." And you keep planning the same hikes for a shrinking group, wondering where everyone went.

## Who sets the pace

So here's the part a host can actually change. A group doesn't have a natural pace. The pace is whatever the person in front decides. That's it. That's the whole mechanism.

Which means you get to choose it. And the choice that makes a hike work is to set it for whoever's slowest — not as charity, just as the plan.

Then go walk back there. Not to check on anybody. To talk. The back of the group stops being the back the moment the host is standing in it.

Almost everything else follows from that one decision.

When the host is at the back, the pace sorts itself out without anyone negotiating. Nobody has to ask the fast people to slow down, because the fast people are simply ahead, doing their thing, and the group is held together by the person who chose to stand where the group needed standing. You're not managing anyone. You're just placing yourself.

## Small craft

The rest is small craft. Put real numbers in the invite. Five kilometres. One steep bit near the start. Rocky underfoot. Two hours, probably three, because we'll stop a lot.

Numbers aren't discouraging. They're respectful. They let a person say yes to the right thing, or say not this one, and stay in the group either way. A vague invite forces people to guess. A specific invite lets them decide.

Choose a shape with a way out. Out-and-back means anyone can turn around whenever they like and it costs them nothing. A loop with a lower path means there are two ways home. Nobody has to announce a retreat or feel like they're quitting. They just take the other fork.

Put stops where there's a reason for them. A bridge. A view. A place to fill water. Then nobody has to ask for a break — the trail asked on their behalf. This matters more than you'd think. The person who's struggling the most is the person least likely to say "can we stop." So build the stops in. Make them structural, not requested.

And when the last person arrives, don't applaud. Don't say you made it. Hand over the water and keep talking about the dog you were arguing about ten minutes ago. The greatest gift you can give someone who's been struggling is the sense that their arrival is unremarkable. That they were always part of the conversation, even when they were a few minutes behind.

## More of your people

The fast people can go be fast another weekend, with each other. It's a good day out. It's just a different day out than this one. Nobody is being punished. You're just being clear about what today is for.

Do this a handful of times and something shifts. The one who kept saying no says maybe. Then yes. Then they start bringing the good snacks and choosing the route.

A hike that fits everybody isn't a watered-down hike. It's the one where more of your people are actually there. And that's the whole point of going together in the first place.
