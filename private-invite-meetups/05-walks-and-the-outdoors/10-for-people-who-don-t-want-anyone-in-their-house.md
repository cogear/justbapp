# For people who don't want anyone in their house

*A door you never open can still be the start of a circle.*

## The Door You Don't Open

There's a picture that comes with the word hosting. Your door, your kitchen, your kettle, people in your rooms. Maybe a table with food on it. Maybe a hallway where coats go. It's a warm picture, and for some people it's an easy one. For others it sits there like a locked gate.

If that's never going to be you, it can feel like the whole thing is closed off. Hosting is what other people do. People with clean houses. People with spare rooms. People whose kitchen doesn't have a stack of mail on the counter and a dishwasher that's been making a noise since March.

So you don't invite anyone. Not because you don't want them — you do — but because the only version you can imagine starts with tidying a house you'd rather nobody saw. And that tidying feels like a mountain, and the mountain never gets climbed, and the weeks go by.

You don't need the house. Hosting needs a time, a place, and someone willing to name both out loud. None of those things live indoors.

## Reasons That Don't Need Fixing

Maybe you share the place with someone who'd rather you didn't have people over. Maybe it's small. Maybe the landlord is strict, or there's a baby asleep, or someone at home who needs it quiet. Maybe you rent a room and the common areas aren't really yours to offer.

Or maybe it's simpler than all of that. You just don't want people in your rooms. Home is where you stop being looked at, where you take off the version of yourself that the world requires, and you'd like to keep it that way. That's not antisocial. That's a boundary, and a reasonable one.

And plenty of people have quietly stopped seeing anyone because the only door they could imagine opening was their own. It's a strange thing to lose friendships over. Carpet. A sofa that's seen better days. A bathroom that's fine but not, you know, guest-ready.

None of that is a flaw to work on. It's a fact about you and your life. And a fact you accept is much easier to build around than one you keep apologising for. The apologising is what actually drains you — not the hosting, not the friendship, but the low hum of feeling like you're failing at something you never signed up for in the first place.

## An Act, Not an Address

Because hosting was never really about the address. Strip it down and it's one person deciding something will happen, and then telling other people they're wanted there. That's the whole thing. The rest is decoration.

A bench does that. The bottom of a hill at nine on Saturday morning. The far gate of the park, and you'll be the one in the green coat. A café that you don't own and don't need to — you just need to show up first and stay until the last person leaves.

Someone was invited. Someone would be missed if they didn't turn up. That obligation is the engine of the whole thing, and it runs perfectly well in the open air. It runs on a footpath. It runs on a bench by the river. It runs anywhere someone is willing to say "I'll be there, and I'd like you to come."

## The Host's Work, Outdoors

So do the work. Be specific about the place. Not the park — the gate on the east side of the park, by the big oak, the one you can see from the bus stop. Vague plans fall apart quietly in group chats. "Let's hang out sometime" is not a plan. It's a feeling, and feelings don't get people out of bed on a Saturday.

Arrive first. Standing there before anyone else is most of the job. It means nobody walks up to an empty spot and wonders whether they got it wrong, whether they're early, whether everyone changed plans and forgot to tell them. Being first is the simplest way to make a place feel held.

Bring slightly more than you need. A spare flask, extra chocolate, a bag for wet things, an extra pair of gloves in winter. A small surplus is what welcome looks like when there's no kitchen to put it in. It says "I thought about you being cold" without saying anything at all.

Introduce the people who don't know each other. Say when it'll end, so nobody has to guess whether leaving is rude or staying is expected. Then do it again in three weeks. Same gate, same hour. Not because it has to be identical, but because rhythm is what turns a one-time thing into a thing that exists.

## Your Door Stays Shut

That's how it becomes a thing rather than an event. People stop waiting for an invitation and start assuming it. Someone texts the group: "Saturday, same time?" And you realise you haven't had to persuade anyone of anything for weeks. Which is the whole point of any of this.

You can be the one everybody knows to meet, and never hand a single person a mug in your kitchen. Your door stays shut. Your circle doesn't.

Keep your rooms. Offer your time instead. That's a whole offer, not a substitute for one. The walk won't be less than the dinner. It will just be different, and probably longer, and maybe more honest — because nobody's worrying about the washing up.
