# The Sunday walk

*A time and a place that keeps existing — so your friendships don't have to wait for a plan that never comes.*

## The corner by the bakery

There's a corner near my house, next to the bakery. Sunday morning, nine o'clock. I stand there whether anyone else shows up or not.

That's the whole design.

Nobody RSVPs. There's no date to coordinate, no poll to send, no confirmation to chase. It isn't really an event at all. It's a time and a place that keeps existing without me asking anything of anyone.

This started because I got tired of trying to schedule my friends. Texting back and forth, hunting for a Saturday that worked for five people, watching the energy drain out of the idea before anyone even met. It turned out the fix wasn't a better plan. It was a plan I never had to make twice.

## Where plans go to die

Most friendships don't end. They stall in the group chat.

Someone says we should get together. Everyone agrees warmly. Then come the polls, the maybes, the three-week hunt for a date that works. By the time a date is found, the wanting has gone out of it. It quietly dies. Not because nobody cares. Because deciding, over and over, is more work than showing up.

And the deciding usually lands on the same person. The one who organises. Eventually they get tired, and the whole thing goes quiet.

That's the part nobody talks about. The friction in friendship isn't the friendship. It's the logistics. It's the repeated, unpaid labour of being the person who holds the calendar. People don't stop caring about each other. They stop wanting to be the one who sends the first message. So the real question isn't how to care more. It's how to remove the part that exhausts everyone.

## Decide once

A standing walk removes the deciding. Same day, same time, same corner. Chosen once, then never again. Nobody negotiates. You come or you don't, and either way it's happening next week.

That's hosting as craft, not personality. You spend the effort once, up front, and then repetition carries it. You're not throwing something. You're keeping a time. There's a big difference. A dinner party has a before and an after. A standing walk just has a during, and it comes back around every seven days.

The first month it might be you and one other person, walking in silence for ten minutes. That isn't a slow start. That's the thing already working. You're building something that doesn't depend on turnout, and that's the only kind of thing that survives long enough to matter.

## How to keep it going

Send the same short message every week. Sunday, nine, the corner by the bakery. Don't dress it up. Don't ask who's coming. A question demands an answer, and answers are what people avoid. A statement just sits there, available whenever someone needs it.

Then the hard part. Go on the weeks nobody comes. Walk it alone. And when you see them next, don't mention it. Nobody should ever have to pay for missing a Sunday. The moment someone feels guilty for not showing up, the invitation starts to curdle. It becomes an obligation, and obligations are what people avoid.

Don't keep score. The friend who turns up twice a year isn't failing you. A standing invitation only stays warm as long as it's free to decline. The day you start counting who came and who didn't is the day it starts to die. That's not easy. You'll want to notice. You'll want to tally. Resist it.

Give it an ending, too. A bench, a bakery, coffee for whoever has time. People relax when they know where the thing stops. An open-ended walk sounds lovely in theory. In practice, it makes people hesitate, because they don't know what they're agreeing to. A walk that ends at coffee is a walk with a shape. You can decide to go in the first five minutes, because you know it's only the first five minutes of something with a known end.

## What frequency gives you

A year in, you'll notice something odd. You won't remember any single walk. They blur together. But you'll know these people's lives in a detailed, unremarkable way you can't get from dinner twice a year.

You'll know that someone's knee is bothering them. You'll know their kid changed schools. You'll know they've been sleeping badly, or that they finally finished the thing they'd been avoiding. Not because they told you in a grand update. Because you walked next to them twenty times and it came out in pieces, the way real life does.

That's what frequency gives you and ambition never will. Not a memorable evening. A place in someone's week that they don't have to think about.

You're allowed to make something small and keep making it. You don't need a better idea. You need the same one, next Sunday. The bakery will still be there.
