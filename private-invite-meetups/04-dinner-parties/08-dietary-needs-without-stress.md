# Dietary needs without stress

*A dietary need is information, not a problem to solve perfectly.*

## The small worry

Somewhere between sending the invitation and standing in the grocery aisle, a small worry shows up. What if I make the wrong thing. What if someone sits down and can't eat any of it.

And on the other end of that invitation, someone is drafting a text. Deleting it. Rewriting it so it sounds lighter than it feels. *Sorry to be a pain — I can't do dairy.*

That text takes longer to send than it should. Because the person on the other end already feels like they're complicating something that was supposed to be simple. And the host already feels like they might get it wrong. Two people, each holding a small worry, each trying not to make it the other person's problem.

## Two ways it goes sideways

There are two ways this goes sideways.

The first is never asking, and finding out at the table. When all you can offer someone is bread and an apology, and the evening has a crack in it that nobody quite knows how to paper over.

The second is asking so hard it becomes an event. Three follow-up questions. A separate menu. A second pan. Your guest watching you rearrange the entire evening around their body, feeling the weight of that attention whether they wanted it or not.

Both come from the same idea — that a dietary need is a problem you have to solve perfectly. It isn't. It's information. Like the time. Like the address. You wouldn't build an evening around the fact that someone arrives at seven. You'd just plan for it.

## Ask once

So ask once. Early. In the same breath as the time and the address, before anyone has to work up the nerve to tell you.

*Anything I should know about food?* That's the whole question. Sent to everybody, not just the person you're wondering about. This matters more than it seems. When you ask only the person you suspect, you've already made it a thing. When you ask everyone, it's just part of how you host. Normal. Ordinary. The same as asking about arrival time.

Then believe the answer. You don't need the reason behind it. Allergy, religion, illness, or just plain don't-like — none of that changes what you do next. Someone told you what they can eat. Good. You don't need to understand it. You just need to hear it.

## Build backwards

Now build backwards. Take the meal you were already planning and find the version where most of it works for everyone at the table. You're usually closer than you think.

The cheese moves to a small bowl beside the salad instead of through it. The sauce goes in a jug on the side. Butter becomes olive oil, and nobody notices but you. The bread stays. The dessert gets a fruit option that was already on your list anyway.

Most of the time you're not making two dinners. You're making one dinner with a small adjustment in how it's assembled. The difference between a meal someone can't eat and one they can is often just a matter of where the ingredient sits — mixed in, or offered alongside.

Then put everything on the table at once. No announcements. No *this one's yours.* Nobody should have to eat dinner while being explained to the room. If something's uncertain, say it quietly, once, on the way past. *There's cream in that one.* Then leave it alone and let them choose.

A guest who has to manage a dietary restriction all day, every day, does not need to manage it differently at your table. They need it to be less work, not more.

## Fed, not handled

Here's what your guest actually carries home. Not the menu. Not the fact that you made a special plate. The fact that they ate the same dinner as everybody else, and nobody made a moment of it.

That's a kind of welcome you can't fake and don't have to perform. It doesn't require effort so much as restraint — the willingness to let something be ordinary that the world keeps making extraordinary.

Ask early. Believe what you hear. Cook something most people can eat. Put it on the table and let it be dinner.

Everyone at your table gets to be fed. Including you.
