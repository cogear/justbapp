# Potlucks, coordinated

*How to feed a table together without leaving dinner to chance.*

## Nobody brought dinner

A potluck can fail in a very specific way. Everybody brings something. Everybody is generous. And somehow, at seven o'clock, nobody brought dinner.

It usually starts with two words. Said kindly, meant kindly. *Bring whatever.*

Chips. Another bag of chips. Three bottles of wine. And one gorgeous cake, for nine people who never got a real meal.

The table looks full and the table is empty. That's the particular strangeness of a potluck gone sideways. There was no shortage of effort or goodwill. There was just no organizing principle. And goodwill, it turns out, doesn't assemble itself into a meal.

## Six people guessing

*Bring whatever* sounds like generosity. Usually it's a decision handed off. And your friend, standing in a store aisle after work, doesn't want that decision.

So they guess. And they guess safe, because nobody wants to be the one who got it wrong. Safe is chips. Safe is wine. Safe is whatever they saw someone bring last time.

That's how a table tilts. Not from laziness. From six kind people all guessing in the same direction. Nobody did anything wrong. Everyone was being considerate. Considerate people, acting independently, tend to converge on the easiest option. That's not a character flaw. That's just how uncoordinated decisions work.

And you're in the kitchen, cooking around a hole you can't measure. You can't plan a meal out of fog. You don't know if you're the main course or the side dish or just the person who provided the venue. So you cook something big to be safe, and now you've done all the cooking anyway, plus you've got three bags of chips and nothing to eat them with.

## A job, not a choice

Here's the turn. A potluck doesn't divide the hosting into six pieces. You're still the host. It just means you're not doing all the cooking.

Which means the coordinating is yours. And asking for something specific isn't bossy. It's the kindest thing on the list. You're not imposing. You're removing a question.

*Could you bring a green salad?* That one sentence ends forty minutes of standing in a store, wondering. It gives someone a job instead of a choice.

Most people would rather have a job than a choice. A job they can do on the way over. A job they can feel good about completing. A job that lets them walk in the door and say *I brought the salad* instead of *I brought... this, I hope it's enough, I didn't know what you needed.*

This is the part of hosting that doesn't get talked about enough. The skill isn't in the cooking. It's in the assigning. It's in knowing what the meal needs and matching those needs to the people coming. Someone who works late gets something small and easy. Someone who loves to bake gets dessert. Someone who's nervous about cooking gets ice and lemons. You're not delegating. You're distributing care in a way that actually lands.

## How you actually do it

So here's the shape of it. You make one warm thing that holds the middle of the meal. That's yours, and it's enough. A pot of chili. A tray of roasted vegetables. Something with weight and warmth that says *this is dinner* when people sit down.

Then you hand out the rest, by name, out loud. Salad to one person. Bread to another. Something to eat standing up before dinner. Dessert to whoever loves dessert.

And for the friend coming straight from work: ice. Lemons. A bag of something crunchy. A real job, sized to a real day.

Keep the list where you can see it. If something's still missing two days out, say so. That's not pressure. That's information. *Hey, we still don't have anyone bringing bread — want to grab a loaf?* is so much easier than *bring whatever* and hoping.

And when someone asks whether store-bought is fine — yes. Right away. No pause in your voice for them to hear. The pause is where shame lives. Don't leave space for it.

## Made together

What shows up is mismatched. Different bowls, different dishes, one thing still in its plastic container. It looks like a table people made together, because it is. Nobody's food is being judged. It's just dinner, assembled.

Don't keep score. Nobody wins a potluck. The minute you start ranking dishes, your friends stop being guests and become contributors. Reciprocity matters — people want to give, want to participate, want to feel like they showed up for the group. But the moment you start tracking who brought the expensive cheese last time and who just brought chips, you've turned a dinner into a ledger. That kills the thing you're trying to grow.

Ask clearly. Cook one thing. Let the rest walk through the door. Then do it again soon — before you've had time to turn it into an occasion. Frequency beats ambition. A casual potluck every few weeks builds more warmth than one elaborate dinner every six months. The repetition is what makes people comfortable. The second time, they know the rhythm. The third time, they don't ask if store-bought is fine. The fourth time, they text you first: *What should I bring?*

That's the sound of hosting working. Not a perfect table. Just a table that keeps happening.
