# Backyard volleyball

*The net is the only invitation you need to give.*

## A line down the middle

Put a net up in a yard, and the yard changes. It stops being grass. It becomes a room with two halves and a line down the middle.

You don't have to explain it. People walk in, see the net, and start looking for someone to stand next to. A net is an invitation you don't have to say out loud. It tells people what's going to happen here, and that they're allowed to join.

This is what hosting looks like when it's working well — not a performance, not a schedule, just an object placed in a space that does the inviting for you. The net says: something is about to happen here, and you're part of it.

## What the net brings back

For a lot of us, though, a net brings something back. Gym class. Being picked near the end. The sound of a whistle and someone counting you. The sting of a real volleyball on flat wrists. The feeling of being judged for not being good enough at something that was supposed to be fun.

And if you're hosting, you have your own version of that fear. The ground is uneven. You don't own a real ball. One of your friends actually played in college. What if nobody wants to play? What if it's awkward? What if people humor you for five minutes and then drift back to the patio?

So the net stays in the garage. Everyone stands around holding a drink, being pleasant. The afternoon stays exactly as polite as it started.

Politeness is what happens when no one is willing to risk the thing that would actually bring people together.

## Mostly conversation

Here's the thing about volleyball that nobody says out loud. The ball spends most of its life in the air. Which means most of the game is people standing around, looking up, talking. Waiting for it to come down. It's mostly conversation with occasional jumping.

And the rules make you share. Three touches to get the ball back over the net. Which means at some point, you have to hand it to somebody else. You can't monopolize the game even if you try. The structure won't let you.

Nobody needs a hero out here. The best moment in any backyard game is the ugly save — a forearm, a knee, a wild sideways lunge into the tomato plants. That's the moment people remember and laugh about later. Not the clean spike. The chaos.

Volleyball is the rare game where the whole point is keeping something off the ground, together. You can't do it alone. That's not a metaphor. That's just the rules.

## Set it up kindly

So: put the net up before anyone arrives. Low. Lower than regulation, honestly. Low enough that the shortest person in the yard can get one over on the first try. The height of the net is a kindness you can offer before anyone even touches a ball.

Buy the soft ball. The squishy one that doesn't sting your wrists. Nobody in the history of backyards has complained that a volleyball was too gentle.

Then hand out one house rule. A bounce is fine. Catch it if you need to. We're not counting anything, and nobody is going to be told they're out. You're not lowering the standard — you're removing the thing that makes people freeze up.

Put chairs at the edge, close enough to talk. The people sitting down are still part of the game. They're the audience, the commentary team, the ones holding drinks for the players. And when the sides get lopsided, shuffle. Not as a correction — just new teams, new game, keep going.

## Until the light goes

Play until the light goes. That's the natural ending. Nobody has to announce it — the ball just gets hard to see. The sun does the work that a host would otherwise have to do, calling the thing to a close without anyone feeling like they're being told to wrap it up.

Then everyone stands around a while longer, grass on their ankles, the ball forgotten under a bush. This is the part you were really hosting for. Not the game itself, but the looseness that comes after. The conversation that got easier because everyone moved their body together for an hour.

You don't need a real court, or a clean yard, or anyone who knows what a set is. You need a sagging net and the willingness to put it up again.

The second time is easier than the first. By the fifth, people show up already wearing the right shoes.

That's how hosting works. Not ambition — frequency. You put the thing up. You do it again. Eventually people stop wondering whether they'll be invited and start wondering what side they'll be on.
