# Board game night, set up right

*The game matters less than the table you put it on.*

## Eight o'clock

Picture it. Six people in a living room, standing in front of a shelf of board game boxes, being polite about it. Someone says, "Whatever you guys want is fine." Someone else picks one up, reads the back, puts it down. Nobody wants to pick. Nobody wants to be the one who picked wrong.

This is where game night falls apart. Not in the game itself. In the twenty minutes before it, while everyone's standing around and nobody's in charge.

The energy people walked in with — that little buzz of anticipation — drains out in the gap between arriving and playing. And by the time you finally sit down, half the room has already settled into a low hum of "this is fine."

## Passing the decision around

We think a good game night depends on having the right game. So we buy more of them. We build collections. We ask the group what they feel like playing. We hand the decision around the room like a hot dish nobody wants to take first.

But a group can't choose. A group can only agree, slowly, to something nobody's especially excited about. And by then the mood has shifted. You went from "let's play something" to "let's just get through this."

Meanwhile the table still has yesterday's mail on one end. The good chair is in the other room. Somebody's sitting on a stool, balancing a plate on their knees, being a good sport about it. Everyone is making do, and making do is not the same as being comfortable.

## You already decided

Here's the shift. You picked the night, so you pick the game. Before anyone arrives. One game — and one shorter one in case the first ends early.

This isn't bossy. It's a kindness. People came over to be hosted. Making the small decisions for them is most of what hosting actually is. They've been making decisions all week. At work, at home, in traffic. Game night is a chance to show up and have someone else say, "Here's what we're doing."

Say it at the door. "We're playing this one. It takes about an hour. I'll walk everybody through it once you've all got a drink."

That one sentence does more for the evening than any game on the shelf.

## The table, the light, the snacks

So: clear the whole table. Not tidy — clear. Nothing on it but the game. A board takes more room than you think, and so do elbows. People need space to lean in without knocking something over.

Then sit in every chair. Can each one see the board right side up? Can each one reach the middle? If not, move something. Do it now, not while people are watching and feeling guilty that you're rearranging for them.

Put light directly over the table. Overhead, a lamp dragged closer, whatever you've got. Card text is small, and tired eyes get quiet. People won't ask for better light. They'll just squint and feel a little older than they need to.

Snacks that don't leave a fingerprint. Pretzels, grapes, nuts in a bowl. Save the wings for a night with no cards in it. Drinks go on a side table, not the board — because at some point, someone will reach across, and you'd rather they reach across nothing important.

And read the rules before people come. Once, out loud to yourself, badly. You don't have to be an expert. You just have to know where the game starts, so the first five minutes aren't spent flipping through a booklet in silence while six people watch.

## The shape of the evening

Say an end time out loud. "We'll wrap up by ten." People relax when they know the shape of the evening. The ones who want to stay longer will stay. The ones who need to go won't feel trapped.

Start when you said you'd start. The person running late would much rather walk in mid-game than be the reason six people sat around waiting for them. And they will be late, sometimes. That's fine. The evening doesn't have to hold for it.

None of this is about being impressive. A cleared table. A good light. One decision already made. That's most of it, and every bit of it is learnable. You don't need the perfect game. You need a lit table and a host who chose.

Then do it again in three weeks. And again three weeks after that. That's how it turns into a thing people count on — not because the game was great, but because the table was clear, the light was on, and someone had already decided.
