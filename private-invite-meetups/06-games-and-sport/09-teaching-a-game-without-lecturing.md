# Teaching a game without lecturing

*The rules aren't the game. The game is the game — and your job is to get people into it as fast as you can.*

## Eight minutes in

You've seen this. Eight minutes into the rules explanation, someone reaches for their phone. Someone else is nodding along, but their eyes have gone somewhere else entirely. And you keep going, because you're not finished.

Nobody's bored of the game. They haven't played it yet. They're bored of the lecture. And the lecture was your idea.

Here's the thing nobody tells you. Teaching the game is part of hosting the game. It's a skill of its own, and it isn't the same skill as knowing the rules. Plenty of people who can play beautifully can't teach a soul. And plenty of warm, patient hosts turn into anxious lecturers the moment a box opens.

You can be good at this. It just isn't what you think it is.

## Where the lecture comes from

It happens for a good reason. You know this game, you love it, and you want everyone to have a fair shot at it. So you try to hand them the whole thing at once, up front, before anything can go wrong. You're trying to be generous. You're trying to be fair.

But a rulebook read aloud isn't teaching. It's a transfer of anxiety. Yours, about being a fair host, handed to people who never asked to carry it.

And nobody can hold twelve rules for a game they can't picture yet. They've got nowhere to put them. So the rules go in, and slide straight back out. You're pouring water into a glass that hasn't been made yet.

This is the part that's hard to see when you're the one holding the box: your thoroughness isn't protecting them. It's stalling them. The fastest path to understanding isn't more information. It's experience to hang the information on.

## Show, don't brief

Give them the shape first. One sentence: here's how you win. Everything else in this game is in service of that, and now they've got somewhere to hang it. One sentence. Not a paragraph. Not "well, basically, the idea is…" One clean sentence, and then you stop.

Then stop describing the turn and take one. Slowly, cards face up, thinking out loud. "I want this, so I'm doing that." They'll learn more from watching thirty seconds than from hearing three minutes. You're not transmitting a system. You're showing them what it looks like when a person plays this game.

That shift — from describing to showing — is the whole thing. Describing is abstract. Showing is embodied. People learn movement by watching someone move, not by listening to someone describe movement. Games are the same. Let them see a turn happen, and most of your explanation becomes unnecessary.

## The first round doesn't count

Play the first round open. Everybody's hand visible, take-backs allowed, nothing counted. Say the words out loud — *this one doesn't count* — and then actually mean it. Not "it doesn't count unless you want it to." It doesn't count. You said it, so it's true. That's hosting.

Hold back the rules you don't need yet. The scoring exception that comes up once every four games can wait until the game it comes up in. Answer questions when they arrive, not before. A rule explained in the second it matters lands and stays. The same rule, ten minutes early, evaporates on the way to the table.

And you'll get something wrong. You'll play it wrong for two rounds and somebody will find the line in the book. Laugh, fix it, keep playing. Nothing was ruined. It was a game. The fact that you got it wrong and survived is itself a lesson for everyone at the table: this is low-stakes. We're here to try things.

Show one turn. Then get out of the way. The game is a better teacher than you are. Let it do its work.

## What they remember

Watch for the moment it turns. Somewhere in round two, the questions change. They stop asking whether they're allowed to do something and start asking whether it's a good idea. That's them arriving. That's the game becoming theirs.

Nobody remembers the rules of the first game they learned at your table. They remember whether they felt slow, or watched, or stupid. They remember whether they wanted to come back.

You get to decide that part. Not by being a better explainer. By being patient for about ten minutes — patient enough to stop talking, patient enough to let the first round be messy, patient enough to trust the game to teach what your lecture never could.

The host's real job isn't to make the game make sense. It's to make the table feel safe enough to not make sense yet.
