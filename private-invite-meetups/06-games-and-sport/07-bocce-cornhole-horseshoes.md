# Bocce, cornhole, horseshoes

*The best party games are the ones that leave your mouth free to talk and your feet free to move.*

## One Hand Is Enough

Here's the whole case for these three games, and it's a small one. You can play them holding a drink.

One hand is enough. Which means your mouth is free, and so is your attention.

Bocce. Cornhole. Horseshoes. You throw a thing toward another thing, and whoever gets closest wins. Nobody needs more than a minute of instruction. There's no rulebook to go find. Nobody has to watch a tutorial first or pretend they remember how scoring works.

This is not a knock on other games. Card games and board games are good. Trivia is good. But most games ask you to commit your whole self — eyes on the table, turn coming up, brain working the angles. While people think, the talking thins out. And if the game is too hard, somebody spends the afternoon feeling like they're losing in front of people they like. That's not what you invited them for.

## The Standing Circle

You know the alternative. Everyone in the yard in a loose standing circle, cups in hand, waiting for something to begin.

That circle has a shape. Three conversations going, one of them dying, and nobody with a reason to move their feet. People are being social because that's what they came to do, but the structure isn't helping. The host feels the pressure to keep things going. The guests feel the pressure to seem like they're enjoying themselves. Everyone is working harder than they need to.

A game like bocce or cornhole fixes the circle by breaking it. You throw, then you walk. Twenty feet down the lawn to see whose ball landed closer, then twenty feet back. The group reshuffles every round.

## The Walk

And while you're walking, you're talking. Not about the game. About the job, the dog, the thing you meant to say last time. The game is just the reason your feet are moving.

The throwing is almost incidental. It's an excuse to stand up, walk somewhere, and end up beside a different person than you were talking to before. Without anyone having to engineer that. Nobody has to say "let's mix it up" or "why don't you two chat." The game does the mixing, quietly, every round.

Nobody sits out. Your uncle who doesn't run and your niece who's eight can be in the same round, and it isn't a kindness extended to either one. They're both just playing. The playing field is uneven and nobody cares, because nobody came to win. They came to be outside with people, and the game makes that easy.

## How to Actually Have One

So own one set. Bocce, cornhole, horseshoes — whichever one you'll actually carry to the car. Keep it somewhere you can grab it without making a decision about it. The trunk is good. The garage shelf behind the bikes is not.

Set up wherever the ground is. None of these need a court. A driveway, a strip of gravel, a patch of grass with a slope in it. The slope makes it better. Imperfection gives people something to talk about between rounds — whose ball rolled, whose didn't, whether that patch counts.

Keep score out loud, and keep it badly. Somebody will lose track around eight. Let them. The score is a rhythm to move to, not a record anybody's keeping. If you're keeping a strict tally, you're doing something more serious than what's happening, and people will feel that.

Let people come and go. Someone leaves to flip the burgers, someone new picks up their bag. No roster. No substitution to announce. The game absorbs whoever's standing there and releases whoever's done. That's the whole structure.

## Two Stakes in the Dirt

The first time someone put two horseshoe stakes in the dirt, they probably thought it was too small a thing to count as hosting. It took four minutes.

But people stayed two hours past when they meant to leave. Not because the game was good — it wasn't, particularly. Because they had somewhere to put their hands, and something to walk toward. They had a reason to be standing next to someone new. They had a rhythm that made the afternoon hold its shape without anyone having to hold it.

Hosting isn't about the food or the space. It's about giving people a way to be together that doesn't require constant effort from anyone. These games do that almost for free.

You are allowed to host with a game that asks almost nothing of anybody. You are allowed to keep it small enough that it happens again next weekend.
