# Competitive people, managed

*A night doesn't fall apart when someone wants to win. It falls apart when nobody said what the night was for.*

You know the moment. You're six rounds into a board game, or a card game, or some trivia thing somebody pulled off a shelf. The room has been warm. People have been laughing. And then something shifts.

One person leans forward. Their voice drops half a step. They stop making jokes and start doing math.

Nobody says anything. But the table knows. The night just narrowed.

They're not being difficult. They're playing a different game than everyone else, and nobody told them so.

## The two moves we usually make

Most of us handle this one of two ways. We tease the person until they ease up. Or we say nothing, let it run, and quietly stop enjoying ourselves.

The teasing works a little. But it costs something. You've just told someone that the way they care is a flaw. They'll hear it. They'll get smaller for the rest of the night. Maybe they'll laugh it off, but they'll remember that the group thought their intensity was embarrassing.

Saying nothing costs more. By the last round, half the table has checked out. They're being polite. They're waiting for it to be over. And they'll remember that feeling next time you invite them. Not the game. The feeling.

## The real problem isn't the wanting

Wanting to win isn't the problem. The problem is that nobody said out loud what you were all playing for.

Half the table thinks this is a conversation with dice in it. The other half thinks it's a contest. Both are completely reasonable. They just don't match. And the mismatch is what makes the air go strange.

A competitive person in a room that agreed to compete is a gift. They raise the stakes. They make it matter. Games are better when someone actually cares whether they win. Think about the best match you've ever watched. Someone was trying. That's what made it worth watching.

That same person in a room that came to talk feels like a bulldozer. Same person. Different agreement.

## Say it before you deal

So say it before you deal. "This one's cutthroat, play mean." Or, "We're loose tonight, nobody's keeping score." Five seconds of hosting. It reorganizes everybody at the table.

You don't need a speech. You just need a sentence. People relax when they know what they're in. The tension comes from guessing.

Give the intense one a real opponent. If you've got two people who want blood, sit them across from each other. Now two people are satisfied instead of one person being quietly managed. The rest of the table can watch the match and chat around it.

Lean toward games with luck in them. Luck gives the beginner a floor to stand on. And it gives the sharp player something to blame that isn't a person. A bad roll is nobody's fault. A bad hand is just a bad hand. It keeps the outcome from feeling like a verdict on anyone's worth.

## When it tips anyway

Sometimes it still tips. The room gets tight. You feel it. You don't need a speech here either.

Stand up. Put the kettle on. Break the frame with your body instead of your words. "One more hand, then something dumb and loud."

That's it. You've changed the temperature without correcting anyone. Nobody has to feel like they ruined things. The night just moves.

## Permission

You're the host. You're allowed to shape the evening. Not by correcting anyone at the table, but by setting the terms early, before the terms are needed. That's the whole craft. You're not controlling people. You're creating a room where people know what kind of room they're in.

And if the competitive one is you — there's nothing wrong with you. Just ask what game the table is playing before you start playing yours. It's a question, not an apology.

Most people aren't hard to be around. They're just unmatched to the moment. That's a fixable thing. And fixing it quietly, before anyone notices it needed doing, is what hosting is.
