# The standing breakfast

*a kind of invitation you only send once — because after that, it stops being a plan and starts being a thing that happens.*

## sent once

There's a kind of invitation you only have to send once. Not because you're lazy. Because after that, it isn't a plan anymore. It's just a thing that happens.

Saturday, eight o'clock. That's it. That's the whole arrangement.

Whoever comes, comes. There's coffee. There are eggs if you made it to the shop. The door's open from eight, and that's all anyone needs to know.

You're not checking in. You're not following up. You put the kettle on and the table out, and the week does the rest. Some people show. Some don't. The breakfast doesn't care. It just keeps happening.

## death by scheduling

Most friendships don't end in conflict. They end in scheduling. In the fourth message about which Thursday works. In nobody replying, and nobody minding much.

We say we should do this more often. And we mean it completely. But meaning it has never once put a person in a chair.

So we wait for an evening big enough to be worth the asking. A dinner, a night out, a proper occasion. And the ask gets heavier the longer you carry it around. Reaching out starts to feel like a favour you're requesting. It isn't. But that's how it feels at nine at night, alone, with your thumb over the screen.

This is the part nobody names. We think friendships need more effort, more ambition, more occasions worth dressing up for. What they usually need is less friction. Fewer decisions. A door that's already open.

## the hour nobody wants

A standing breakfast fixes that. It removes the deciding. Nobody has to be the one who reaches out, because the reaching out already happened, months ago. It's done. You never have to do it again.

And morning is the hour nobody's fighting over. Nothing's claimed it yet. No one cancels breakfast because work ran long — work hasn't started. Saturday at eight is empty space in most people's lives. It's not a sacrifice to give up. It's a corner of the week that was sitting there anyway, waiting to be used.

Two hours at a table, before the day begins, with people who already know your business. You'd pay a lot for that. It costs a pot of coffee and a loaf of bread.

## make it boring

Here's the craft part. Make it boring. Same eggs. Same bread. Same mug on the same shelf. Novelty is a cost, and you'd be paying it every single week.

Don't clean for them. Leave the pans if that's the honest state of your kitchen. Warmth is what people come back for. Tidiness they can get anywhere. A friend wants to walk into a kitchen that feels lived in, not staged. They want to sit down without performing gratitude for the centrepiece.

Some weeks two people come. Some weeks nobody does. You still make the coffee. This is the part that matters most — a standing thing that only stands when it's convenient isn't standing. It's just a recurring plan with an escape clause, and everyone can feel the escape clause.

And keep the invitation short. "I do breakfast Saturdays at eight, come whenever." Then say it again next month, to the same person. People need to hear it twice before they believe it's real. The first time, they think you're being polite. The second time, they think you mean it. The third time, they come.

## fifty mornings

The obligation is the point, gently. Someone invited you. Someone has the kettle on. That small pull is what gets you out of the house — and you're always glad it did. Not because breakfast is extraordinary. Because you almost didn't go, and going was the better version of your day.

Don't keep score on who hosted last. Someone brings pastries eventually. Someone offers their kitchen next month. Let it stay uneven. Uneven is what ongoing looks like. The moment you start tracking fairness, you've turned a kitchen table into a ledger, and nobody relaxes at a ledger.

A year of Saturdays is fifty mornings with people you love. And nobody planned a single one of them, except the first.

You sent one message. You made a lot of coffee. You left the door open. That was enough.
