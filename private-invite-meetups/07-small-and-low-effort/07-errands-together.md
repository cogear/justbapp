# Errands, together

*The fastest way to see someone is to stop saving them for the good hours.*

## The List on the Counter

There's a list on your counter. Return the parcel. Pick up the prescription. Two bags for the charity shop. The ordinary admin of being a person with a life.

You were going to do all of it alone. You almost always do. Errands feel like something to get through, not something to share. They're the chores that live between the things you'd actually call plans.

But an errand is a place. And a place can hold two people.

## Saving People for the Good Hours

Most of us save our friends for the good hours. The cleared evening. The proper plan. The version of ourselves that's rested and has something to say. We imagine there will be a Saturday with no obligations and no fatigue, and on that Saturday we'll finally call.

So we wait for a Friday that isn't tired, and the weeks go by, and we tell each other we should really do something soon. The text sits there. The intention is real. The months stack up.

Meanwhile your actual life is happening in the other hours. The ones with the list in them. The pharmacy at eleven on a Tuesday. The hardware store on a Saturday morning with a return that's been sitting by the door for three weeks. Those hours are most of them. That's where you actually are.

## Side by Side

Here's the quiet trick of the errand. It gives the two of you something to look at that isn't each other.

Side by side in a car. Shoulder to shoulder in an aisle. Waiting at a counter. Talk comes easier when nobody's being looked at, and the silences don't feel like failures — the task is filling them. You're reading labels. You're looking for the right aisle. The conversation doesn't have to carry the whole weight of the hour.

You don't have to be entertaining. You don't have to have news. You don't have to answer the question "how have you been?" in a way that sums up your entire month. You just have to be going somewhere, and bring someone.

Something about facing the same direction loosens people up. There's a reason car trips produce conversations that dinner tables don't. You're not across from each other, performing. You're next to each other, in motion, with a shared task that asks almost nothing of either of you.

## Come With Me

You're still the host. That doesn't disappear because there's no table. You chose the errand. You named the hour. You drove, or you waited outside their building. You made it easy to say yes by not making it feel like a thing.

And the invitation is almost nothing: I'm going to the hardware store Saturday morning, come with me. Say it like an offer, not a favour you need. Not "I know it's boring." Not "only if you want to." Just a plain sentence, offered like coffee.

The garden centre. The post office. The dump run. Walking the dog before the heat. Picking up the cake. Anything you were doing anyway.

Then it's finished, and nobody has to invent a reason to leave. The errand ends the visit for you. That's a kindness for both of you. No long goodbyes in the hallway. No wondering if you've stayed too long. The task is done, the car is parked, the day continues.

## Where Your Life Actually Is

The house never had to be tidy. There was no house. There was nothing to prepare. No meal to plan, no guest room to ready, no version of yourself to assemble.

Just an hour of a Tuesday. A car with two people in it. A receipt in your pocket, and something crossed off a list.

Do it again next month. Then again. Not one perfect evening a year — a hundred small ordinary ones. The kind of friendship that gets built in ordinary hours is a different thing from the kind that gets built in planned ones. It's looser. It has more room in it. It doesn't require a special occasion to be real.

You are allowed to invite someone into the dull parts of your life. That's where most of your life is. And it turns out those hours hold people just fine.
