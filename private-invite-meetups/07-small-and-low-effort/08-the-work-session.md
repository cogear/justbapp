# The work session

*The easiest invitation you'll ever make asks for almost nothing — and changes more than you'd think.*

## The thing that never happens

There's something you've been avoiding. The form, the folder, the email you owe someone. Not hard. Just the thing that never happens. Three weeks now. Maybe six.

You don't need more discipline for it. You probably just need somebody sitting across the table, doing their own version of the same thing.

Not to help you. Not to check on you. Just there, with their own work, in the same light.

This is something most people never try, and it's strange when you think about it. We'll drag ourselves to a gym because other people are there. We'll show up for a meeting because someone expects us. But the tasks that belong to no one but us? We leave those to willpower alone, and willpower is famously unreliable.

## Saved for the good parts

We invite people over for the good parts. Dinner. A birthday. The evening we cleaned the bathroom for. Company gets saved for the hours we think are worth showing.

So the ordinary hours go to us alone. The afternoons of paperwork and half-answered messages. And the boring task sits there, week after week, on a list nobody else can see.

Meanwhile someone you'd like to see more of is in their own kitchen, avoiding their own version of it. Both of you busy. Neither of you together.

There's a quiet loneliness in this that we don't name. Not dramatic loneliness. Just the low hum of doing everything by yourself because you assumed company had to be earned, prepared, and presented. The truth is most of life is ordinary hours. If friendship only lives in the good parts, it's going to be a thin friendship.

## Come work here

So invite them to work. Not to talk, not to eat. Bring your laptop, your envelopes, the folder you're dreading. Two o'clock until four. That's the whole invitation.

You'll each do your own thing, side by side. That's the design, not a compromise. Two people, one table, two separate piles.

And here's why it works. You told someone you'd be there, so you're there. The chair holds you. The task gets finished in the wake of a small promise you made to a friend.

It's the easiest invitation to say yes to, because it asks almost nothing. Nobody has to be charming. Nobody has to bring anything but their own unfinished business. The bar is so low it's almost touching the ground, and that's exactly the point.

## How to host it

Hosting this asks almost nothing of you. A cleared table. A chair that doesn't hurt. Something hot to drink. The wifi password, said out loud so they don't have to ask.

Don't tidy. Really. The dishes in the sink are proof this isn't a performance — it's just your Tuesday with one more person in it.

Say when it starts and when it ends. Halfway through, stop for ten minutes and actually talk. Then back to it. An ending time is a kindness; nobody has to guess when to leave.

Then do it again. Same day, same hour, next week. Small and repeated does more for a friendship than big and rare. A weekly two-hour work session adds up to over a hundred hours a year together. That's real time. That's a friendship with weight.

## Sideways

Some of my closest friendships were built sideways. Not over deep conversation. Over hours where neither of us was trying to be interesting, and the work got done anyway.

There's a kind of intimacy in shared silence that talk can't manufacture. You learn someone's posture, their sighs, the way they rub their eyes at 3:15. They learn yours. You see each other without the costume of being a host or a guest. Just two people doing what needs doing, near each other.

You don't have to make an occasion out of company. You can open the door, point at the empty chair, and get on with your afternoon.

Come do your thing. I'll do mine. That's a real invitation. And it counts.
