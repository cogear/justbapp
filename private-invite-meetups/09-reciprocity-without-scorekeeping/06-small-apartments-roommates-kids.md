# Small apartments, roommates, kids

*The size of your place is almost never the real reason you're not hosting.*

There's a sentence I hear more than almost any other. I'd host, but my place is too small.

Sometimes it's roommates. Sometimes it's a kid asleep down the hall. Sometimes it's one room, one table, and a couch that seats two people who like each other.

Whatever the shape, the conclusion is the same. Hosting is for people with more room than me. I'll be a guest for now. And for now turns into years.

## Being seen

I don't think size is usually the real problem. The real worry is being seen. Your bathroom. The mattress on the floor. The particular unglamorous way you actually live.

We imagine our friends walking in and quietly doing arithmetic. Measuring the square footage against some standard we invented, or inherited, or absorbed from a magazine. So we wait. For the bigger place. For the better lease. For the year the kids finally sleep through.

Meanwhile, someone else hosts every time. And you start carrying a quiet debt you don't know how to hand back. Not because anyone's counting. Because you are.

That debt is a strange thing. Nobody asked you to carry it. Nobody is tracking it. But it settles in anyway — this sense that you've taken more than you've given, that your friendship is slightly out of balance. And the irony is that the very thing that would resolve it — inviting people over — is the thing you keep postponing.

## What small rooms actually do

Here's what small rooms do that large rooms don't. They put people close together. Nobody can drift to the edge. Knees touch. There's one conversation instead of four, and everybody's in it.

Some of the best nights I can remember happened in rooms where somebody had to sit on the floor. Nobody apologized. We just moved a chair and stayed until late. The closeness was the thing. Not a problem to solve but the point.

A small room removes escape routes, and in a strange way that's a gift. You can't hide. You're in the circle. You belong to the evening in a way that doesn't happen when people can scatter across an open floor plan and start separate side conversations.

Your turn doesn't have to look like anyone else's turn. It only has to happen.

## Working with what you have

So. Roommates first. Tell them a week out, and invite them by name. Not permission — inclusion. A roommate who's invited often becomes a second host, someone who refills drinks and clears plates. A roommate who's ambushed becomes weather — something to be endured, not part of the evening.

Kids. Host early — four in the afternoon, when everyone's still cheerful. Let them be in the room. Kids underfoot aren't a hosting failure. They're a fact about your life, and your friends came for your life, not a tidied version of it. If bedtime falls in the middle of the gathering, that's fine. People understand. They've been there.

Small space. Cut the guest list before you cut the evening. Three people fit anywhere. Serve one thing — a pot of something, bread, a salad. Put the drinks in the kitchen so people have to stand together to reach them. Movement creates conversation. A bottleneck is a feature, not a flaw.

And if the apartment truly won't hold it, leave. A bench. Your stoop. The park at the end of the block, a blanket, a bag of oranges. You picked a time and gathered people. That's hosting. It was never about the walls.

## What people remember

Nobody walks home remembering square footage. They remember that you asked. That somebody made a plan, and they were on the list.

Frequency beats ambition. A slightly cramped Tuesday, six times a year, gives you more of a friendship than the perfect dinner you keep postponing until conditions improve. The perfect dinner is a ghost. It doesn't exist. The cramped Tuesday is real, and it builds something the ghost never will.

So let them see the small kitchen. The loud radiator. The kid in pajamas. That isn't the cost of the invitation. That is the invitation.

Your actual life, offered without apology, turns out to be the thing people wanted all along.
