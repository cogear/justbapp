# Specific beats open-ended

*The kindest invitation is one that's already been decided.*

## Sometime

You've said it. I've said it. Standing in a doorway with your coat half on, meaning every word: we should do this more often.

And then the door closes, and a month goes by, and then three. And you both still mean it.

"Sometime" isn't a day. It's a feeling we have about each other. Warm, genuine, and completely empty. It has nowhere to land.

We keep doing this because it feels safe. Nobody can reject "sometime." Nobody has to awkwardly check their calendar or explain why that weekend won't work. The door is closing, the moment is already dissolving, and we want to leave something warm behind. So we do. But what we leave behind is a sentiment, not a plan.

## Homework

Open-ended invitations sound generous. No pressure, no demands, all the doors left open. But look at what they actually do. They hand the whole job to the other person.

Now they have to pick the date. Guess what you had in mind. Guess whether you meant it, or whether it was just something nice to say while leaving.

That's a lot of work to accept a favour. So mostly, nobody does it. The plan dies politely, and you stay fond of each other from a distance.

"Let me know when you're free" is not an invitation. It's homework. It transfers every decision — and every risk — to the person you were trying to reach. They have to figure out the logistics, gauge your sincerity, and be the one to stick their neck out. That's not generosity on your part. That's avoidance dressed up nicely.

There's also something quieter going on. When you leave the invitation open-ended, you're protecting yourself from rejection. If they never follow up, you never have to hear a no. But you also never get a yes.

## Something to hold

A specific invitation is different. It's a decision you already made, handed to someone else, finished. Thursday. Seven. My place. There's soup.

Now there's something to hold. Something you can say yes to, or no to, in four seconds. Both of those are easy. "Sometime" has no answer at all — that's why it never gets one.

And being specific isn't pushy. It's the opposite. You did the thinking so they don't have to. The hosting starts there, before anyone's even in your kitchen.

The strange part is that people are relieved. A clear plan is easier to receive than an open door. It tells them they were actually wanted, on a particular evening, at a particular table. Not in the abstract, not as a vague someday gesture, but right now, at this address, with soup on the stove.

## Four small facts

So: a day. A time. A place. And then one more thing most of us leave out — what it actually is.

"Come over Thursday at seven. I'm making chili. It'll be four of us. Home by ten." Now nobody's wondering what to wear, or how long they're committed to, or whether they need to eat before they come.

Small and clear beats big and vague, every time. Tuesday, tea, an hour actually happens. A proper dinner party, one of these days, quietly does not.

The last detail — what it is — does more than the other three combined. It sets the temperature of the evening. Chili and home by ten tells your guest this is low-key, short, easy to say yes to. You're not just inviting them over. You're telling them what kind of evening they'd be walking into, so they can picture it.

And if they can't make it, you've still given them something to work with. A real no. And very often, "what about the week after?"

## Pick a Thursday

You're allowed to name a day. To decide the shape of it before you ask. To be the one who makes the thing real instead of possible.

The vague invitation keeps you safe. Nobody can turn down "sometime." It also keeps your table empty, and your good intentions filed under someday.

So pick a Thursday. Say it out loud, with the time in it. Someone you like is waiting for a real invitation — one they don't have to build themselves.

The simplest version of hospitality isn't a clean house or a good meal. It's a clear one. A Thursday, a time, a thing that's happening whether or not they come. And a seat saved, just in case.
