# How far ahead to ask

*The hardest part of inviting someone over isn't the asking. It's the deciding when to ask.*

There's probably a message on your phone right now that you haven't sent. You know who it's for. You know what it says — something simple, like *come over Thursday* or *soup this weekend?* What you don't know is when to send it. So it sits there. Another week goes by.

Too far ahead and it feels like a production. Too close and it feels like an afterthought. You wait for the right moment, which never seems to arrive. And the thought stays a thought. That's the only real failure here — not bad timing, but no timing at all.

## The problem with three weeks

Ask someone three weeks out and something strange happens. The thing you're asking about stops being an evening and becomes an appointment. It sits on the calendar getting heavier, competing with everything that shows up after it.

A far-off Saturday is easy to say yes to. That yes doesn't cost anything yet — it's just a dot on a screen. But three weeks is a long time for a plan to stay warm. Enthusiasm has a shelf life. What felt lovely when you asked can feel like a chore by the time it rolls around. People start to quietly wish they'd said no, or they forget the plan entirely and schedule something else on top of it.

You wanted a relaxed dinner. You got an obligation.

## The problem with tonight

Ask the night before and you get the opposite trouble. Not rudeness, exactly. But the feeling of being a patch — like someone counted heads, came up short, and thought of you. Even if that's not what happened, it's how it can land.

Short notice isn't inherently rude. But it carries a subtext. The subtext is: I didn't plan around you. Which might be true and fine, but it's not the same as: I thought of you and wanted you here.

## About a week

Somewhere between too early and too late there's a window. For most small things — dinner, a walk, coffee at your kitchen table — it's about a week.

A week is long enough that someone can shift one small thing to make room. Short enough that they can picture the evening. Next Thursday still feels like a real day, with weather and traffic and a way to get to your door. It hasn't gone abstract yet.

Close enough to feel real. Far enough to be possible. That's most of it.

## Adjusting from there

Adjust from that week, gently. Six people around a table, give it two weeks — there are more schedules to coordinate. Parents of small kids, longer still. They're not being difficult. They're negotiating with another household, a sitter, the math of bedtimes. Give them room to do that math.

And don't lose the short notice entirely. *I made too much soup, come at seven* — that's one of the warmest messages you can send. It says: you're someone I don't have to plan for. You're easy. You're close.

That only works if you do it often, though. Which is the quiet trade underneath all of this. If this is the one gathering of the year, it needs runway. If there's another one in three weeks, tonight is plenty. Frequency changes everything. When people know there's always another one coming, the pressure on any single evening drops. They can say yes lightly. They can say no without guilt.

## The calendar isn't flat

One more thing worth remembering: the calendar isn't flat. December is crowded. So is the end of summer, when everyone tries to squeeze in last things. In those stretches, ask earlier — or ask smaller. A Tuesday. A weeknight nobody else is competing for. A lunch. A walk instead of a meal.

The goal isn't to find the perfect slot. It's to find a real one — a time that actually exists in someone's life, not a gap between obligations.

## Pick a day

You don't have to get this exactly right. Nobody's grading the lead time. A week, give or take. Send it on a Sunday for the Saturday after. That's plenty.

The clock that's actually stopping you isn't theirs. It's the one in your head saying *not yet.* Wait for a calmer week. Wait until the house is sorted. Wait until the fridge is right.

There is no calmer week. There's this one, and the one after it.

Pick a day. Send the message. Let people arrange themselves around it. They usually do.
