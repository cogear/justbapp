# Invite three people this month

*A small, specific invitation beats a perfect plan that never leaves your head.*

## The whole assignment

Here's the whole thing. Invite three people into your home this month.

Not thirty. Three. One evening, one date, one message you actually send instead of almost sending.

That's it. I'm going to spend a few minutes on it anyway, because the small version is the one people skip. We tend to think the real thing must be bigger than what we can actually do, so we wait, and the waiting becomes the whole story.

Three is small enough that you can't hide inside the planning. There's almost nothing to prepare. There's only the sending. And the sending is where most of us get stuck — not because we don't care, but because caring quietly turns into overthinking.

## Sometime isn't a day

Most of us carry an invitation that never quite becomes one. We say it out loud — *we should get together* — and we mean it, completely. But sometime isn't a day. Sometime has no hour, no address, nothing anyone can say yes to.

So it drifts. A month passes, then a season. And we call the drifting busyness, when really it's just a sentence we haven't said yet.

This is worth pausing on. The people you miss are usually one specific sentence away. Not a better friendship. Not more free time. One sentence, with a day inside it. The gap between "we should hang out" and "Thursday, seven, my place" is enormous in feeling and tiny in effort. That tiny effort is the whole door.

## Start with the date

So start with the date, not the people. Open your calendar. Find an evening in the next four weeks when you'd be home anyway — tired, nothing planned. That one.

Write it down. Now the gathering exists before anybody's been asked. You're not requesting permission for something vague. You're offering a place to be, on a Thursday.

Then keep the invitation small enough to be true. *Thursday, seven, my place, there'll be soup.* That's the message. No apology for the flat. No promise you'd have to live up to. No menu, no theme, no attempt to make it sound like more than it is.

The honesty is what makes it work. People can feel when an invitation is real versus when it's a performance of enthusiasm. A flat, specific invitation says: I mean this. I have room for you. That's more welcoming than any polished version.

## Three names, sent today

Now, three names. Someone you already love and somehow never see. Someone you've been curious about for a while and never followed up with. And whoever came to mind on their own, just now, while you were reading this.

Send them separately, today. Separately matters — a group thread turns an evening into a logistics problem, and logistics is where good nights go to die. You want each person to receive a message that feels addressed to them, not a broadcast. You want each person to be able to say yes or no without performing their answer for a crowd.

Then put the phone down. Let people answer in their own time. One of them will probably say no — and that's just the price of asking. It's much lower than it feels.

Two out of three is still a room with people in it. Two out of three is a night you'll remember more clearly than the six weeks around it.

And if it goes even slightly well, pick the next date before the last person finds their coat. Once a month, roughly, for years — that beats one perfect party. Frequency is the thing. Not the food, not the space, not the guest list alignment. Just showing up again and again, until it becomes ordinary, until the ordinary becomes the thing you'd miss most if it stopped.

## You don't have to be good at this yet

Nobody is, at first. Hosting is a craft, not a personality trait. It gets easier every time you do it, the way anything does — not because you learn to perform, but because you stop needing to.

You need one date, three names, and a door you're willing to open.

The month is already moving. Somewhere inside it there's an ordinary evening that could become the thing people mention years later, casually, like it was nothing. That's how the best ones always feel in the moment — like nothing, like just a Thursday with soup.

So. Who are your three?
