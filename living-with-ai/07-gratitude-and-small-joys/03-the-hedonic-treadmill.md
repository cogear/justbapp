# The Hedonic Treadmill

## The moment you got what you wanted

Think about something you really wanted at one point.

A raise. A new apartment. A relationship. A degree. A piece of tech that promised to “change everything.” You pictured how good it would feel to finally have it—how it would settle you, validate you, relax you.

Then it happened. You got it.

For a while, it was exactly as satisfying as you imagined. Maybe even more.

And then something subtle took over: the extraordinary became ordinary. The new thing blended into your life like it had always been there. Your happiness didn’t stay at the peak. It drifted back toward “normal.”

And soon, almost automatically, your mind started reaching for the next upgrade.

That cycle has a name.

## What psychologists call hedonic adaptation

Psychologists refer to this as **hedonic adaptation**: our tendency to adjust to improvements in our circumstances. We get used to the good. We stop noticing it. The emotional “buzz” fades.

This doesn’t mean you’re ungrateful. It doesn’t mean the goal was pointless. It means your brain is doing what brains do: calibrating to whatever becomes familiar.

It’s why a new car feels thrilling… until it’s just “the car.” It’s why moving to a nicer home brings relief… until it becomes “the house.” It’s why a relationship can start out electric… and then turn into the routines of real life (which can be wonderful, but less dramatic).

Over time, you can end up in a loop:

1. **Desire** (“If I only had that…”)
2. **Achievement** (“Finally!”)
3. **Normalization** (“This is just life now.”)
4. **New desire** (“Okay, but what’s next?”)

When you’re in the middle of it, it can feel like motivation. Like ambition. Like progress.

But it can also feel like restlessness you can’t quite explain.

## Why your brain works this way (and why it hurts now)

From an evolutionary perspective, hedonic adaptation made a lot of sense.

If our ancestors got comfortable too quickly, they stopped striving. They stopped scanning. They stopped preparing. In a world of scarcity and real danger, complacency could be fatal.

So the brain developed a bias toward “not enough.” Toward wanting more. Toward being pulled forward.

That bias helped humans survive.

But now the environment has changed.

Most of us aren’t dodging predators. Yet our minds still operate as if contentment is risky. As if feeling satisfied means we’ll stop growing, stop achieving, stop staying safe.

So we keep running. And running. And running.

Welcome to the **hedonic treadmill**: the sense that you’re moving, achieving, upgrading—but not arriving.

## How the treadmill sneaks into modern life

The treadmill is especially loud today because so many systems are built to keep it spinning.

Social media constantly shows you what someone else has. Ads are designed to make your current life feel slightly insufficient. Algorithms reward novelty, not depth. Even productivity culture can turn “growth” into a never-ending checklist.

In that context, hedonic adaptation becomes less like a quirk of psychology and more like an operating system.

It can show up as:

- Always needing a new goal to feel okay  
- Feeling underwhelmed right after a win  
- Downplaying what you’ve accomplished (“It’s not that big of a deal”)  
- Fantasizing about a future version of life where you’ll finally relax  
- Treating the present as a waiting room  

None of this makes you broken. It makes you human.

But you don’t have to stay stuck.

## Slowing the treadmill with deliberate attention

If hedonic adaptation is the problem, the counter isn’t “try harder” or “want less.”

The counter is **deliberate attention**.

It’s choosing to re-notice what you’ve stopped seeing.

Because adaptation doesn’t remove the good from your life—it removes your awareness of it.

That’s why you can have a comfortable home and still feel unsettled. A loving partner and still feel like something is missing. Meaningful work and still feel restless. When blessings become background, your mind starts scanning for the next hit of novelty.

Slowing the treadmill looks like practicing “returning” to what’s already here.

A few simple ways to do that:

- **Savor on purpose.** Take 20 seconds to fully feel something good: warm sunlight, clean sheets, the taste of coffee, a quiet morning. Your brain learns what you repeat.  
- **Name what’s working.** Once a day, finish the sentence: *“One thing I’m glad I don’t have to struggle with today is…”*  
- **Use “once” language.** Remind yourself: *“I once wished for this.”* The commute, the job, the home, the relationship status—some version of you longed for it.  
- **Create small renewals.** Tiny changes can wake you up to what you have: rearrange a room, take a different walking route, put your phone away at dinner. Novelty can be used to appreciate, not just to chase.  

This isn’t about forcing positivity. It’s about recovering vision.

## Permission to be human (and to look again)

Here’s the honest truth: your brain will adapt to the good. That’s its nature.

You don’t need to shame yourself for it. You don’t need to “fix” your desires. You just need to notice when the treadmill is speeding up—and practice stepping off, even briefly.

Not because ambition is wrong, but because **a life that’s always reaching is a life that rarely arrives**.

So ask yourself:

What have you stopped seeing?

Look again.

## Closing reflection

Maybe the point isn’t to stop wanting things. Maybe the point is to stop outsourcing your sense of “enough” to the next milestone. The extraordinary is still here—quietly waiting for your attention.
