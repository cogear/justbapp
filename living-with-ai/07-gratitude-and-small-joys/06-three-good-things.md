# Three Good Things

## A five-minute practice with surprisingly long-lasting results

What if I told you there’s a small habit—free, simple, and doable even on your busiest days—that research suggests can increase happiness and reduce symptoms of depression? Not for an hour. Not just for the weekend. In some studies, the benefits last for months.

It sounds like the kind of promise that usually comes with a catch.

But the “catch,” if you can call it that, is consistency. You have to actually do it.

The practice is called **Three Good Things**, and it’s almost exactly what it sounds like.

## The research (and why it matters)

In the world of positive psychology, “Three Good Things” is one of the most cited gratitude-based exercises because it’s both **low-effort** and **high-impact**. Researchers have used variations of it in studies involving everyday adults, students, and even people working in high-stress environments.

The basic finding is consistent: when people intentionally reflect on positive moments, their mood improves and their attention shifts. Instead of your mind defaulting to “What went wrong today?” you begin to build the muscle of “What went right?”

That shift isn’t about pretending life is perfect. It’s about balancing the mental scoreboard. Most of us are already experts at collecting evidence for what’s missing, what’s stressful, and what needs fixing. This practice helps your brain collect a different kind of evidence, too—evidence that good things happen, even in ordinary days.

## The method: Three Good Things (and the tiny twist that makes it work)

Each evening, write down **three good things that happened today**.

That’s it.

They don’t need to be impressive. In fact, the small stuff often works better because it’s easier to repeat daily and easier to overlook.

- The first sip of coffee that actually tasted amazing  
- A text from a friend that made you feel remembered  
- Sunshine after three days of gray  
- Finishing a task you’d been avoiding  
- A moment of laughter that loosened the tightness in your chest  

It’s the ordinary miraculous—catalogued and appreciated.

Here’s the twist that deepens the effect: **for each good thing, write one or two sentences about why it happened.**

This matters because it moves gratitude from passive to active.

Instead of: “I’m lucky,”  
you start to write: “My friend checked in because we’ve built a relationship where we show up for each other.”

Instead of: “I got a break,”  
you might write: “I planned ahead, and that made today smoother.”

You’re connecting the good to causes—your choices, your relationships, your environment, your effort, your values. That connection is part of what makes the practice stick.

## The rewiring: training your mind to scan for good

Your brain is designed to notice threats and problems. That’s not pessimism—it’s survival wiring. If something might go wrong, your mind wants to catch it early.

The downside is that this bias can make daily life feel like a stream of annoyances, failures, and unfinished business, even when many things are also going right.

When you do Three Good Things consistently, something subtle starts to happen:

You begin noticing good moments **during the day**, not just at night.

You catch yourself thinking, “That was nice—I’ll write it down later.” You start saving small wins. Your attention shifts from scanning for problems to scanning for *proof that life contains support, ease, and meaning too.*

This is not toxic positivity. You’re not denying the hard parts. You’re broadening the frame.

And over time, that broadening can be powerful. Days don’t necessarily become easier, but they can feel **less bleak**, less monochrome. You’re building a more accurate emotional record.

## Making it sustainable: a routine you’ll actually keep

The key is consistency, not perfection.

Try attaching it to something you already do:

- right after brushing your teeth  
- when you plug your phone in to charge  
- as part of your evening wind-down  
- while the kettle boils or the shower warms up  

Keep it lightweight. Notes app is fine. A sticky note is fine. A dedicated notebook is lovely, but not required.

If you miss a night, don’t turn it into a story about failure. Just restart the next evening. The practice works because it’s cumulative—like compound interest for your attention.

If you want to go one step further, end with a gentle question:  
**“What did today teach me about what I value?”**  
Often your three good things reveal your real priorities—connection, calm, progress, beauty, play, health, contribution.

## Permission to begin (and to keep it simple)

You don’t need a dramatic life change to feel better. Sometimes you need a small daily practice that reminds your brain: *good exists here, too.*

Five minutes a night. Three small goods. A sentence about why they happened.

That’s the whole thing.

Tonight, try it. And as you write, notice what it feels like to see your day not as a blur of obligations, but as a place where small goodness kept showing up.

What were your three good things today?
