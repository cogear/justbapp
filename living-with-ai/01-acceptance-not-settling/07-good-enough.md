# Good Enough

## The experiment: try “good enough” on purpose

What if you sent the email without rewriting it one more time?

Not the careless email. Not the confusing one. Just the clear one.

For the next week, try a small experiment: choose “good enough” on purpose. Pick one or two moments each day where you’d normally reach for extra polish, extra checking, extra tweaking—and stop earlier than you usually would.

Then watch what happens.

Because most of us don’t actually know what “good enough” feels like. We know effort. We know over-effort. We know that familiar itch of, *It’s almost there…* even when it’s already there.

This week, give yourself a different data point.

## What “good enough” looks like in real life

Good enough is the email that’s clear rather than perfectly phrased.

It’s the meal that’s healthy rather than photo-ready.

It’s the workout you actually do rather than the ideal routine you keep postponing.

It’s the conversation that’s honest rather than impressive.

It’s shipping the project when it works, instead of adding features nobody asked for.

Good enough is not a vibe. It’s a choice you can make in specific moments. And it’s easier to practice when you define it clearly:

- **Clear beats clever.**
- **Complete beats intricate.**
- **Real beats performative.**
- **Done beats perfect.**

When you choose good enough, you’re not lowering standards across your entire life. You’re simply refusing to spend premium energy on tasks that don’t deserve it.

## The pull toward perfect (and why it’s so persuasive)

Perfectionism rarely looks like vanity. It often looks like responsibility.

It says: *If I just try a little harder, I can prevent criticism, disappointment, or regret.*

It says: *If I don’t catch the mistake now, it’ll prove something about me later.*

It says: *If I do this perfectly, I’ll finally feel calm.*

That last one is the trap. Perfectionism promises relief, but usually produces more tension. The finish line keeps moving because the goal isn’t the task—it’s the feeling of certainty.

And certainty is hard to come by, especially in a world where everything is visible, measurable, and shareable.

When you live with AI—tools that can draft, rewrite, optimize, suggest—you also live with an endless supply of “better versions.” There’s always another rewrite. Another prompt. Another improvement to chase. The work becomes a slot machine: maybe the next pull will produce the perfect wording.

But the problem isn’t that better options exist.

The problem is believing you’re obligated to pursue them.

## The cost of perfect: the last 10% is the most expensive

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most of the time, nobody notices the difference between your 90% effort and your 100% effort—except you.

You notice the extra hour spent adjusting formatting.

You notice the stress of rereading every sentence.

You notice the evening you sacrificed to make something “just a bit better.”

And you definitely notice what you didn’t do because perfection took the time: resting, connecting, taking a walk, starting the next project, or simply being done.

That’s why “good enough” isn’t a failure mode. It’s a strategy.

It’s recognizing that the last 10% of polish can cost 50% of the energy. Sometimes that trade is worth it—when something truly matters, when stakes are high, when the work represents a core value or a meaningful commitment.

But most tasks aren’t sacred. They’re supportive. They’re functional. They’re meant to move life forward, not consume it.

## Choose your 100% (instead of trying to give it to everything)

This isn’t about caring less. It’s about caring *on purpose*.

Not everything deserves your 100%.

Reserve that level of attention for the things that genuinely shape your life:

- Your health and relationships  
- Work that aligns with your values  
- Commitments that have real consequences  
- Projects that you want to be proud of years from now  

Let everything else be good enough.

This is where AI can be a surprising ally. Use it to get to “good enough” faster: draft the email, outline the plan, summarize the notes, generate options. Then stop when it’s clear and functional. Don’t let the tool lure you into infinite refinement just because refinement is available.

A helpful question: **“If I stopped now, what’s the realistic downside?”**  
Not the imagined downside. The realistic one.

Often, it’s minor. Sometimes it’s none.

## Permission: a sentence you can borrow

Try this when you feel yourself reaching for one more tweak:

**This is what is. This is good enough. I’m allowed to stop now.**

Say it out loud if you can.

Then hit send. Serve the meal. Publish the post. End the work session.

And notice: the world doesn’t end.

You don’t get struck by lightning for being human. People still understand you. The project still functions. Life still moves forward.

But you gain something you didn’t have before: time, energy, and peace of mind for what comes next.

## Closing thought

Good enough is not giving up—it’s growing up. It’s learning that your attention is a finite resource, and your life is bigger than any one email, meal, or paragraph. Choose your moments for excellence. Let the rest be complete, real, and done.
