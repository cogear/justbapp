# The Maximizer’s Trap

## The paradox nobody advertises

There’s a strange paradox hiding inside modern life.

The people who work the hardest to get the *best* results often end up the most exhausted, most anxious, and—quietly—most miserable.

Meanwhile, the people who settle for “good enough” tend to look… lighter. They make decisions faster. They move on sooner. They enjoy what they chose.

This isn’t laziness or low standards. It’s a different strategy for living.

And it has a name.

## Maximizers vs. satisficers

Psychologist Barry Schwartz popularized the idea of the **Maximizer’s Trap**: the tendency to believe that if you just search a little longer, compare a little more, optimize a little harder, you’ll land the perfect choice—and then finally feel satisfied.

Schwartz noticed that people tend to fall into two camps:

**Maximizers** feel compelled to explore every option and select the absolute best. They want the optimal outcome, and they don’t want to leave anything on the table.

**Satisficers** decide what “good enough” looks like ahead of time, and once they find an option that meets that standard, they choose it and move on.

If you’re thinking, “Okay, but surely maximizers are happier because they get better results,” you’re not alone.

But research repeatedly finds the opposite: maximizers report **lower life satisfaction**, **more regret**, and **higher rates of depressive symptoms**. They may achieve more on paper, but they tend to enjoy it less.

It’s not that maximizing never works. It’s that maximizing often comes with hidden psychological costs.

## Why “better” doesn’t feel better

Maximizers often do make objectively stronger choices. The car they buy is higher-rated. The apartment they rent is brighter, safer, closer to transit. The laptop they choose benchmarks better.

But after the purchase? After the decision? The mind doesn’t rest.

Because once you’ve trained yourself to believe there is a best, you also train yourself to believe you might have missed it.

So instead of enjoying what you picked, you keep evaluating it.

- “Maybe I overpaid.”
- “Maybe there’s a better model coming next month.”
- “Maybe that other neighborhood would have been quieter.”
- “Maybe I should have kept looking.”

The cruel part is that *the grass isn’t just greener somewhere else—it’s greener everywhere else, forever.* When your brain is tuned to optimization, every alternative becomes a possible upgrade, and every choice becomes a potential mistake.

## The programmer’s version of the trap

This pattern shows up clearly in creative and technical work.

In programming, for example, maximizing can look like adding features nobody asked for. Polishing code long past the point where it meaningfully improves the user experience. Researching tools for hours when any of the top three options would work just fine.

It feels productive because it’s effort. It feels responsible because it’s “quality.”

But often it’s just avoidance dressed up as excellence.

You’re not building anymore—you’re searching. You’re not shipping—you’re optimizing. You’re not solving the problem—you’re proving you can solve it perfectly.

And in the meantime, “good” is sitting right there, ready to be used.

## The real cost of the search

Satisficers aren’t settling for mediocrity. They’ve just internalized a truth that maximizers resist:

**The search costs something.**

Every extra hour spent comparing is an hour you could have spent using the thing, enjoying the thing, learning through experience, or simply living.

And the costs aren’t only time.

Maximizing also tends to increase:

- **Decision fatigue** (because you’re constantly evaluating)
- **Opportunity cost** (because other parts of life wait while you optimize)
- **Regret** (because you can imagine more alternatives)
- **Anxiety** (because you feel responsible for finding the “best”)
- **Diminishing returns** (because small gains require huge effort)

At a certain point, “better” becomes so expensive that it stops being better.

## How to escape without lowering your standards

The path out of the Maximizer’s Trap isn’t to care less.

It’s to decide *sooner*.

A practical way to do this is to set **pre-commitment rules**—simple standards you choose in advance so you’re not negotiating with yourself forever.

For example:

- “I’ll look at 10 apartments max, then pick the best of those.”
- “I’ll spend 30 minutes reading reviews, then buy.”
- “This feature is ‘done’ when it solves the user’s problem and passes tests.”
- “I will choose a tool by Friday, and then I’m committed for three months.”

You’re not lowering the bar. You’re drawing a finish line.

Because a lot of maximizing isn’t actually about quality—it’s about the fear of closing doors. Pre-commitment helps you close them on purpose.

## Permission is a skill

The most underrated part of this is emotional, not logical.

You have to practice saying:

This is what it is.  
This choice is good enough.  
I’m allowed to stop searching now.

That’s not complacency. It’s maturity.

“Good enough” isn’t the enemy of great. **Endless searching is.**

## Closing thought

If you’re stuck in the Maximizer’s Trap, try shifting the goal from “make the perfect choice” to “make a solid choice and build a great life on top of it.”

The right decision matters—but not as much as your ability to live with it, commit to it, and move forward.
