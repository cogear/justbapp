# The Fixer

## The confession I don’t love making

Let me be honest about something that took me a long time to name.

I’m a fixer.

I’m the person who feels most alive when there’s a problem on the table. Something’s broken, someone’s stuck, the deadline is looming—suddenly I’m alert, energized, confident. I take pride in being the one who can figure it out.

And for a while, that story feels like the best kind of identity: reliable, capable, useful.

## The good story (and why it feels so good)

In almost every company I’ve worked for, I’ve become the person people turn to when something’s not working.

A system fails. A process gets messy. A teammate is overwhelmed. A meeting goes sideways. I’m there with solutions, frameworks, next steps. Sometimes I’m not even asked—I can *feel* the problem in the room and my brain starts assembling an answer like it’s a puzzle I’ve solved before.

On paper, this is admirable. It looks like leadership. It looks like generosity.

And sometimes it truly is.

Fixing can be a genuine gift: reducing stress, restoring momentum, helping someone feel supported. The world needs people who can troubleshoot and steady the ship.

But there’s another layer beneath the good story, and it’s harder to admit.

## The harder truth: fixing can be a selfish act

Here’s the part that stings.

Sometimes I don’t fix because it’s the most loving thing to do. I fix because it makes me feel important.

There’s a subtle ego rush that comes with being needed. The moment someone says, “Can you take a look at this?” my nervous system interprets it as proof of value. Fixing becomes a quick way to climb the invisible ladder: *I’m better than you, because you needed me.*

That’s not a flattering truth, but it’s an honest one.

And when I don’t acknowledge that motivation, it runs the show. I start seeking out broken things like a heat-seeking missile, not because they’re truly my responsibility, but because they offer an immediate hit of significance.

## When fixing becomes a way to avoid my own life

This is where it gets personal.

Being “the fixer” has pulled me away from my own goals. I’ve noticed how hard it is for me to focus in shared workspaces because some part of me is listening for distress signals. I’m half-working, half-waiting for a chance to be useful.

I’ve abandoned my own projects to fix things for others.

Not always because they needed me more, but because fixing feels better than building.

Building requires patience. Building is slow. You can work for hours and have nothing obvious to show for it. It asks for faith, for consistency, for the willingness to be a beginner.

Fixing is different. Fixing is immediate. There’s a clear problem, a clear solution, and a clear sense of completion. It offers quick closure—and quick praise.

Sometimes fixing is also a sneaky form of avoidance. If I’m busy solving someone else’s chaos, I don’t have to face my own uncertainty. I don’t have to sit with my own unfinished work, my own imperfect plan, my own fear of not being exceptional.

## Seeing clearly: acceptance as a form of freedom

It would be easier to keep the “helper” story and leave it there.

But the helper story is incomplete.

Here’s where acceptance comes in—not the kind that excuses everything, but the kind that tells the truth. Knowing this about myself—accepting it—is a kind of freedom. Not permission to keep doing it unchecked. Clarity about what I’m working with.

Acceptance doesn’t mean approving of everything you do. It means seeing clearly, without the blur of shame.

Shame keeps you vague. It makes you defensive, dramatic, or numb. Clarity makes you practical. You can spot the pattern in real time:

- The moment you perk up when someone struggles  
- The little surge of ego when you realize you have the answer  
- The temptation to jump in before you’re invited  
- The quiet relief of being needed instead of focusing on your own work  

Once you can see it, you have choices.

You can ask: *Did they ask for help?*  
You can pause: *Is this mine to fix?*  
You can consider: *What would serve them more—my solution, or their growth?*  
You can redirect: *What would happen if I stayed with my own work for one more hour?*

## Permission: you’re not your pattern

One of the most freeing shifts is letting go of fixing as an identity.

I’m not “a fixer” as some permanent label. I’m a person with a tendency—a well-practiced strategy—that developed for reasons that probably made sense at some point.

Maybe it earned love. Maybe it earned safety. Maybe it earned belonging.

But I don’t have to live inside it forever.

The goal isn’t to stop being capable or supportive. The goal is to help without feeding the ego. To contribute without disappearing from your own life. To choose fixing deliberately, not compulsively.

## Closing thought

The real growth isn’t in pretending you’re purely selfless. It’s in being honest about what you get from being needed—and then deciding, with open eyes, what you want to build when no one is watching.
