# The Cult of Busy

## The Answer We Give Without Thinking

Ask someone how they’re doing and notice how often the conversation takes a familiar turn.

“Busy.”  
“So busy.”  
“Crazy busy.”

We say it like a badge. Like we’ve been selected for some elite club where exhaustion is the entry fee.

And maybe we have. Somewhere along the way, being overwhelmed stopped sounding like a warning sign and started sounding like a status symbol.

If you’re busy, you must be needed. Important. In demand. If you’re not busy, the implication is uncomfortable: *What do you do all day?* Are you falling behind? Are you… replaceable?

That’s the quiet pressure underneath the casual answer.

## When Leisure Was the Flex

This obsession with busyness is strangely new.

For most of history, leisure was the real marker of wealth and power. The privileged classes weren’t admired for grinding themselves into the ground. They were admired for having time—time to think, to host, to create, to rest. Work was something done by servants, laborers, and people without options.

Today, we’ve flipped the script.

The modern hero is the person who wakes up at 4:30 a.m., answers emails while brushing their teeth, stacks meetings until dinner, then “catches up” late into the night. We applaud the eighty-hour week. We romanticize the hustle. We share productivity hacks like they’re secrets to salvation.

Rest, in this worldview, is suspicious. Rest has to be justified. You can’t just relax—you have to *earn* it. And even then, you should probably optimize it.

## The Performance of Productivity

Here’s the uncomfortable question: how much of our rushing is real necessity—and how much of it is performance?

Because looking busy and being effective are not the same thing.

Looking busy is:

- replying instantly so no one doubts your dedication  
- booking back-to-back meetings because empty space feels “unproductive”  
- keeping Slack open like a loyalty pledge  
- saying yes automatically, then resenting your calendar later  
- multitasking until nothing gets your full attention  

It’s not always intentional. Often it’s social. In many workplaces and communities, busyness is a kind of protection. If you’re visibly overloaded, no one can accuse you of not trying. If you’re constantly in motion, you don’t have to sit still long enough to ask harder questions about what you’re doing and why.

But the cost is real.

Chronic rushing correlates with worse health outcomes (stress, sleep disruption, inflammation), lower life satisfaction, and—ironically—lower productivity. When you’re always sprinting, your thinking narrows. Your creativity dries up. Your patience thins. You become reactive instead of intentional.

You get a lot done, but it doesn’t feel like it counts. Or it counts, but you don’t feel it.

## Why We Cling to Busy

Busyness offers more than a schedule. It offers an identity.

For many of us, “busy” is shorthand for:

- “I matter.”  
- “I’m contributing.”  
- “I’m keeping up.”  
- “I’m safe.”  

It can also be a way to avoid emptiness. Quiet can bring up uncomfortable emotions: uncertainty, grief, loneliness, a sense of not knowing what’s next. When your calendar is full, you don’t have to feel those things as sharply.

And in the age of AI, this gets even trickier.

As more tasks become automated, the old definition of productivity starts to wobble. If a tool can do in seconds what used to take you hours, what does “hard work” mean now? Some people respond by doubling down—filling the saved time with more tasks to prove their worth.

But what if that’s not the point?

What if the real upgrade isn’t doing more, faster… but living better, on purpose?

## Leaving the Cult (Without Burning Your Life Down)

You don’t have to quit your job or move to a cabin in the woods to step out of the cult of busy.

You can start smaller. Quietly. Practically.

Try this:

**Stop competing in the “overwhelmed Olympics.”**  
Next time someone asks how you are, experiment with a different answer: “I’m okay.” “I’m doing well.” “I’m tired, but I’m handling it.” Honest doesn’t have to be dramatic.

**Create margin like it’s a necessity, not a luxury.**  
Leave ten minutes between meetings. Protect one evening. Block a morning with no calls. Margin isn’t wasted time—it’s where your nervous system finally gets to exhale.

**Measure effectiveness, not activity.**  
At the end of the day, ask: “What actually mattered?” Not “How much did I do?” but “What moved the needle?” The answer is often smaller than your to-do list wants to admit.

**Let AI take the busywork—then resist refilling the space.**  
If automation gives you time back, don’t automatically spend it. Keep some of it. Use it for rest, learning, deep work, relationships, or simply being human.

## Permission to Be Well

Having time isn’t failure. Having ease isn’t laziness. Having margin isn’t something to apologize for.

We were never meant to perform our worth through exhaustion.

You don’t have to join the cult of busy. You can be well, actually—and that’s the real achievement.

A brief reflection to carry with you: if your life looked less busy, what would become possible—not just in your calendar, but in your mind?
