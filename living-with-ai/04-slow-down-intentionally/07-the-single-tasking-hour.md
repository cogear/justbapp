# The Single-Tasking Hour

## The Enemy of Presence

Multitasking sounds like a superpower. A way to squeeze more life into the same 24 hours.

But if you’ve ever tried to answer emails while half-listening to a meeting, scrolling your phone while eating, or “relaxing” with a show on while also checking messages… you already know the truth:

Multitasking is the enemy of presence.

When we do multiple things at once, we don’t do any of them fully. We’re physically here, mentally elsewhere—everywhere and nowhere.

And it’s not just a philosophical problem. Research suggests that what we call “multitasking” is mostly rapid task-switching. Your brain isn’t actually doing two complex things at the same time; it’s bouncing back and forth. That switching has a cost—time lost reorienting, more mistakes, lower comprehension, more stress.

Even more frustrating: heavy multitaskers often perform worse even when they’re not multitasking. It’s as if the habit trains the brain to crave novelty and interruption, making sustained attention feel uncomfortable.

So if you’ve been wondering why it’s harder to focus than it used to be, the issue might not be your willpower.

It might be your environment. And your habits.

## The Single-Tasking Zone

Here’s a simple counterspell: create a single-tasking zone.

Designate one hour per day—or even one hour per week—where you do one thing. Just one.

No phone beside you. No “quick check” of anything. No extra tabs waiting in the background. One activity gets your full attention, the way attention is meant to be used.

This isn’t about becoming a productivity robot. It’s about reclaiming your ability to be fully here.

And the best part is you can use your single-tasking hour for almost anything:

- **Work:** writing, designing, planning, coding, studying—anything that benefits from depth  
- **Leisure:** reading, walking, stretching, journaling, listening to music  
- **Connection:** a conversation with someone you care about, without half an eye on a screen  
- **Mundane life:** cooking, cleaning, organizing—done slowly and attentively  

The activity matters less than the quality of attention you bring.

Single-tasking turns ordinary moments into something richer. It’s not that the world changes. It’s that you finally meet it.

## Why It Feels So Different

Single-tasking often feels… weird at first.

If you’re used to constant switching, your mind may protest. You might feel the urge to check your phone, open a tab, or “make use of the time” by stacking tasks. This isn’t a sign you’re doing it wrong—it’s a sign you’re noticing the addiction to stimulation.

Then something shifts.

With fewer interruptions, the mind settles. Attention stops fragmenting. You start to experience a gentle momentum: one thought leading to the next, one page leading to the next, one step leading to the next.

This is why people describe single-tasking as **luxurious**. It creates space. It feels like time expands.

And it changes how you feel afterward.

An hour of scattered attention often leaves you tired and vaguely dissatisfied. You did a lot, but nothing landed. It’s like eating snacks all day and still feeling hungry.

An hour of single-tasking leaves you satisfied and actually accomplished. Same hour. Different quality entirely.

## The AI Era: Attention as a Daily Choice

Living with AI means living with unprecedented convenience—and unprecedented distraction.

There will always be another notification, another suggestion, another feed designed to keep you engaged. AI makes information faster, entertainment infinite, and task management frictionless. That’s a gift. But it also means you have to become more deliberate about what you give your mind to.

Single-tasking is one of the simplest ways to practice that deliberateness.

It’s not anti-technology. It’s pro-human.

It reminds you that your attention isn’t just a resource for apps and algorithms to harvest. It’s your life force. It shapes your relationships, your learning, your creativity, your emotional regulation—everything.

## Starting Today (Without Making It a Big Deal)

You don’t need to overhaul your routine. Just start small and specific.

Pick **one hour this week**.

Then set it up so you can actually succeed:

- Put your phone in another room (not just face down)  
- Close extra tabs or use full-screen mode  
- Tell someone you’ll be unreachable for an hour  
- Choose one clear task: “read,” “write,” “walk,” “clean the kitchen”  
- If your mind wanders, gently return—no drama  

If one hour feels too big, start with 20 minutes. The point isn’t perfection. The point is experiencing what it’s like when something gets all of you.

Because once you feel that difference, it becomes easier to choose it again.

## Permission

Here’s the quiet truth underneath all of this: you get to decide what receives your attention.

Not your inbox. Not the group chat. Not the endless stream of “just one more thing.”

You.

Give something all of you—just for an hour. See what happens when you show up completely.

A brief reflection to carry with you: attention isn’t only how you get things done. It’s how you live.
