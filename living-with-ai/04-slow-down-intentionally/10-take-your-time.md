# Take Your Time

## The phrase we say but rarely live

“Take your time.”

It’s one of those phrases we toss out automatically—when someone is deciding what to order, telling a story, finding the right words. It sounds kind. Spacious. Human.

But most of us don’t actually take our time. We borrow it from the margins. We squeeze it between meetings, errands, notifications, and the background hum of “what’s next?”

What if we treated time differently—not as something that’s always slipping away, but as something we’re allowed to claim?

Not in a dramatic, quit-your-job way. In a daily, practical way. In the way you take a breath before answering. In the way you let the kettle boil without filling the silence with scrolling.

## Reclaiming your relationship with time

This isn’t really about productivity hacks. It’s about relationship—your relationship with time and, by extension, your relationship with your own life.

So many of us have been trained into what you might call the cult of busy. Busy is proof you matter. Busy is a shield against discomfort. Busy is what you say when someone asks how you are, because “fine” feels too exposed and “tired” feels too true.

The problem isn’t that you have things to do. The problem is that “things to do” become the only mode you live in.

Reclaiming time starts with noticing the moments you’re already rushing through:

The breakfast you ate while standing at the counter. The morning you “got through” instead of lived. The walk you took while mentally composing emails.

These aren’t failures. They’re signals. And signals can be answered.

## Small transitions create real space

One of the simplest ways to slow down is to honor transitions.

We tend to treat transitions like inconveniences: the walk from the car to the office, the minute between calls, the pause after sending something important.

But transitions are where your nervous system catches up to your life.

Try letting them exist. Give yourself ten seconds before you open the next tab. Sit in your car for one breath before going inside. Stand up slowly instead of springing into motion like an alarm.

These micro-pauses don’t make you lazy. They make you present.

And presence, as it turns out, changes everything.

## Single-tasking is a form of respect

Multitasking isn’t just a habit—it’s a mindset that says, “This moment isn’t enough on its own.”

When you single-task, you’re not being old-fashioned. You’re practicing a kind of respect:

Respect for your attention. Respect for the person you’re talking to. Respect for your own mind.

You can start small. Eat without a screen once a day. Take a shower without planning your next three moves. Do one thing—just one—until it’s done.

At first, it might feel uncomfortable. Your brain will look for an escape hatch. That’s normal. We’ve trained ourselves to fill every quiet space.

But the reward is subtle and powerful: you begin to feel your life again.

## Slow meals teach you how to savor

A slow meal is about more than food. It’s training in savoring.

When you eat quickly, the meal disappears. When you eat slowly, it becomes a moment you can actually remember. Taste shows up. Texture shows up. Gratitude shows up. Sometimes conversation shows up.

You don’t need candlelight and a perfect table setting. You just need to stop treating eating like something to “get out of the way.”

If you can’t slow down every meal, choose one. Even a snack.

Let it take time. Let it be ordinary and still meaningful.

## Use “periods” to punctuate your day

Most days run together because nothing marks them. We move from one thing to the next like a sentence with no punctuation.

Try adding periods.

A short walk after work. Tea before you start your evening. A few minutes of stretching before bed. A small ritual that tells your brain: that part is complete.

These punctuation marks create a feeling of shape—and shape creates memory.

## The ninety-year-old test

Imagine you’re ninety, looking back.

What will you remember?

Not the tasks you completed. Not the emails you cleared. Not the efficient way you powered through your list.

You’ll remember the moments you were actually in.

The meal that lingered. The conversation that wandered. The ordinary afternoon when nothing happened—and somehow everything did.

People say, “The days are long but the years are short.” That becomes painfully true when we aren’t there for the days. When we rush through each moment, the moments blur. We look up and years have passed, and we can’t quite account for them.

Slowing down is one way of making sure your life is made of moments you can actually keep.

## The final permission

You can rush through your life, checking boxes, always heading toward a future that never quite arrives. That’s one way to live.

Or you can slow down—sometimes, intentionally. You can choose to inhabit your life at a speed that allows you to notice it.

This is what is. You are allowed to take your time.

To pause between things. To be where you are. To move at the pace that lets you be present.

Take your time. It’s the only one you have.
