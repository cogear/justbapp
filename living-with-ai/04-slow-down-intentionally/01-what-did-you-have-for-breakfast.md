# What Did You Have For Breakfast?

## A strangely hard question

Quick: what did you have for breakfast three days ago?

If you’re drawing a blank, you’re in good company. Most people can’t answer that without guessing. And it’s tempting to conclude the same thing every time: *It must not have been memorable.*

But that’s usually not the real reason.

The issue isn’t that the meal was boring. It’s that we weren’t fully there for it.

## The “elsewhere” habit

Breakfast is often the most “elsewhere” meal of the day.

We eat while scanning email. While reading headlines. While checking messages. While mentally rehearsing a conversation we’re nervous about. While building a to-do list in our head.

The body is at the table, but the mind has already left the room.

And when you’re not present for an experience, your brain doesn’t tag it as important. It doesn’t get filed as a moment worth storing. So it fades fast—sometimes before you even leave the kitchen.

This is how days start to blur.

Not because nothing is happening. Because our attention is split into so many tiny fragments that nothing fully lands.

## Why memory follows attention

Memory isn’t just about how “big” something is. It’s about how much of you showed up.

When you focus on something—taste, texture, smell, warmth, sound—you’re giving your brain data. You’re also giving it a signal: *this counts.*

When you multitask, you aren’t actually doing two things at once. You’re switching rapidly between tasks. That constant switching dilutes the experience. It’s like trying to take a photo while jogging: you might capture an image, but it’s blurred.

So it’s not surprising that you can remember a dramatic vacation meal from years ago, yet struggle to recall Tuesday’s toast.

## The meals you remember (and what they have in common)

Think of a meal you remember clearly.

Maybe it was dinner on a trip, somewhere you’d never been before. Or a holiday table at someone’s home. Or a quiet evening with people you love, talking long after the plates were cleared.

The food might have been incredible. Or it might have been simple.

What made it memorable often wasn’t the menu—it was the pace.

No rushing. No pressure to optimize the moment. No constant tug from a device. You lingered. You were there for the whole thing. Your attention wasn’t divided into ten different places.

Presence is the ingredient that makes a moment last.

And that’s the bigger point: this isn’t just about breakfast. It’s about the difference between experiencing your life and merely passing through it.

## Living with AI: why “more information” can mean less life

We live in a time where attention is under aggressive competition.

Phones aren’t just tools—they’re portals to endless “just one more” options. News, social media, messages, work dashboards, AI-generated summaries, reminders, recommendations. There’s always something else to check, something else to learn, something else to respond to.

And that’s the paradox: you can be informed, organized, and efficient—and still feel like your days are slipping through your fingers.

When you outsource too much of your attention to feeds and alerts, you don’t just lose focus. You lose *texture.* The small sensory anchors that make a day feel real.

A mindful breakfast is a tiny rebellion against that pattern. Not anti-tech. Just pro-presence.

## Tomorrow’s experiment: fifteen minutes that belong to you

Here’s an invitation that’s almost laughably simple:

Tomorrow morning, before you check your phone, eat breakfast. Just breakfast.

Fifteen minutes. No screens. No standing at the counter while scrolling. No “quick look” at notifications.

Sit down if you can. Eat slowly enough to notice the first few bites. Actually taste the food. Notice what “warm” feels like. Notice the crunch, the softness, the sweetness, the salt. Notice the urge to speed up.

If thoughts about the day show up (they will), you don’t need to fight them. Just return to the meal. Bite, chew, swallow. Sip. Breathe.

Then, later that evening, ask yourself: *Do I remember it?*

Not as a test. As a curiosity.

And notice something else, too: does the day feel different when it starts with presence instead of urgency?

## Permission to be where you are

If you’ve been missing your meals, it’s not because they’re not worth remembering.

It’s because you weren’t there.

That’s not a moral failure. It’s a modern default. The world trains us to treat every quiet moment as an opportunity to consume more information.

But you’re allowed to have fifteen minutes that don’t produce anything. Fifteen minutes that don’t optimize anything. Fifteen minutes that simply belong to your senses.

Be there for breakfast.

It’s a small practice—but it has a way of spreading. One fully experienced meal can become a more grounded morning, which can become a more intentional day.

## Closing thought

You don’t need a retreat or a new routine to start showing up for your life. You just need one ordinary moment, lived all the way through.

Tomorrow morning is waiting—quietly, patiently—to be remembered.
