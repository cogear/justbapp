# The Energy Audit

You know that feeling when you’re running on empty—but you can’t quite explain why?

Maybe you slept “enough,” your calendar looks normal, and nothing dramatic happened. And yet you’re depleted. Not just tired, but *drained*. The kind of drained that makes simple decisions feel heavy and small tasks feel oddly impossible.

An energy audit is a simple way to stop guessing. Instead of trying to “push through” or making big life changes based on vibes, you collect real information about where your energy is going—and what helps you get it back.

This isn’t about becoming perfect or hyper-optimized. It’s about seeing clearly.

## Where Does Your Energy Actually Go?

Most of us track time more than we track energy.

We know what we did all day, but not what it *cost*. Two people can have the same schedule and feel completely different by 6 p.m. because energy isn’t just about hours—it’s about friction.

Energy drains can hide in plain sight:

- The meeting that seems short but leaves you tense for hours  
- The “quick” text thread that turns into emotional labor  
- The background anxiety of too many open loops  
- The task you avoid all day that quietly taxes you the whole time  

Until you name the pattern, it all blends into one vague sense of “I’m exhausted.” And vague problems are hard to fix.

## The Audit: A One-Week Experiment

For one week, track your energy levels throughout the day.

Set a reminder every few hours (or tie it to natural transitions: morning, midday, afternoon, evening). When the reminder goes off, do a quick check-in and label your state:

- **Energized**
- **Neutral**
- **Depleted**

That’s it. Keep it simple so you’ll actually do it.

Then add two quick notes:

1. **What were you doing?**  
   Working, commuting, scrolling, cooking, in a meeting, alone, cleaning, planning, parenting—whatever it is.

2. **Who were you with (if anyone)?**  
   A specific person, a group, coworkers, clients, friends, family, or “alone.”

If you can, add one more layer that makes the audit much more useful:

### Identify the domain of depletion

Not all tired is the same tired. Try labeling what feels most affected:

- **Physical:** heaviness, sleepiness, low stamina, body tension  
- **Mental:** foggy thinking, decision fatigue, inability to focus  
- **Emotional:** irritability, sadness, numbness, feeling “raw”  
- **Social:** wanting to withdraw, feeling over-peopled, low capacity for interaction  

Why does this matter? Because the remedy depends on the diagnosis.

A nap might help physical fatigue but do nothing for emotional depletion. A long walk might clear mental fog but not restore you after a difficult conversation. When you know what kind of energy you’re losing, you can choose recovery that actually works.

## Finding Patterns (Without Making It Personal)

At the end of the week, look back over your check-ins. You’re not searching for perfection—you’re looking for trends.

Start with a few straightforward questions:

### What activities drain you the most?
Notice the repeat offenders. Some will be unavoidable (certain work responsibilities, caregiving, life admin). But others may be optional or adjustable.

Ask yourself:

- Can I reduce how often I do this?
- Can I shorten it?
- Can I batch it?
- Can I get support, delegate, or automate part of it?
- Can I change *how* I do it so it costs less?

This is where living with AI can quietly help: not by replacing your life, but by lowering the friction. Drafting an email, summarizing notes, creating a plan, or generating a first version of something can reduce mental drain and decision fatigue—especially during low-energy windows.

### What energizes you?
Look for what reliably lifts you, even a little. Often it’s not the “big self-care” stuff. It’s small, repeatable moments:

- A focused work sprint with clear goals  
- Movement that feels good, not punishing  
- Time outside  
- Creative play  
- Clean surfaces and fewer open loops  
- Deep conversation with the right person  
- Quiet solitude without guilt  

If your week contains almost none of what energizes you, that’s not a moral failure—it’s a systems issue. The audit helps you see the gap.

### Who drains you? Who restores you?
This part can feel uncomfortable, so keep it neutral. The goal isn’t judgment. Relationships are complex, and some people are absolutely worth the energy they require.

But it’s still useful to notice:

- Do you feel lighter or heavier after interacting?
- Do you feel seen—or managed?
- Do you feel like you have to perform?
- Are you doing most of the emotional work?

Once you see the pattern, you can be strategic: shorter interactions, clearer boundaries, different timing, more recovery afterward, or balancing draining connections with nourishing ones.

### When are your low points?
You might discover you consistently dip:

- Mid-afternoon  
- After certain meetings  
- On Sunday evenings  
- After long stretches without alone time  

This is gold. If you know when you tend to crash, you can plan renewal *around* it instead of being surprised every time.

## The Goal Isn’t Zero Drains—It’s Strategy

Life costs energy. That’s normal.

The point of an energy audit isn’t to eliminate every draining activity or never feel tired again. It’s to become intentional:

- Protect your energy where you can  
- Spend it where it matters  
- Build recovery into the places you already know you’ll need it  

This is how you move from “I don’t know what’s wrong with me” to “I see what’s happening—and I have options.”

## Permission: Let It Be True

There’s something quietly powerful about writing it down.

Not because the data is fancy, but because it gives you permission to stop arguing with reality. You can’t manage what you don’t see. The audit makes the invisible visible—and that changes everything.

See where your energy goes. Then you can decide where you want it to go.

A brief reflection to end: if you did nothing this week except notice, you’d still be moving forward—because awareness is the first form of care.
