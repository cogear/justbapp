# Beaches, piers, and boardwalks

*How the edge of the water became the easiest place in the world to belong.*

## Already there

There's a pier near where I used to live. Most mornings, before the light was fully up, the same four or five people were already on it, fishing. Nobody organized that. No one sent an invitation. No one takes attendance.

The pier just sits there, open at both ends, and people come.

That sounds almost too simple to mention, but it's worth slowing down on. A beach has no host. It's nobody's living room. There are rules, sure — posted ones about hours and fires and dogs — but there's no person whose gathering it is. No one is curating the guest list. No one is checking that you're contributing enough. The place is just *there*, and you can be there too.

That's not a small thing. It's the whole reason it works.

## Something to look at

Water solves a problem that indoor rooms can't.

In a room, if you're not talking, something feels off. Someone has to fill the silence or explain it. On a pier, you're both looking at the same horizon. The silence is the point. You can stand next to a stranger for twenty minutes watching the same swell come in, and it isn't awkward at all. The water is doing the talking. It's always doing something — a shift in the light, a bird cutting through, a wave that breaks differently from the last one — and that's enough to hold your shared attention.

Piers and beaches do something else too. They give you a reason to be there that isn't *meeting people*. You're fishing. You're walking. You're watching boats. You're looking for shells. The purpose makes the standing around legal. You don't have to justify your presence to anyone because the place itself supplies the justification. You're doing what people do at the edge of the water.

This matters because it removes the social pressure entirely. You're not there to perform. You're not there to network. You're there because the pier is there and so are you, and that's enough.

## A hallway with no rooms

A boardwalk is a hallway with no rooms attached. You walk one direction, then you turn around and walk back. Everybody passes everybody, twice. There's something about that rhythm — the slow repetition, the same faces going the opposite way — that makes familiarity build on its own.

And the dress code flattens everyone out. Shorts, sandals, sunburn. It's hard to tell who has money at the beach. Hard to tell who's supposed to be important. The boardwalk doesn't care about your title. It doesn't care about your shoes because everyone's shoes are flip-flops or nothing.

It costs nothing, or close to it. That matters more than we admit. A place isn't really public if you have to keep buying something to stay in it. The coffee shop that lets you sit for two hours is being generous, but you still feel the low hum of obligation. The beach never asks you to buy a thing. You can be there from dawn to dark and no one will tap you on the shoulder.

## February people

The regulars aren't the crowd in July.

They're the ones who show up in February, when the wind is bad and the fried-food stands are shuttered. The man with the folding chair. The woman who swims when the water is far too cold. The guy who walks to the end of the pier and back, every single day, in any weather.

These are the people who make a third place a third place. Not because they decided to, but because they kept showing up until the showing up became the thing.

When talk does start, it starts sideways. Something about the tide. Whether anything's biting. A comment about the wind. Nobody asks what you do for a living. Nobody needs to. The conversation stays close to the ground — close to the water, close to the weather, close to what's right in front of you. That kind of talk has a low ceiling and a warm floor. You don't have to be interesting. You just have to be there.

You become one of them by repetition, not by introducing yourself. Show up enough times at the same hour and eventually you get the nod. That's the whole membership process. No form, no fee, no test. Just the nod.

## You can just leave

And then you leave.

That's the part I keep coming back to. No round of goodbyes. No *thanks for having me*. No favour sitting there waiting to be returned. You didn't borrow anything and you don't owe anything. You drive home with sand in your shoes and the whole exchange is complete.

You can come back tomorrow. You can skip a month. Nothing about the place holds it against you. The pier will still be there. The water will still be doing something. The regulars will still be doing their regular things, and if you show up again they'll give you the nod like you never left.

People have gathered at the edge of the water for as long as there have been people. You don't have to arrange it. You don't have to host it. You don't have to bring anything.

You just have to go and stand there.
