# Parks as third places

*A place near you where nobody is expecting you — and that's the whole point.*

## A gap in the hedge

There's a place near you where nobody is expecting you. No reservation. No dress code. No reason you have to give anybody. You walk in through a gap in a hedge, and that's the whole procedure.

It's the park. Grass, a path, some trees somebody planted eighty years ago. Probably the plainest public room your city owns. Also one of the last ones that costs nothing.

We tend to overlook parks when we think about where community happens. They don't advertise. They don't have a brand. Nothing about them announces that they're doing important social work. But that quietness is exactly what makes them work. A park doesn't need to announce itself because it isn't trying to attract you. It's just there. Open. Waiting for no one in particular, which means it's waiting for everyone equally.

## Everything asks first

Think about where you actually see people. Most of it asks something first. A purchase. A membership. A code on a keypad. Somebody's name on the booking, holding a table for seven.

And the warmest option — someone's home — asks the most. An invitation you have to receive. A thank-you. A turn to have them back. It's a kindness. It's also a small ledger, and part of you keeps count.

So whole weeks go by with almost no company in them. Not because we stopped wanting people. Because every road to people had a form to fill in first.

This is the quiet erosion of casual company. The structures that used to hold low-stakes social life — benches, corners, open ground — have thinned out. What's left mostly asks you to commit in advance. The park is one of the few exceptions. You don't commit. You show up.

## Nobody's ground

A park has no host. Nobody stands at the edge of the grass to greet you. Nobody notices whether you came today. Nobody is owed an explanation when you go.

The ground there doesn't belong to anyone. Which is another way of saying it belongs to everyone the same amount. You're not a guest. You're a person standing on public land, like all the others.

Status doesn't travel well across a lawn, either. Out here, the man with the good job and the man looking for one are just two men in a park. The usual signals — clothes, cars, the way a doorway opens for you — lose their traction on open ground. A park flattens things, not by force, but by indifference. It simply doesn't care who you are, and that indifference is a relief.

That's neutral ground. The entry fee is showing up. There is no second fee.

## Becoming a regular

Here's the part that surprises people. Go at roughly the same hour, along roughly the same loop, and the park slowly fills with faces you recognise.

The man doing slow laps with his hands behind his back. The woman who stretches against the fence. You might not learn their names for a year. You'd notice straight away if they stopped coming.

Some days it's a nod. Some days it's ten minutes about nothing — the tree they took down, the new fence, the state of the path. Both count.

That's how a regular is made. Not by effort. By repetition. By being in one place often enough that your absence would register. Nobody signs you up. Nobody welcomes you. You just keep arriving, and one day the place knows you back.

## Leaving freely

And when you've had enough, you leave. No round of goodbyes. No coat to hunt for in a bedroom. Nobody to reassure that you had a lovely time.

You step back into your evening a little lighter than you arrived, carrying nothing you'll have to return.

The gate is open. It's been open the whole time. You don't need an invitation to a place that was never anyone's to give.

Maybe that's what we keep forgetting to value. Not the big gatherings, not the planned evenings, but the open ground that asks nothing and gives back company anyway. The park will be there tomorrow. So will the gap in the hedge. All you have to do is walk through it.
