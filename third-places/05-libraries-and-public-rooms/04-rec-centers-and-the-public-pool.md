# Rec centers and the public pool

*A room that belongs to anyone with four dollars, where belonging doesn't start with a conversation.*

## Six fifteen

Six fifteen in the morning. The public pool. Four lanes. Six people. The same six who were here yesterday, and the day before that.

Nobody organized this. No group chat. No sign-up sheet. No one is hosting. And still, most mornings, it happens anyway.

The chlorine smell. The echo. Somebody's kickboard slapping the water. This is a room, and it belongs to everyone who paid the four dollars.

There's something steadying about that. The pool doesn't care what kind of week you had. It was open at six. It will be open at six tomorrow. You can count on that in a way you can't always count on people.

## Waiting to be asked

We tend to think belonging starts with a conversation. Somebody notices you. Somebody says, come sit with us. Until that happens, we assume we're only in a building.

And a lot of rooms that welcome you want something back. You were invited, so now you owe a visit. You joined the group, so now you owe your presence next month. Warmth with a small debt attached. It's not wrong — it's just not free.

So we wait to be asked. And we stay home on a Tuesday, in a town with a warm public room that's open until nine, because it hasn't occurred to us that we could just go. That the door is the invitation. That the posted hours are the welcome.

## The water does the talking

The rec center doesn't work that way. It's public. A few dollars, sometimes free with a library card. The doors open at a posted hour and close at a posted hour, and the person at the desk does not need you to come back.

That last part matters more than it sounds. Most rooms in our lives are run by someone who needs something from us — our attention, our participation, our RSVP. The rec center desk needs nothing. You're not keeping it afloat by showing up. You're just in it.

And the activity does the talking. Lap swimming. Pickup basketball. The row of bikes by the window. You are beside people, doing a thing, and nobody has to fill the silence.

Which is exactly why talking starts. In the shallow end, catching your breath. On the bench between games. Small, unhurried, about the water temperature and not much else. The conversation happens because you're already sharing a space, not because anyone is working to create one.

No host. No invitation. Nothing owed on the way out.

## Pick an hour

Becoming a regular here is mostly a matter of picking a time. The eleven a.m. crowd is a different crowd than the six a.m. one. The evening lap swimmers don't overlap much with the morning water aerobics people. Choose an hour that fits your life, and keep going back to it.

Three weeks in, you'll know things you never asked to know. Who swims slow in the fast lane. Whose shoulder is bad this month. Who always forgets their towel. They'll learn your first name, or they won't, and either way you're part of the picture.

You don't have to declare yourself. You don't have to introduce yourself to the room. You just keep arriving at the same time, and the room does the rest.

And you are not on a roster. Skip a week. Skip a season. Come back in March and the lane is there, the same as it was. Nobody is keeping score. Nobody is wondering where you went. You step back in and you're a regular again by the second lap.

## Permission

There's something else about the pool. Everyone is half-blind without their glasses. Everyone looks a little ridiculous in a cap. Whatever you do for a living is hanging on a hook in the locker room.

You don't have to be good at it. Nobody is grading your stroke. You can swim badly for years in a public pool, and the public pool will keep letting you in. You can be the slowest person in the lane and the lane will not judge you. It will hold the same amount of water either way.

And the leaving is the quiet gift. No goodbye. No explanation. Nobody at the door asking when you'll be back. You just go.

Show up. Do your laps. Nod at the person in the next lane. Go home. That's the whole thing.
