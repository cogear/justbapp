# Laundromats

*The wash costs the same for all of us, and the room asks nothing more than that.*

## Forty Minutes

Somewhere near you there's a room that's warm, and bright, and open later than almost anything else. Nobody owns the chairs. Nobody's expecting you.

It smells like soap and hot cotton. There's a hum you can feel through your shoes.

And the machines will keep you there for forty minutes, whether you planned on it or not. You can't hurry a rinse cycle.

That forty minutes is the part we tend to resent. But it's also the part that makes the room what it is. The machines set the pace, and for once, that pace isn't yours to optimize. You're on the clock of something larger and slower than your phone. There's a strange relief in that, if you let it land.

## Waiting in the Car

Most of us treat that room as an errand. Load the machine, wait in the car. Or stand by the door, scrolling, watching the timer count down.

That makes sense. We learned somewhere that waiting is wasted time, and that a room only counts if something's happening in it. If nothing is being produced or consumed or performed, the room must not be worth staying in.

So we spend the forty minutes trying to be somewhere else. And then we drive home to a quiet apartment, and the week is a little emptier than it needed to be.

The irony is that the emptiness isn't from a lack of people. There are people right there, three chairs over, doing exactly what you're doing. The emptiness comes from having decided the room doesn't count.

## No One's House

Here's what I'd offer instead. That room is one of the last places you can go where nobody is hosting.

No one invited you. No one's house it is. Nobody at the door deciding if you belong.

That matters more than it sounds. Most rooms come with a debt — an invitation to return, a favor to repay, a thank-you owed on the way out. Someone's home means someone cleaned for you, someone planned around you, someone will notice if you leave early. That's lovely in its own way, but it's not free. It is never free.

Here, you paid the machine. That's the whole arrangement. You owe the room nothing and the room owes you nothing. You can arrive when you like and leave when you like, and no one will think twice about either.

And everyone's there for the same plain reason. Dirty clothes don't care what you do for a living. The wash costs the same for all of us. There's a leveling that happens in a laundromat that's hard to manufacture anywhere else. The lawyer and the line cook are both watching the same spin cycle, hoping the same dryer doesn't eat their quarters.

Which makes talking easy, because nothing's riding on it. Which dryer runs hot. Whether the change machine got fixed. You can talk, or you can just sit. Both are fine. The silence in a laundromat isn't awkward the way it is at a dinner party, because no one is performing hospitality and no one is receiving it. You're just in a room together, and the room is doing the work.

## Same Night, Same Chair

So try this. Pick the same night each week. Tuesday, say. Bring your laundry, and one other thing — a book, a crossword, nothing at all.

Then sit down. Not in the car. In the plastic chair by the window, where the light off the machines pools on the floor.

Do that four or five weeks and something happens without any effort from you. The attendant nods. The man with the green basket nods. You've become a regular, and nobody had to arrange it.

Nothing has to be said for it to count. Being known by face is a kind of belonging too — smaller than friendship, and steadier than you'd think. It won't show up in your contacts list. It won't ask how you're doing in a way that requires a real answer. But on a hard Tuesday, someone recognizing you by the door can be the difference between a night that feels like nothing and a night that feels like something.

And when the dryer stops, you fold your clothes and you leave. No goodbyes owed. Nobody tracking when you'll be back. That freedom to leave is what makes the arriving safe. You stay because you want to, not because you're trapped.

## Permission

You don't need a better reason to be somewhere than clean clothes. The laundry is the ticket, and the ticket is enough.

You are allowed to count a plain room with strangers in it as company. You're allowed to stay the whole cycle. You're allowed to walk out the moment it ends.

Warm clothes in your arms, cold air on the walk home. That's a good enough Tuesday.
