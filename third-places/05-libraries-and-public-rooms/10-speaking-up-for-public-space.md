# Speaking up for public space

*The room is free when you walk in. Keeping it that way is something else.*

## Free to you

You've been walking through a door that doesn't charge you anything.

No ticket. No membership. Nobody asking why you came or how long you plan to stay. You sit down. You read. You look out the window. You leave when you feel like it.

That's the whole arrangement, and it's easy to take it for granted, because it asks nothing of you.

But free to you isn't the same as free. Someone pays for that room. Someone voted for it. Usually years ago. Usually quietly. A decision made at a council meeting you never heard about, by people you never met, funded by neighbors who may never set foot inside.

The heat. The lights. The chairs. The hours the doors stay unlocked. All of it is a decision somebody makes again every single year.

## How rooms close

Places like this rarely close all at once. They get trimmed.

Sunday goes first. Then the evening hours. Then the branch across town. A staff position disappears. The summer reading program gets quieter. Nothing dramatic. Just less.

And it happens quietly. There's no fight, because the people who use the room and the people who decide about the room almost never speak to each other. One group sits in the chairs. The other group looks at a budget. They are in the same town but in different conversations.

And once a room like that is gone, it doesn't come back. Nobody builds a new free room. They just stop keeping the old one. The building becomes something else. The chairs go to storage. The door locks, and it stays locked.

## You are the number

Here's the part I didn't understand for years. These rooms survive on two things. How much they get used. And whether anyone says out loud that they matter.

The first one you already do.

Every time you walk in, you're counted. The card scan at the entrance. The head count at four o'clock. That's you, arguing for the place without saying a word. Not because you meant to argue. Just because you showed up, and showing up is data.

The person deciding the budget has probably never been in that room on a Tuesday afternoon. They're looking at a number on a page. If the number is good, the room stays open. If it's not, it doesn't.

You are the number. That's not a metaphor. That's literally how it works.

## Ninety seconds

The second part takes about ninety seconds.

There's usually a card on the counter, or a survey nobody fills in. A comment box near the door. A form on the website. Write the plain truth. Something like: I come here on Thursdays. Please keep the Thursday hours.

That's it. You don't need to be eloquent. You need to be specific and you need to exist on the record.

When a levy shows up on a ballot — that small line about the library, the pool, the parks — that's the room. That's the heat and the lights and the unlocked door, reduced to a line item most people skip past. The people who vote yes are the people who remember being inside.

And when someone asks what you use it for, tell them. Three sentences in an email to a city council member counts. So does saying it once to the person at the desk, who will pass it along. So does mentioning it to a neighbor who didn't know the room existed.

Nobody's asking you to run anything. Not to organize, not to lead, not to take charge of a room that isn't yours. Your role hasn't changed. You show up. You sit down. You leave freely. But somewhere, on a form or in an email or to a person standing near you, you say that the place matters. That's the whole ask.

## Owed forward

You still owe this place nothing.

That's important, and it's the part that makes these rooms what they are. You can come in, sit for an hour, and leave without a word to anyone. No reciprocity. No debt. No obligation to return the favor. The door is open, and that's the entire transaction.

But the door was open when you got here because someone you'll never meet said it should be. Years ago, maybe decades ago, someone wrote a letter or showed up to a meeting or voted for a levy, and the door stayed unlocked.

You can do that much for whoever walks in next.

You don't have to love it. You don't have to belong to it. You don't have to give it a name or a story or a place in your identity. You just have to say it matters — once, plainly — and then go about your day.

Ninety seconds now. A door open later. That's the trade.
