# Town halls and the meetings nobody attends

*A room with folding chairs and no host is the most overlooked third place in your town.*

## Rows of empty chairs

There's a room in your town with folding chairs set out in rows. Tonight, most of them will stay empty.

The meeting is on the calendar. The doors are unlocked at seven. Nobody is going to call and make sure you know.

We tend to read that as a shame. I've started reading it as an opening.

## Somebody else's room

We assume a room like that belongs to somebody else. To the people who know the acronyms. To whoever shows up angry with a printout about a fence.

We assume you need a reason. A grievance, a proposal, a stake in the item on page four. Something to justify the chair you're taking up.

So we stay home. And the room stays open anyway. Lit, heated, mostly empty, week after week.

This is one of the quieter costs of modern life. We've forgotten that civic rooms exist for us. Not for the officials at the front table. Not for the activists with prepared remarks. For anyone who walks in and sits down. That's the whole idea. A public room is public. The chairs are for whoever comes.

## No host

Here's what's strange and good about a public meeting. Nobody's hosting it.

There's a chair, and an agenda, and a gavel. There is no host. No one greets you. No one takes your coat or asks how you heard about it. No one is keeping track of whether you came back.

Nobody invited you, which means nobody can un-invite you. That room is open because a room like that is required to be open. It was open before you thought of it.

This is the quality that makes a place a genuine third place. Neutral ground. No invitation needed. No reciprocity debt. You don't owe the room anything in exchange for being in it. Not a comment, not a signature, not a follow-up email. You can sit through forty minutes of drainage easements and leave at eight-fifteen. No one will ask where you're going.

Try finding that at a dinner party. A book club. A friend's apartment. Those are good things, but they come with strings. You were invited. You are expected to contribute. You will be noticed if you leave early. The public meeting asks nothing and tracks nothing. It just stays open.

## Go with nothing

So: find the calendar. Your town keeps one. Planning commission, school board, library trustees, the transit committee nobody has heard of. Pick whichever meets closest to your door.

Go with nothing. No cause, no question, no printout. Sit in the back, where the chairs are cold and the coffee is bad. Let it be boring. Most of it will be boring.

Then go again. Around the second or third time, you'll start to see them. The woman with the thermos. The retired man taking notes in pencil. The two who argue in the parking lot and then drive home in the same car.

You'll learn things you never planned to learn. Where the sidewalk money goes. Which streetlight has been out since March. Who owns the empty lot behind the grocery store.

This is how third places work. You don't build them. You don't host them. You just keep showing up until the place becomes familiar and the faces become familiar and you realize you are one of the faces now. It happens without effort, without a membership form, without anyone making space for you. You made the space yourself by sitting in it.

## The whole membership

Those are the regulars. Not a club. Nobody's collecting dues, nobody's assigning you a role. Just people who kept turning up until the room knew their faces.

You can become one of them by doing nothing but arriving. That's the entire requirement. Show up, sit down, go home when you want to.

A town isn't only its streets and its shops. It's also a fluorescent room on a Tuesday night, with more chairs than people in it, waiting to see who comes in.

The doors are unlocked. They have been all along.
