# What a screen can't replace

*A screen can carry a conversation across an ocean. It just can't hand you a room.*

## The room itself

A screen does real things. It can hold a room of people awake at three in the morning. It can carry a conversation across an ocean. That matters, and we shouldn't pretend it doesn't.

But there's one thing it can't hand you. It can't put you in a room with other bodies.

Which sounds obvious. It isn't. Almost everything a third place does for you runs through that one plain fact. A screen is very good at delivering people to you. It's not good at delivering a place. And the place is doing more work than we tend to give it credit for.

## Everything it has to remove

Think about what a screen has to strip away in order to work. Smell. Temperature. The noise at the next table. The way a room sounds half full versus full. The person who walks past your chair. The dog under the stool. The rain somebody tracks in on their shoes.

All the things nobody chose to include.

Those aren't decoration. That's the texture your body reads, underneath thinking, to decide whether it's safe to loosen its grip. A room tells your nervous system something. It says: you are here, among others, and nothing is required of you right now. A screen can't send you a room's temperature. It sends you words. Words are good. They're just not the same thing.

## Quiet is company

Here's the part that surprised me. In a good room you can say nothing for an hour and still not be alone. Sitting near people counts. Your body counts it.

Online, silence reads as absence. Stop typing and you're gone. There's no way to simply be there, quietly, at the edge of things. The platform doesn't know what to do with you if you're not producing something — a message, a reaction, a sign that you're still in the room.

In a physical third place, quiet is company. You can nurse a coffee, half-listen, nod at the man who always takes the corner seat. Nothing owed. Nothing performed. You're not contributing content. You're contributing presence, and that turns out to be enough.

This is one of the core characteristics Oldenburg described — the third place as a space where conversation is the main activity, but where you're free to be a spectator too. You can listen without speaking. You can drift in and out of attention. The room holds you either way.

## Arriving and leaving

There's also the walk. Ten minutes there, ten minutes back. Your body gets to arrive somewhere and then leave somewhere. A browser tab gives you none of that. There's no threshold to cross, no door to push open, no shift from street to interior. One moment you're somewhere else. The next moment you're "there." And then you're not.

And being recognised in person is a different thing. Someone lifts a chin. Pours your usual without asking. You didn't earn it and you didn't request it. You just kept showing up. That's how becoming a regular actually happens. Not a profile. Not a username. A face the room has seen enough times to stop wondering about.

Leaving is different too. You stand up, push the chair in, step out into the evening. The visit ends in your legs. Closing a tab ends nothing. The body doesn't register it as a departure, because there was never an arrival.

## Permission

None of this makes screens wrong. It makes them partial. Some things only travel through air. Shared weather. Being close enough to hear someone laugh at something you didn't hear. The way light changes in a room as the afternoon turns. The particular hum of a space where people are choosing to be, without anyone having organised them.

So keep the screens. And every so often, put your actual body in a room with people who owe you nothing. Order something. Stay a while longer than you meant to.

No goodbyes required. Nothing owed on the way out. That isn't rudeness. That's the whole design.
