# Using online to find offline

*A screen is a poor place to sit with people — but it's very good at pointing you toward where they actually sit.*

## What the screen is good for

Here's something worth saying plainly: a screen is a poor place to sit with people.

But it's very good at one thing. It knows where things are. And when they happen. And how to get there.

That's not a small thing. Most of the reason people don't have a place to go is simply that they don't know where it is. Not because the place doesn't exist. Not because their town is barren or their neighborhood is unfriendly. They just don't have the address.

You picture a village pub or a barbershop from a film, and you assume your town doesn't have one. Usually it does. It just isn't advertised where you're looking. It's not on the feed. It's not curated for you. It's just there, on a street you've driven down a hundred times, with its door open and its lights on.

## Addresses and hours

Somewhere online, right now, there's a page listing a chess night at a café. A run group that leaves from the bridge at seven. A library noticeboard someone photographed and posted. A thread for your city where somebody asked the exact question you're asking.

Opening hours. A recurring time. An address. A note that says the back room fills up around eight.

This is what the internet is genuinely good for. Not being the place. Pointing at it. A map is not the country. But it's how you find the country.

The mistake is treating the map like the destination. You scroll through photos of the café and feel like you've been. You read the thread about the run group and feel like you've joined. You haven't. You've looked at a map. The map did its job. Now it's your turn.

## One question

But not everything you find is a place you can just walk into. So here's the filter. One question.

Would this be happening tonight whether or not I showed up?

If the answer's yes — the doors open at six, the tables are already there, people drift in — that's the kind of thing you're looking for. Nobody's hosting. Nobody's counting heads. Nobody will notice if you come late or leave early or skip a week.

If it's an invitation — someone's living room, a guest list, a reply you'd feel bad breaking — that's something else. Lovely, maybe. But it comes with a debt. It comes with someone who planned it for you, who will notice your absence, who you'll feel you owe.

The other thing doesn't. That's the whole point. No host. No obligation. No reciprocity. You arrive because you felt like it. You leave when you feel like it. Nobody is keeping score.

Would this happen without me? If the answer's yes, you can also leave whenever you like. You can sit for twenty minutes and go home. You can stay three hours. The place doesn't care. It'll be there next week either way.

## To the threshold

Use the screen to lower the cost of the first visit. That's its real gift — reducing the friction of showing up somewhere new.

Find the door. Learn whether you order at the counter or wait to be seated. Find out what time the room actually fills, so you're not the first one standing there alone. Look at a photo so the space is already a little familiar when you walk in.

Then close the laptop. That's where its job ends. At the doorway. It can carry you to the threshold. It can't walk you in.

You'll be new. You'll probably sit near the edge and nurse a drink and leave after forty minutes. That's fine. Nobody's watching as closely as you think. People in third places are mostly doing their own thing — reading, chatting with the person next to them, staring into space. They are not evaluating you. They are not waiting for you to perform. You're just another body in the room, and that's all you need to be.

The part that matters isn't the finding. It's the going back. Same place, next week. That's all a regular is. Someone who came back.

## Permission

You don't have to organize anything. You don't have to start a group, or post about it, or turn it into a project. You don't have to invite anyone. You don't have to bring something.

You just have to know where the door is, and then use it. Once. And then again.

The screen found it for you. You're the one who gets to be there.
