# Where Oldenburg's criteria break down

*A short checklist defines what makes a place a third place. Hold it up against a room on a screen, and a few of the items quietly snap.*

There's a short list of things that make a place a third place. Eight characteristics, drawn from Ray Oldenburg's work. You don't need to memorize all eight to use them. You just need to hold them up against a room and see what holds.

Do that with a room on a screen — a forum, a group chat, a comment section, whatever you happen to live inside — and something interesting happens. Some items pass without a problem. Conversation flows. People return. There's a sense of familiarity. Some items bend a little but still hold, more or less.

And a few of them just snap. Quietly, in ways that are very hard to notice from the inside.

Today, just the ones that break. Not to win an argument about screens. Not to take a side. Just so you know what you're actually standing in.

## Who owns the room

Start with neutral ground. That's the first and maybe the most important item on the list. In a third place, nobody at the table owns the table. Someone sells you the coffee and then leaves you completely alone. The rules of the room are older than anyone sitting in it. You don't owe the owner anything. The owner doesn't owe you anything. That neutrality is what lets everyone relax.

Online, every room has a landlord. Somebody owns the machine it lives on. Somebody wrote the terms of service, and can rewrite them tonight while you sleep. Somebody reads what you said and sells what they learn from it. The platform decides what's visible, what gets buried, what disappears.

That isn't a footnote. Neutral ground is the wall the whole structure leans on. When the ground is owned by someone with a stake in what you do there, it isn't neutral anymore. It's borrowed. And borrowed ground feels different under your feet, even if you can't quite name why.

## Someone decides if you belong

The whole point of a third place is that no one is hosting. There's no invitation, so there's nothing to accept, and no favour to return. You walk in because the door is open. You sit down. You exist there without having been wanted or unwanted by any particular person. That absence of a host is what makes it restful. Nobody is taking care of you. Nobody is performing welcome.

Online, someone approves you. Someone hands you a role, or doesn't. Someone can end your sentence halfway through. A moderator, an admin, an algorithm — whatever the shape, there is someone whose function is to decide whether you get to be here. However kind that person is, that's a host. And you arrived by invitation, whether you received one explicitly or not.

Being a guest, even a welcome one, is a different feeling in the body than being a regular. Guests behave. Guests are grateful. Guests can be asked to leave. Regulars just are. The difference sounds small. It isn't.

## The scoreboard and the clock

Then there's the leveller. In a third place, your standing is simply what you're like to sit near. Nobody sees your salary. Nobody sees your résumé. You get to be nobody in particular, and you get to be valued or not valued based on the texture of your presence, not your credentials.

Online, your name arrives with numbers attached. Post counts. Followers. A badge for how long you've been here. A karma score. A verification check. Ranks don't level a room. They sort it. It's hard to be nobody in particular where there's a scoreboard hanging over the whole space, quietly ranking everyone in it.

And a third place closes. That's part of the gift. You step out, the door locks behind you, and your life carries on without it until you choose to come back. The boundary gives the place its shape. It's a place because it has edges.

The room on your phone never closes. It's beside you at two in the morning. Something you can't leave stops being a place you visit. It becomes weather — ambient, everywhere, impossible to step out of.

## An honest accounting

None of this makes online rooms worthless. Many of them are genuinely meaningful. People find real connection there, real community, real belonging. But it makes them a different shape than a third place. And it's fair — kind, even — to say plainly where the shape doesn't match rather than pretending the label fits when it doesn't.

So ask the plain questions. Who owns this room. Who decides whether I belong here. Can I walk away from it owing no one anything. Does it close.

The answers won't be all good or all bad. But you'll know what you're standing in. And that — knowing what a place actually is, rather than what it claims to be — is worth more than any verdict.
