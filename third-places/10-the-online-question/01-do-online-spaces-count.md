# Do online spaces count?

*The honest answer isn't yes or no. It's a question of what a third place actually does — and whether your screen gets you there.*

## A room with no address

There's a room you go to most nights. It has no address. You open it with your thumb, in the dark, after everyone else is asleep.

Some of the people in it, you've known for years. You've never heard their voices. Not once.

Does that room count?

It's a question people usually ask with their jaw already set. One way or the other. I think it deserves better than that.

## Both easy answers let you off the hook

There are two easy answers, and both of them let you off the hook.

The first one says no. Not real connection. Get outside, meet real people, put the phone down. It's said with such confidence that you stop arguing — even when that room was the only place anyone noticed you'd gone quiet for three days.

And that dismissal costs something. For some people, the walk to the corner isn't a walk. Night shifts. A small town where everybody already knows everything. A body that doesn't cooperate on stairs or sidewalks. A kid asleep down the hall and nobody to sit with. Telling those people their rooms don't count doesn't hand them a better one. It just takes the name off the door.

The second answer says of course it counts. Community is community, wherever it lives. Which sounds generous — until you notice it saves us from ever asking what we're actually getting when we sit down in that room. It lets us stop looking. And looking matters.

## Change the question

The honest answer starts by changing the question.

Not: is this real? What you feel there is real. The warmth is real. The familiarity is real. The ache when it goes quiet is real. Nobody gets to tell you otherwise.

Ask instead — what does a third place actually do? And does this do that?

A third place isn't home and isn't work. Nobody hosts it. Nobody invited you. You walk in without arranging anything, and you walk out without owing a single person a thing. No thank-you note. No guilt. No sense that you used up a turn and now have to return one.

And then you come back. Often enough that the people there half expect you. That's the whole shape of it. Neutral ground, no host, and regulars.

That's a specific shape. Not everything warm is that shape.

## Hold your rooms up against it

So hold your rooms up against that. Not to win an argument with anyone. Not to prove something to the person who told you to get off your phone, or to the person who told you online community is just as good. Just to see what you've actually got.

Is there a host — somebody whose place it is, who you'd be letting down if you stopped showing up? Was there an invitation you had to accept? Do you feel a pull of obligation when you'd rather stay quiet?

Can you arrive with nothing to say and still belong? Can you leave without explaining where you're going? Are there regulars — people who would notice the shape of your absence, not because they're owed an RSVP, but because your face is simply part of the room?

Some of your rooms will pass part of that. Some won't pass any of it. Very few pass all of it. That's not a failure of the internet. It's a property of the architecture. Most online spaces are designed around a host — an account, an algorithm, a person whose audience you are. That structure is baked in before you ever arrive.

## Partly

So the answer isn't yes, and it isn't no. It's: partly. Some of them. Some of the time.

Partly is an unsatisfying thing to hold. We want a clean verdict so we can move on. But partly is the useful answer, because it tells you which part — what those rooms are truly giving you, and what they've quietly been standing in for.

Maybe the group chat is your third place. Maybe it's your living room and you didn't notice. Maybe the forum is your third place on Tuesdays and your obligation on Fridays. Maybe nothing online has ever been your third place, and that ache you feel there is something else — connection without ground, warmth without a door you can walk back through.

You don't have to defend them. You don't have to give them up. You're allowed to just look, plainly, and let the answer stay complicated a while. The looking is the thing. Most people never do that part.
