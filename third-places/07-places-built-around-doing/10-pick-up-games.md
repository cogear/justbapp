# Pick-up games

*The most ancient form of public life still works — you just have to step past the fence.*

## The Court at Six

There's a court about ten minutes from where I live. Chain nets that clink when the ball drops through. A crack running right through the free throw line, patched badly, patched again. Most evenings around six, there are enough people there for a game.

Nobody organized it. Nobody sent out a message or booked the time or called around to see who was free. It happens the way water finds the low spot in a field — because the conditions are right, and somebody showed up first, and then somebody else showed up, and then there were ten.

Pick-up games are one of the oldest kinds of public life we have. They predate apps, leagues, sign-up sheets, and the idea that recreation needs to be scheduled and managed. And they still work. Not despite their roughness, but because of it.

## Standing Outside It

The hardest part isn't the playing. It's the fence. Standing on the outside of it with a ball under your arm, waiting for somebody to come over and tell you it's alright to walk in.

We've all been there. You circle the court once. You stretch like you're warming up, but really you're just stalling. You're waiting for permission that nobody is going to give you, because nobody has the authority to give it.

So we watch instead. We decide we're not good enough yet. We tell ourselves we'll come back when we've practiced, and then we don't practice, and then we don't come back. The fence turns into a wall made of our own excuses, and the court we were nervous about becomes a court we've forgotten we wanted to play on.

Here's what I wish somebody had told me sooner. A game that's a player short does not care how good you are. It doesn't need you to be polished. It doesn't need you to be ready. It cares that you exist, that you have a body, and that you're standing right there with your shoes already on.

A four-on-four game looking for a tenth is the most welcoming sentence in the English language, even though nobody ever says it out loud.

## Nobody Owns the Court

Because nobody's hosting this. There's no captain, no coach, no organizer with a clipboard and a list your name has to be on. No rsvp, no group chat, no "sorry I can't make it tonight." The court belongs to whoever's on it, for as long as they're on it. That's the whole arrangement.

The rules get made out loud, in the moment. First to eleven, win by two. Call your own fouls. Winners stay on, losers sit. Shirts and skins if it comes to that. Nobody wrote it down. Nobody voted on it. Everybody follows it, because everybody wants the same simple thing — to keep playing.

You didn't get invited. You got counted. Somebody looked at the numbers, looked at you, and pointed. "You're on him." That's your entry. Those are very different feelings, getting invited and getting counted, and only one of them leaves you owing something.

This is the quiet genius of the pick-up game. It operates without the social machinery that makes most gatherings feel like work. You don't bring anything. You don't owe anyone a thank-you note afterward. You don't wonder if you were a burden. You showed up. They needed a body. The math worked.

## Becoming a Regular

Come back enough times and something shifts without anyone announcing it. Nobody throws you a welcome party. There's no moment you can point to. But one week you realize someone passes you the ball before you've asked for it. Someone remembers that you go left every time, and they start cutting you off, and you start going right, and they start cutting you off there too.

You'll know people by their game long before you know their names. The guy with the ugly jump shot who never misses it. The one who calls fouls on himself with a little smile, like he's proud of his honesty. The kid who's getting better every week in front of you, and you can see it happening, and you root for him quietly.

That's the entire membership requirement. You came more than once. Nobody voted. Nobody welcomed you in. You didn't even notice it happening. You just became part of the arithmetic — the number people counted on when they looked at the fence and saw you standing there again.

## Leaving Freely

And then one evening your legs are done. You look at the light going thin and orange over the trees, you pick your jacket up off the fence, and you leave.

No goodbye speech. No working the room. Nobody to thank for having you, no host to find and compliment. The game closes over the space where you were and keeps going without you, the way a stream closes over a stone — and that isn't coldness. That isn't indifference. That's the whole point.

You came freely. You played freely. You left freely. Nobody kept a ledger.

Come back Thursday. Come back next spring. Come back after a year away. The court keeps no records and holds no grudges. It doesn't remember that you turned the ball over three times last week. It doesn't remember that you didn't show up for a month. It needs bodies, and you have one. That's always been enough.

The fence is just a fence. Walk through it.
