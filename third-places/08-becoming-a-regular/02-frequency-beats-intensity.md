# Frequency beats intensity

*A short visit you'll repeat does more than a long one you won't.*

## Twenty minutes

There's a coffee counter near me I stop at four mornings a week. I stay about twenty minutes. Nothing much happens there. No deep conversation, nothing you'd call an experience. And it's quietly become one of the most important rooms in my life.

It took me a long time to work out why such a small thing mattered. Then it landed. Frequency beats intensity.

Not because a short visit is somehow better. Because the short visit is the one you'll actually do again tomorrow. That's the whole trick. The best visit is the one that's small enough to survive a busy week.

## Waiting for the gap

We tend to think closeness gets built in big blocks. The long dinner. The three-hour catch-up. The night that turns into something. So when the week is tight, we skip the small stuff and wait for a gap that never quite opens.

The visits get rarer. And rare visits get heavy. You walk in half-expecting the hour to deliver — a real conversation, a good story, some proof the trip was worth it.

A room can't do that on command. Nowhere can. So the evening's fine, and fine feels like failure, and you quietly decide the place isn't for you.

We've all done this. Saved the coffee shop for a Saturday when we have proper time, then Saturday arrives and there's laundry and a errand and a phone call, and the visit slips again. The place stays a stranger.

## The arithmetic

Familiarity was never made of depth. It's made of repetition. Someone has to see your face enough times that it stops being new to them. That's the entire requirement.

Four short mornings a week is about sixteen appearances in a month. One long afternoon is one. Same room, same people behind the counter, completely different arithmetic.

Nobody's keeping score on purpose. But something in us keeps score anyway. Somewhere around the sixth visit, a face behind the counter changes a little when you walk in. A nod replaces the polite scan. That's the whole mechanism. Nothing cleverer than that.

The shift isn't dramatic. It's barely perceptible. But you feel it — the way you feel when a door opens a little easier than it used to.

## Small enough to repeat

So make the visit small enough that you'll repeat it without negotiating with yourself. Not the big outing. The stop on the way. Fifteen minutes and a newspaper. One drink at the quiet end of the bar.

And here's what makes that possible: there's no host. Nobody invited you, so nobody's tracking your arrival, and nobody needs an excuse when you go. Nothing is owed in either direction. This is the quiet structural gift of a proper third place — it removes the social weight that makes showing up feel like work.

In a hosted setting, every visit carries a small ledger. You owe a thank-you. You should bring something. You can't leave too early or it looks rude. That ledger is exactly what wears you down over time. A neutral ground carries no ledger. You can walk in, take a stool, drink the thing, and leave. Do that on Tuesday, and Thursday, and the Tuesday after, and one day you'll realise the room knows you.

## Then go again

It's slow. Honestly, it's a bit dull at first. Eight visits in, it can still feel like nothing is happening at all. You'll wonder if you're doing it wrong.

Then someone starts your order before you say it. Or a person you've never spoken to says something about the rain, and you answer, and that's that. None of it came from intensity. All of it came from turning up.

The room didn't change. You did, by being there often enough to stop being a stranger.

So don't wait for the free afternoon. It isn't coming. Go for twenty minutes, then go again this week, and let the rest take its time.
