# Talking to strangers, calibrated

*The art of saying just enough, and not one bit more, in a place where you owe nobody anything.*

## Two Stools Down

There's a person two stools down. You've seen them in here before. Same afternoon, roughly the same seat. You could say something.

And then you don't. You look at your cup instead, and the moment closes quietly, the way those moments do. No harm done. Nothing gained either.

Here's what I think that hesitation actually is. Not nerve. Dosage. Nobody ever taught you how much to say to a person you half-know, in a place where neither of you owes the other anything. There's no script for this. No class. No etiquette guide that covers the specific, strange, low-stakes territory of the regular you recognize but have never formally met.

We learn how to talk to friends. We learn how to talk to service workers, more or less. But the person who occupies the fuzzy middle — the familiar stranger — that's the one nobody prepared us for.

## Too Little, Too Much

The advice we get is mostly two shouts from opposite directions. Be bolder, put yourself out there. Or: leave people alone, everyone's busy. Neither is any use when you're actually sitting there with your coat still on.

Because silence isn't the only way this goes wrong. There's the other way. The question that's too big too early. The story that keeps going. A stranger quietly working out how to get away from you.

Nobody wants to become a debt. Not to a stranger. I think that's the fear underneath the fear. If you say too much, you create a kind of social gravity. The other person now has to respond with enough, or feel guilty for not responding with enough. You've turned a neutral room into a place where someone owes someone something. And the whole reason the room works in the first place is that nobody owes anybody anything.

## A Dial, Not a Door

So try a better word than courage. Calibration. You're not deciding whether to speak. You're deciding how much — and the answer is almost always less than you think.

The smallest useful unit is a remark that doesn't need an answer. About the rain. About the thing you're both already looking at. Said sideways, into the room, not aimed at anyone's face.

Start small enough to be ignored. If they don't pick it up, nothing happened. Nobody got rejected. You both go back to your coffee, and the room stays easy. This is the thing to internalize: a remark that lands softly enough to be ignored is not a failed remark. It's a calibrated one. It did its job, which was to make contact possible without making it mandatory.

## Follow, Don't Lead

Aim at what you're both in, not at each other. The song. The line that isn't moving. The dog under the stool. Questions about a person are heavy. Questions about a room weigh almost nothing.

Then listen for the size of what comes back. A word means a word. A sentence means a sentence. If they turn their body toward you and keep going, you can go further. Follow. Don't lead.

And stop it before it needs stopping. Good to see you — then back to your book. You don't owe them the rest of the hour. They don't owe you the next one. That's the whole arrangement. The beauty of a third place is that you can arrive and leave freely. That freedom doesn't end when you speak to someone. It's what makes speaking safe.

## Not a Test

Do this once a week and nothing dramatic happens. Do it for a few months and there are four or five people in here who know your face, maybe your first name. That's all it builds. That's plenty.

You don't have to be good at this. You only have to be calibrated. Small first. Slow after. Free to stop at any point, with no explanation owed to anyone.

The stranger two stools down isn't a test you're taking. Just a person in the same room, on the same ordinary afternoon. The room is the point. The conversation is a bonus that the room makes possible — and only because the room asks nothing of either of you.

Sit with that long enough and it stops feeling like a risk. It starts feeling like weather. Something that happens in a place where people are, because people are there.
