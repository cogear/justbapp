# The car and the cul-de-sac

*How we rebuilt the ground under our feet, and lost the places in between.*

## The street you live on

Think about the last time you talked to someone on your street without meaning to. Not a planned visit. Not a text first. You were both just outside, and it happened.

For a lot of us, that's hard to remember.

It's easy to think we got colder, busier, more closed off. That we lost something in ourselves. I don't think that's it. I think the ground under us got rebuilt, and nobody told us.

The physical world we move through every day changed shape. And the shape it took made the small, unplanned encounters we used to have almost impossible. Not because we stopped wanting them. Because the architecture stopped allowing them.

## A room that moves

Start with the car. A car is a room. A small, private room that moves. You get in at your house. You get out at the place you meant to go. Nothing happens in between.

Through the windshield, every other person is traffic. Something to be judged by speed and lane. Not a face. Not someone you might see again tomorrow.

Walking is different. You pass people. You pass an open door, a bench, a window with the lights on. Most of those passings turn into nothing at all. That's what makes the rare one feel light instead of loaded. There's no obligation in it. You didn't arrange it. Nobody owes anyone a follow-up.

In a car there is no passing. There's only leaving, and arriving.

The space between home and everywhere else used to be shared space. Now it's a sealed tube.

## The street that goes nowhere

Then there's the shape of the street itself.

A cul-de-sac is built so that nobody drives through it who doesn't already live on it. That was the point. Quiet. Safe. No through traffic.

And it worked. Nobody comes through. Which also means: nobody comes through.

The street is a pocket with one opening, and the only people inside it are the ones who were already inside it. A place strangers never pass can't slowly become a place where strangers stop being strangers. And slow is the only speed that ever works for that.

Familiarity doesn't happen all at once. It happens in layers. A nod one week. A name the next. A conversation six months later that neither of you planned. That only works if people are physically near each other, repeatedly, without a reason.

## The trickle

A public place needs passersby. Not events. Not a big turnout. Just a steady trickle of people going by on their way to something else, some of whom come in.

Take away the sidewalk and you take away the trickle. Now every visit costs a decision, a set of keys, and a parking spot. You can't stop by on the way to nowhere. You have to intend it.

Intention is heavier than habit. It's the difference between a place you go and a place you find yourself.

So the ordinary places thinned out. The corner store, the bench, the stoop, the café where you'd sit without buying anything. And what was left was home, work, and the drive between them.

## Nearest beats nicest

None of this was anybody's plot. People wanted a quiet street, a driveway, a car of their own. They got all three. The bill arrived later, and it arrived quietly.

But there's something useful buried in it. If you want one of those places in your life — a neutral place where you can show up without an invitation and leave without owing anyone anything — the first question isn't which one is best. It's which one you can reach on foot.

Nearest beats nicest. Every time. Because the thing you're after isn't a great night out. It's a place your feet already know how to get to. A place where the friction between wanting to go and actually being there is close to zero.

You don't have to arrange anything. You don't owe anyone there a thing. You just have to be able to get there without deciding to.

That's not a small thing. That's the whole thing.
