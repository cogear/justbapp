# Who lost the most

*When third places disappeared, they didn't disappear evenly — and the people who needed them most lost the most.*

## Not evenly

When something disappears, it doesn't disappear evenly. Some people lost a habit. Some people lost almost everything they had outside their own front door.

So it's worth asking who used those places most. Not the people with big houses and full calendars. The people who had nowhere else to be.

The story of third places closing isn't one story. It's a hundred different losses, sized differently depending on who you were, and what else you had.

A café owner once told me he could predict who'd be most upset when he shut down — not the regulars who came after work, who had other places to go. It was the retired man who came at the same time every morning, the teenager who sat with a single drink for two hours. They weren't losing a convenience. They were losing a room.

## What money bought instead

If you have money, you can buy something that looks like what was lost. A membership. A class with the same faces every week. Company on a schedule, for a fee.

And you can have people over. But a house is a hosted thing. Someone invites, someone tidies, someone owes a return. It's a good evening. It isn't a place you can walk into unannounced and leave when you like.

That distinction matters more than people think. The beauty of a third place was never just the company. It was the freedom — arriving without an invitation, leaving without a farewell, sitting without owing anyone a thing. You can't replicate that by entertaining at home, because hosting and hosting-back creates its own quiet ledger of obligation.

Some people simply moved to the few neighbourhoods where the old pattern survived. Those places got expensive, which tells you how rare they've become.

## The ones without options

Teenagers lost the most obvious thing. No car, no money, no room that isn't also the family's. They needed somewhere free to sit that nobody was in charge of.

Old people lost quietly. Retirement ends the workday's company. Years thin out the address book. With no counter to sit at, the world narrows to the width of a living room.

And anyone new to town. If nothing is open to walk into, you don't meet your neighbours. You just live near them, for years.

It's strange when you think about it — proximity has never been higher. We're stacked on top of each other in apartment buildings and subdivisions. But without neutral ground, proximity doesn't become acquaintance. It just becomes background.

## The loss ran downhill

Then there's everyone whose home is crowded, or shared, or someone else's. A third place gave people a room they didn't have to own.

People who couldn't drive. People who worked nights and came home as everyone else was heading out. The place on the corner didn't ask them for a car or a free Saturday.

And the people at home all day with small children. Whoever's home while everyone else is out needs a public room more than anybody, and had the fewest left.

So the loss ran downhill. The people with the least to fall back on had the most taken from them. That's usually how it goes. It's worth saying plainly.

## Permission

None of this is yours to fix. You're not in charge of anyone's loneliness, and you were never meant to be the host of the neighbourhood.

But you can notice who isn't there. The bench that's gone. The kid with nowhere to sit. The neighbour you've never spoken to in nine years.

And you can keep walking into the places that are left. Take a seat. Come back next week. A place open to anyone stays open by being used.

So ask it, when a place closes and you're wondering whether it mattered. Who had nowhere else to go?
