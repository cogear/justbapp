# Zoning, and why nothing is walkable

*A century of quiet rules sorted our lives so far apart that gathering anywhere now requires keys and a tank of gas.*

## The end of the driveway

Think of the nearest place you could sit with a stranger and nobody would ask why you came. Now think about how you'd get there.

For most of us, the answer involves keys. A car door. A seatbelt. A route.

It isn't that you don't want to walk somewhere. It's that there's nowhere to walk to. And that wasn't a preference or an accident. It was a decision, written down in a document almost nobody reads.

Zoning is the quiet law of where things are allowed to be. And for roughly a century, it has been very clear about one thing: the places where people gather don't belong near where people live.

This matters more than it sounds. When the corner bar, the coffee shop, the bench by the newsstand — all the small, ordinary places where you can just show up — get zoned away from where people actually sleep, something structural happens to community. The gathering places don't disappear from desire. They disappear from geography.

## Sorted into kinds

A hundred years ago, cities began drawing maps that sorted the world into kinds. Houses here. Shops there. Work somewhere else entirely. Each use in its own colored zone.

The reasoning was sensible at first. You don't want a tannery beside a nursery. Nobody argues with keeping the smoky, noisy, smelly stuff away from where people sleep. But the lines kept getting cleaner. And cleaner. Eventually the small stuff got separated out too. The bakery. The tavern. The barber's chair.

After the war, the new maps became the only maps. Almost everything built since the 1950s has been built inside them. Two generations have grown up inside a system that treats mixing — a shop next to a home, an apartment above a café — as a problem to be solved rather than a pattern to be protected.

The third places that survived this sorting did so in spite of the rules, not because of them.

## Distance, required

Separation has arithmetic. Give every shop a parking lot, and the shops move apart. Set every building back from the road, and the walk gets longer. Require a minimum lot size, and the houses spread thin.

Do all three — and most zoning codes do — and a five-minute walk becomes a twelve-minute drive. Not because anyone hated walking. Because the math added up that way, one requirement at a time.

You feel it in your body before you can name it. The pause at the end of the driveway. The quiet calculation of whether it's worth it. It isn't far, exactly. It just isn't close enough to be effortless.

And effortlessness is the whole point. A third place only works if arriving takes less energy than deciding to stay home. The moment you have to plan, pack, and drive, the threshold gets too high for casual, uncommitted gathering. You stop being a regular and start being an event planner.

Nothing here is far by accident. Every extra step was required by somebody's rule, written down years before you got here.

## The building that can't be built

You've probably seen an old corner store. A brick building with apartments above it, sitting in the middle of a neighborhood like it has every right to be there. Maybe it's a café now. Maybe it's been three things in the last decade. But the bones of it — the shop at street level, the life above — still hold.

Under most current codes, that building couldn't be built today. Wrong zone. Not enough parking. Too close to the sidewalk. The setbacks are wrong. The lot is too small. The ones still standing are legal only because they came first. The paperwork calls them nonconforming.

That's the whole story in one word. The buildings that held our ordinary gathering places are, on paper, mistakes.

There's something worth sitting with in that. The most natural, human-scale pattern we have — a place to live near a place to gather — is the thing our rules were designed to prevent.

## Not your willpower

So if you've ever felt vaguely lazy for not going out more — for driving past the lit windows instead of stopping, for staying home when you meant to go somewhere — you can put that down.

You're not short on willpower. You're short on destinations within walking distance. And that shortage was engineered by people who believed they were making things tidier.

Where the old fabric survives — a main street, a few blocks with shops at the bottom and life on top — it still works exactly as it always did. People show up without invitations. They sit down. They leave when they're ready. Nobody asks why they came. Nobody tallies what they owe.

Go there. Sit down. You don't owe anyone anything for showing up.

The third place isn't gone because we stopped wanting it. It's gone because we zoned it into nonconformance. But the want is still there, right at the end of the driveway, waiting for somewhere to walk to.
