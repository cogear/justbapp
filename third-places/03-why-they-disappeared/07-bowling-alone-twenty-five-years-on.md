# Bowling Alone, twenty-five years on

*A book title did all the work, and the thing it described is still quietly disappearing.*

## A Title That Did All The Work

In 2000, a political scientist named Robert Putnam published a book with a title that did all the work. *Bowling Alone*. Twenty-five years later, most people who quote it have never read it. The title was enough.

The image was simple. Americans were still bowling. They had stopped bowling in leagues.

It's worth going back to what the book actually said. Not because it was prophecy. Because it was arithmetic. Putnam spent years gathering data, and the data told a story most people could feel but hadn't quite put into words.

## The Counting

Putnam spent hundreds of pages counting things nobody thought to count. Attendance at public meetings. Card games. Picnics. Bowling leagues. How many people you knew by name in the places you went every week.

Every line pointed down. Not off a cliff. Just down, steadily, for thirty years. He called what was draining away "social capital" — an economist's phrase for something much simpler. The number of people who'd notice if you didn't show up.

And the bowling number was the strangest of all. More people bowling than ever before. Fewer than ever bowling with the same faces on the same night.

That gap — more bowling, less togetherness — became the whole metaphor. It captured something the country was doing without quite deciding to do it. We hadn't stopped wanting company. We'd stopped ending up in the same room with the same people on a recurring basis, without anyone organizing it.

## Twenty-Five Years On

So: twenty-five years on. He was right. The lines he drew kept going in the direction he drew them, through two more decades and a pandemic. Nobody serious argues about that part anymore.

The arguing is about why. He had his suspects. The screen in the corner of the room. The long drive home. One generation aging out and the next never picking it up. The suburbs built for cars, not corners. The cost of rent pushing out the kinds of rooms that used to be cheap.

All of those probably matter. But there's something the counting couldn't hold. He measured the joining. The harder thing to measure is the going.

## The Building Was The Point

Because the league was never really the point. The league was the reason to be in the building on a Tuesday. The building was the point.

A bowling alley didn't belong to anyone in it. No one invited you. No one was owed a thank-you. You paid for your lane. The rest — the guy who always took the far end, the woman who knew your shoe size — came free.

You could go alone and not be alone. That's a strange sentence to read now. It used to be an ordinary evening.

This is the part that's hard to recover by organizing. You can start a club. You can plan a dinner. But a planned dinner has a host. A club has a coordinator. The bowling alley on a Tuesday had neither. You showed up because Tuesday was the night. You left when you felt like it. Nobody tracked your attendance. Nobody sent a follow-up text. The whole arrangement ran on nothing more than shared habit and a building that was open.

Lose the league and you lose your Tuesday. Lose the building and you lose the whole cast.

## Still Open

That's the honest state of things, twenty-five years on. Not that we became unfriendly. The rooms closed.

A few are still open. A counter. A bar. A table by the window. A lane at the end of a strip mall that's hung on longer than anyone expected.

You don't need to organize anything. You don't need to bring anyone. Go, sit, pay, leave. Do that enough weeks in a row and someone starts nodding at you.

That's the whole membership. There's no form. No group chat. No one will ask where you were last week. You'll just be the person who sits at that table, until one day you aren't, and maybe someone will notice and maybe they won't, and the room will keep being the room.

That sounds thin, written out like this. It wasn't thin when it was just the way evenings worked.
