# The suburb problem

*You haven't been doing it wrong. The map you were handed was drawn without a place to land.*

## Nothing is wrong with you

If you live in a suburb and you've been quietly looking for somewhere to just exist among people — not a dinner party, not a playdate, not a scheduled thing — you may have decided by now that something is wrong with you. That you're bad at friendship. That you didn't try hard enough. That everyone else has figured out something you haven't.

There isn't anything wrong with you. There's something wrong with the design you're living inside.

The suburb operates on a number that quietly governs your whole social life: twenty minutes. Twenty minutes to anything. Twenty there, twenty back, and a decision required at both ends of every single trip. Nothing is nearby. Nothing is incidental. Every outing becomes an event with a beginning and an end, which means every outing has a cost, and you feel that cost whether you name it or not.

## Separated on purpose

Suburbs were built on a simple idea: keep things apart. Houses here. Shops over there. Work somewhere else entirely. Each function in its own zone, connected by road.

Which means almost nothing you might do sits next to anything else you might do. You can't wander from your front door to a coffee. You can't drop by the hardware store on your walk home. Every visit is a trip — keys, seatbelt, parking lot. A decision instead of a drift.

And no one walks past your door on the way to anywhere, because your street doesn't go anywhere. It curves. It dead-ends in a cul-de-sac. That's not a flaw in your neighborliness or a failure of your warmth. It's geometry. The layout was drawn to give you privacy, and privacy is what you got.

Older neighborhoods, the kind that existed before zoning separated everything, hide their gathering places in plain sight. A corner store. A stoop. A counter you pass every day without planning to. The suburb has no corners. It has arrivals and departures. Nothing in between.

## The backyard answer

The suburb does have an answer for human connection, and it's a genuine one. The backyard. The dinner invitation. The Saturday barbecue. Real friendship, real food, real generosity. People in suburbs are not colder than people anywhere else. They care about each other and they show it.

But somebody has to host. Somebody has to issue the invitation. Somebody buys the food, cleans up afterward, and somebody leaves owing a return invitation. That's a good thing. It's just not the same thing as a third place.

A third place asks nothing of you. You don't wait to be invited. You don't owe a turn. You don't bring a dish or reciprocate next weekend. You walk in, you sit down, you stay as long as you like, you leave, and the ledger stays empty. There is no host because no one is responsible for your being there. You showed up. That's the whole requirement.

## Unlovely and open

So what survives out in suburbia? Usually something unlovely. A coffee shop at the end of a strip mall. A diner just off the highway. The public library. The bar attached to the bowling alley. A hardware store with a couple of stools by the register.

None of it is charming. Fluorescent light. A view of the parking lot. A counter that's seen better decades. It doesn't matter. The only question that matters is whether you can walk in unannounced, sit down, and nobody needs you to explain yourself. No RSVP. No occasion. No reason to be there beyond wanting to be around people for a little while.

Most suburbs have one such place. Some have two. Almost none have six. They're rare, and they're fragile, because they exist despite the design rather than because of it. So when you find the one, go often. Go at the same hour, on the same day. Regulars are what keep a door open. The owner notices. The barista notices. The other regulars notice. You become part of the texture of the place, and the place becomes part of the texture of your week.

## Put the weight down

If you've lived in a suburb for years and never found a place like this, you weren't failing at anything. You were living inside a design that didn't include one. That's not a personality problem. It's an architecture problem.

Naming it doesn't fix your street. The cul-de-sac will still dead-end. The shops will still be twenty minutes away. But it puts the weight down. You can stop searching your own character for the reason you feel disconnected. You can stop wondering what's wrong with you.

The design is the design. You're allowed to see it clearly. And then go looking anyway — for the diner, the library, the unlovely counter with the fluorescent light. It's out there, probably closer than you think, quietly holding a door open for anyone who walks in.
