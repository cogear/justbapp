# Asking a business to host you

*When there's no third place nearby, sometimes the answer is a simple conversation with a business that has empty chairs.*

There's a particular kind of nervous you feel walking up to a counter to ask a stranger for something. The kind that makes the whole thing feel bigger than it is.

Let's make it smaller.

You want somewhere to sit with other people regularly. There isn't one near you. So you're thinking about asking a business if you can use their space.

Here's the first thing to understand: "host" isn't quite the right word for what you're asking. Businesses don't host. They open, they sell, they close. And honestly, that's better than hosting. It comes with no strings attached.

## The thing about a dead Tuesday

You think you're asking a favor. But put yourself behind that counter for a moment. Tuesday at four is dead. The chairs are empty. The afternoon stretches out with nothing in it.

Ten people who buy drinks on a dead Tuesday isn't a favor. It's a good afternoon.

This is the quiet gift of a business. Money settles the account. You buy something, you're square. Nobody owes anybody a thank-you note. Nobody is keeping a ledger of who brought what or who thanked whom. The transaction is clean and complete.

So you're not asking them to care about your idea of community or connection. You're asking a business question. Is it okay if a few of us come here regularly and buy things? Those are easier to answer. And much easier to say yes to.

## Plain words

Keep it plain. A few of us want to sit here every Tuesday at four. Same corner. We'll buy things. Is that all right?

That's the entire ask.

Ask for a day and a time. Not a room. Not a discount. Not a booking. You're asking permission to be ordinary customers on a schedule — nothing more.

Say when you'll arrive and when you'll be gone. Two hours is plenty. People say yes more easily when they can already see the end of the thing. A vague open-ended request feels like a commitment. A defined window feels like a visit.

And if they say yes with a shrug — that's the best yes there is. A shrug means it's not a big deal to them. Which means it won't become one later.

## Leave the door open

Two things to avoid.

Don't ask for the back room with a door that closes. The door is the whole problem. It turns an open afternoon into a private party. The moment you close a door, you've created an inside and an outside. Someone walking past has to decide whether they belong. That's a barrier, even if it's only made of wood.

The point is that someone can see you from the pavement, walk in, order something, and sit down. No invitation. Nobody at the door deciding who belongs. The space stays open in every sense of the word.

And don't ask them to run it. They're not your host, and you're not theirs. Their job here is to keep the lights on and let people be. That's enough. That's actually the ideal arrangement — a place that exists because it's open for business, not because someone is curating an experience.

## Ask the next one

Some will say no. That's fine. A no costs you four minutes of nerves and a short walk down the street. Then you ask the next one.

The nervousness is real but it's not dangerous. It's the same feeling as jumping into a cold pool. You do it, it's over, and you wonder why you hesitated.

And if one yes turns into months of Tuesdays, notice what's happened. Something exists now that doesn't need you. You can skip a week. You can move away. Someone else can show up and find the same corner, the same rhythm, the same loose collection of people who happen to be there.

The corner keeps going without you. That isn't a sad thing. That's the whole point. A third place that depends on one person isn't a third place yet. It's a project. The goal is for it to stop being yours and just become a Tuesday that exists.

Walk up to the counter. Ask the plain question. See what happens.
