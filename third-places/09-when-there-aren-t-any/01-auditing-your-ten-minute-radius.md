# Auditing your ten-minute radius

*You probably haven't actually checked whether there's anywhere nearby to just sit — so here's how to look.*

## Say no fast

Most people, asked if there's anywhere near them to just sit — no host, no invitation, no reason to buy much — say no. They say it fast. Like they already checked.

But I don't think most of us have actually checked. Not properly. Not with a different question in mind. We checked for other things — for coffee, for bread, for parking — and we decided the answer was no based on that. So I want to ask a smaller, more literal question. When did you last look?

## You know it as a route

You know your neighbourhood as a route. The way to work. The way to the school gate. The way to the shop that has the bread you like. You know where to buy things. You know where to park. But you've probably never walked your own streets asking a different question. Where could I stay?

Errands make us blind. We move through our streets with purpose, and purpose narrows what we see. A place with chairs, open late, half full of people who show up every week — you can pass it four hundred times and never once see it as somewhere you could be. It registers as scenery, not as a possibility.

This is how most of us end up believing we have nowhere. We have plenty of places. We just haven't looked at them as places to be rather than places to pass.

## Ten minutes

So here's the frame. Ten minutes. Walking, rolling, driving — whatever ten minutes honestly means for your body and your street. Draw that circle.

Ten minutes is the honest distance. Past that, it becomes a trip. A trip needs a reason, a plan, and energy you might not have on a Tuesday. The places that become yours are almost always the ones you can reach without making a decision about it. You go because it's close, not because you've mustered the will.

And ten minutes takes the fantasy out of it. You're not looking for the café from a film. You're not looking for the place you saw in a travel magazine. You're looking for what's actually there, at the end of your street, this week.

## Walk it, write it down

So walk it. Not looking for a perfect place — just making a list. The bakery with two tables. The laundromat. The library. The bench outside the hardware store. The bar nobody ever photographs.

Three questions at each one. Could I come here without being invited? Could I sit an hour without anyone minding? Is it open at the hours I'm actually free?

If a place needs someone to host you — a dinner, a club with a sign-up sheet, a friend's kitchen — leave it off the list. Lovely, but different. This list is only for places that owe you nothing, and that you owe nothing back. The whole point is that you can arrive and leave freely. The moment someone needs to let you in, it's something else. Something good, maybe, but not this.

Write the hours down too. A place that's lovely at eleven on a Sunday and shut every evening isn't wrong. It's just not built for the hours you actually have. Be honest about when you're free. A place that's perfect but never open when you are doesn't belong on this list yet.

## What counts

You might find four places. You might find one. You might walk the whole circle and find nothing you'd want to sit in twice.

All three are answers. And any answer is lighter to carry than the vague heaviness of assuming there's nothing. The worst case isn't that you find no place. The worst case is that you never look, and you carry around a story about your neighbourhood that might not even be true.

Your ten-minute radius is small, and real, and knowable. Most of us carry a story about it instead. Go and see what's actually there.
