# Parenting, caregiving, and the time you don't have

*When you can't free up an evening, look at the minutes you already have.*

## Eleven Minutes

Most advice about finding your people assumes you have an evening. A whole one. Unclaimed. Open. If you're caring for someone — a kid, a parent, a partner who's ill — you know that evening doesn't exist.

What you have is eleven minutes in a parking lot. Twenty in a waiting room. The gap between drop-off and the next thing. The window between when the medication takes effect and when it wears off. That's the shape of your time now.

It's not nothing. But it doesn't look like enough. Not enough for a phone call that goes somewhere real. Not enough for a dinner. Not enough, it seems, for connection at all.

## The Arithmetic of an Invitation

So when someone invites you to dinner, look at what it actually costs. A date agreed three weeks out. Somebody to cover your absence. A start time you have to hit, which means a departure time you have to hit, which means the whole afternoon bends around it. And then, eventually, the invitation back. You owe them a meal at your place, which means you owe them cleaning, planning, a stretch of energy you don't have.

None of that is unkind. It's just arithmetic that doesn't work. So you say no, and no again, and after a while the asking quietly stops.

And you decide, without ever really saying it, that company is something for later. After. When the years loosen up. When the kid is older. When the treatment is done. When the schedule opens.

But the schedule doesn't open. It reshapes. It finds new ways to fill itself. And the waiting stretches longer than you imagined.

## Nobody's Holding a Chair

But there's a kind of place that asks for none of that. No date. No RSVP. Nobody waiting on your answer. You walk in, or you don't. Nobody's holding a chair for you. Nobody noticed you weren't there last Tuesday, and nobody will notice if you come this Tuesday. And that sounds lonely, but it's the opposite. It's freedom.

That's the whole difference. A dinner needs your calendar and a favor returned. A public counter needs eleven minutes and nothing else.

You can arrive late. You can leave in the middle of somebody's sentence. Nobody's dinner goes cold. Nothing is owed in either direction. You don't have to explain where you were or why you left. You don't have to send a thank-you text the next morning.

## Waiting, Somewhere Else

So stop hunting for free time. Look instead at the time you already spend out of the house, waiting. Between the appointment and the pickup. Between the school bell and the bus. Standing around regardless, scrolling your phone in a parked car.

Move that waiting somewhere with other people in it. The same counter, the same bench, the same bakery. Twice a week, twelve minutes, with the stroller parked right beside you.

Being a regular isn't measured in hours. It's measured in repetition. Show up in fragments, in the same fragment of the week, and after a month the face behind the counter knows yours. After two months, they know your order. After three, they ask how the kid's doing.

This is how it builds. Not through scheduled intimacy but through accumulated presence. Through being a face that shows up enough times to become familiar, without ever having to explain yourself or earn it.

## Permission

You are not a bad friend for not hosting. You don't have to organize anything or invite anyone back. You only have to show up, and then be free to go.

The talk you get will be short and cut in half. Your kid will need the bathroom. Your phone will buzz. You'll have to leave before the story finishes.

It will still be talk with somebody who wants nothing from you. No follow-up. No plan. No next step. Just two people sharing space for a few minutes, which is, it turns out, quite a lot.

You don't have to wait for the years to loosen. Twelve minutes counts. Go where the people already are.
